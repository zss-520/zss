Precise prediction of protein–ligand interactions is crucial for effective virtual screening and the rational design of novel therapeutics. , Extending this, identifying key interaction residues can enhance the efficiency of screening by enabling the exclusion of ligands that do not engage with the functionally relevant regions of the target protein, i.e., by focusing on compounds with a higher likelihood of interacting with biologically relevant regions of the target protein. − This focused approach, integrated with large-scale chemical library screening, facilitates the rapid identification of potential drug-like compounds. − To predict protein–ligand binding residues, dozens of machine learning approaches have been developed, which are typically classified into two categories: structure-based and sequence-based approaches. With the advent of deep learning, both approaches have achieved significant improvements through more effective modeling of residue–ligand interaction patterns. Despite their accuracy, their practical application in large-scale drug discovery is limited due to their intensive computational requirements and limited performance across diverse targets. Structure-based approaches generally utilize three-dimensional protein structures to examine the geometric and physicochemical characteristics of protein surfaces in order to identify potential binding residues. Fpocket is a geometry-based technique that detects ligand-binding pockets by clustering α spheres obtained through Voronoi tessellation. P2Rank predicts ligand-binding sites by learning ligandability patterns on the protein surface using a random forest classifier that analyzes solvent-accessible points. GraphPLBR treats the prediction as a graph-based node classification task, leveraging a deep graph convolutional network to model the spatial relationships among residues. Although these approaches show solid predictive performance, their reliance on high-quality protein structures restricts their use when structural data are lacking or incomplete. Additionally, many require extensive molecular docking or energy calculations, which are computationally intensive and less feasible for large-scale data sets. Sequence-based approaches have recently gained attention as an alternative. These methods infer ligand-binding residues using residue-level features derived only from protein sequences. Each residue is typically encoded as a multidimensional feature vector capturing evolutionary, structural, and biochemical properties, such as position-specific scoring matrices (PSSMs), secondary structure (SS), and evolutionary conservation scores. , Deep learning models then transform these feature vectors into residue-level embeddings, which are processed through convolutional or residual architectures to learn contextual sequence patterns. Representative models such as DeepCSeqSite and BiRDS follow this modeling strategy by encoding residue features and learning binding patterns from sequence context alone. Another sequence-based model is Pseq2sites, which leverages protein language models to construct residue embeddings directly from the amino acid sequence. While sequence-based models have shown comparable predictive performance, they still face a critical limitation in that they do not consider ligand information. Since binding residues are defined by their interactions with ligands, incorporating ligand information is essential for accurate prediction. However, most existing sequence-based models rely solely on residue-level features derived from the protein sequence, without incorporating any ligand information. In addition to this limitation, sequence-based models are affected by the class imbalance in binding residue data sets, where binding residues constitute only a small portion of the entire sequence. This class imbalance affects the behavior of sequence-based models in different ways. Some models achieve high recall by predicting many residues as binding sites, which can lower overall accuracy and increase false positives. Other models predict the majority of residues as nonbinding, which results in high overall accuracy but a failure to detect actual binding sites. This makes it difficult to trust the predictions in practice. To overcome the limitations of existing sequence-based models, we propose LiBRe, a novel deep learning model for predicting protein–ligand binding residues that incorporates both protein sequence and ligand information. Unlike most previous approaches that rely on protein-derived features, LiBRe integrates information from both the protein and the ligand, enabling ligand-specific prediction of binding residues for a given protein target. The model constructs residue representations using a pretrained protein language model, which eliminates the need for tools such as PSSM or multiple sequence alignment. For ligands, we build graph-based embeddings using a graph attention network that captures both structural and chemical features at the atom level. The combined representations allow the model to predict binding residues in a ligand-aware context. To improve robustness in the presence of imbalanced data, we apply contrastive learning, which helps the model distinguish binding residues from nonbinding ones more effectively during training. We demonstrated the feasibility of this approach by constructing binding pockets centered on the predicted ligand-binding residues. We adopted two publicly available data sets, PDBbind­(v.2020) and sc-PDB (v.2017) to construct our training data set. Although our model is sequence-based and does not use structural information as input, these structure-based data sets were employed to obtain reliable residue–ligand binding annotations. PDBbind­(v.2020) provides a comprehensive collection of protein–ligand complexes with experimentally determined binding affinities and associated 3D structural information derived from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Similarly, sc-PDB is a curated resource that focuses on druggable binding sites within protein–ligand complexes, also extracted from the PDB. For each complex, it provides detailed structural information, including separate MOL2 files for the ligand, the corresponding binding site and the associated protein chain(s). Binding residue information for the training set was obtained from these annotated binding site structures as provided in the data sets. For evaluation, we used COACH420 and HOLO4K, two benchmark test data sets first introduced in P2Rank, and their binding residue information was obtained according to the binding residue definition used in that study. To ensure the quality and reliability of the data sets used for model training and evaluation, we applied several preprocessing strategies to remove uninformative or redundant samples. Based on the findings of a previous study that highlighted the adverse effects of excessive sequence padding on model performance and computational efficiency, we excluded protein sequences longer than 1500 residues. Such long sequences accounted for less than 1% of the entire data set, and including them would require excessive padding, resulting in inefficient batching and increased memory usage during training. Additionally, similar to the filtering strategy reported in another study, we removed samples containing fewer than 10 binding residues. Since our objective is to accurately identify protein–ligand binding sites, samples with very few binding residues provide limited information for the model to learn meaningful patterns associated with binding, making it more challenging to distinguish between binding and nonbinding residues. We then applied CD-HIT, with a sequence identity threshold of 0.9 to process protein sequences in the training set. Because ligand-binding residue prediction is highly class-imbalanced, preserving sufficient positive binding residue signals during training is critical. In bioinformatics prediction tasks with similar data imbalance, − a 90% sequence identity cutoff is commonly used to remove near-duplicate sequences while maintaining sufficient training data volume and diversity. Accordingly, we adopted a 0.9 threshold to balance redundancy reduction with effective binding-residue signal preservation during model training. After constructing the training set, we further minimized potential redundancy between the training and test sets by ensuring that no proteins shared the same PDB IDs across the two splits. In addition, to enable a rigorous evaluation of ligand-binding residue prediction performance, we followed prior work such as Pseq2sites, which constructed unseen test data sets by removing structurally similar proteins based on TM-score. Similarly, we used Foldseek to assess structural similarity and excluded from the test set any proteins exhibiting high structural similarity (Foldseek similarity >0.4) to the training data. Finally, because our model is designed to incorporate ligand information, we included only those protein–ligand complexes whose ligand structures could be successfully converted into canonical SMILES format using Open Babel and the RDKit Python package. The proposed model, LiBRe, is a deep learning-based model designed to predict ligand-binding residues within a protein sequence. It consists of four main stages: (i) Residue Encoder, for constructing embeddings of each residue within the protein sequence; (ii) Ligand Encoder, for constructing ligand embeddings; (iii) Residue-Ligand Interaction, for modeling bidirectional interactions between each residue in a protein sequence and the ligand that binds to it; (iv) Ligand-Binding Residue Prediction, for predicting ligand-binding residues based on the residue–ligand interaction embeddings produced by Residue-Ligand Interaction module. Unlike existing sequence-based methods that construct residue feature map through multiple handcrafted stages, our model directly obtains initial residue embeddings using ESM-2, a pretrained protein language model. The Residue–Ligand Interaction module is designed to enable mutual information exchange between the residues in the protein and the ligand through cross-attention, and to construct residue–ligand interaction embeddings by combining the updated residue and ligand embeddings. To achieve this, we apply two cross attention mechanisms: Residue-to-Ligand (R2L) and Ligand-to-Residue (L2R) attention. The Residue-to-Ligand (R2L) attention mechanism incorporates ligand embeddings into each residue embedding, enabling the model to reflect how each residue interacts with the given ligand. Specifically, for each residue x i , the embedding v⃗ residue, i , obtained from the residue encoder, serves as the query. The ligand embedding v⃗ ligand is used as both the key and value. The R2L attention mechanism is formulated in the following eq . 11 v⃗~residue,i=MultiHeadAttn(v⃗residue,i,v⃗ligand,v⃗ligand) In eq , the MultiHeadAttn denotes a 4-head attention mechanism. The Ligand-to-Residue (L2R) attention mechanism, in contrast to R2L, incorporates residue-level context into the ligand embedding, enabling the model to reflect how the ligand attends to the protein. The resulting vector I res–lig, i represents the interaction embedding between residue x i and the ligand, and serves as input to the final binding residue prediction module. The residue-ligand interaction embedding I res–lig, i obtained from the Residue–Ligand Interaction module is used as input to the binding residue predictor. This predictor consists of three feedforward layers and estimates the binding probability between each residue in the protein sequence and the ligand. Based on this probability, each residue is classified as either binding (1) or nonbinding (0). Our model optimizes a weighted combination of two objectives: (1) a weighted binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss for classifying whether a residue is ligand-binding or not, and (2) a contrastive loss , that aims to maximize the distinction between the interaction embeddings of binding and nonbinding residues. The BCE loss trains the model to predict the binding probability of each residue, with a higher weight assigned to the binding class to address the strong imbalance between binding and nonbinding residues. Meanwhile, the contrastive loss is employed to learn discriminative residue–ligand interaction embeddings, encouraging embeddings of binding residues (positive pairs) to cluster closely while pushing those of nonbinding residues (negative pairs) apart in the embedding space. Specifically, the contrastive objective follows the normalized temperature-scaled cross-entropy (NT-Xent) formulation introduced in SimCLR, defined as 16 Lcon=−1N∑i=1Nlog⁡∑j≠i1[yi=yj]exp(sim(zi,zj)/τ)∑k≠iexp(sim(zi,zk)/τ) where N denotes the number of residue–ligand interaction pairs in a mini-batch, z i and z j are the corresponding embeddings, and y i , y j ∈{0, 1} indicate whether each residue is ligand-binding. The indicator function 1 [y i = y j ] equals 1 if the two samples belong to the same class (positive pair) and 0 otherwise. sim­(z i , z j ) represents the cosine similarity, and τ is a temperature parameter controlling the sharpness of the similarity distribution. Therefore, the total loss is defined as 17 L=Lcls+λcon·Lcon where Lcls is the weighted binary cross-entropy loss and Lcon is the contrastive loss computed over residue–ligand interaction embeddings. The hyperparameter λcon controls the influence of the contrastive objective. This joint loss improves robustness to class imbalance by applying weighted supervision, and helps the model learn interaction embeddings that clearly distinguish between binding and nonbinding residues. The model was trained with a batch size of 32 and evaluated with a batch size of 64. We used the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10–4 and a weight decay of 1 × 10–5. A cosine annealing scheduler was applied to gradually reduce the learning rate over epochs. A dropout rate of 0.1 was applied during training to prevent overfitting. All training and inference were conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU. To evaluate models for ligand-binding residue prediction, where binding residues are much fewer than nonbinding ones, we used Precision, Recall, F1-score, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC). The metrics are defined as follows 18 Precision=TPTP+FP 19 Recall(Sensitivity)=TPTP+FN 20 F1−score=2·Precision·RecallPrecision+Recall 21 MCC=TP·TN−FP·FN(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN) In all equations above, TP is the number of binding residues correctly predicted as binding, FP is the number of nonbinding residues incorrectly predicted as binding, TN is the number of nonbinding residues correctly predicted, and FN is binding residues that are incorrectly predicted as nonbinding. Also, to define ligand-binding residues from the model outputs, a classification threshold is required. Therefore, the classification threshold was determined using 5-fold cross-validation on the training set. Specifically, threshold values ranging from 0.1 to 0.9 with a step size of 0.05 were evaluated, and the threshold that maximized the average F2-score on the validation folds was selected. Based on this procedure, a threshold of 0.6 was chosen and fixed for all subsequent experiments. Unlike existing sequence-based methods that construct residue feature map through multiple handcrafted stages, our model directly obtains initial residue embeddings using ESM-2, a pretrained protein language model. Protein language models (PLMs), trained on large-scale protein sequence data sets, are capable of capturing rich contextual, evolutionary, and structural information. Their effectiveness in downstream tasks such as structure prediction, contact map estimation, and functional annotation has been well demonstrated. , By leveraging PLMs, our model obtains biologically meaningful initial embeddings. Our model optimizes a weighted combination of two objectives: (1) a weighted binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss for classifying whether a residue is ligand-binding or not, and (2) a contrastive loss , that aims to maximize the distinction between the interaction embeddings of binding and nonbinding residues. The BCE loss trains the model to predict the binding probability of each residue, with a higher weight assigned to the binding class to address the strong imbalance between binding and nonbinding residues. Meanwhile, the contrastive loss is employed to learn discriminative residue–ligand interaction embeddings, encouraging embeddings of binding residues (positive pairs) to cluster closely while pushing those of nonbinding residues (negative pairs) apart in the embedding space. Specifically, the contrastive objective follows the normalized temperature-scaled cross-entropy (NT-Xent) formulation introduced in SimCLR, defined as 16 Lcon=−1N∑i=1Nlog⁡∑j≠i1[yi=yj]exp(sim(zi,zj)/τ)∑k≠iexp(sim(zi,zk)/τ) where N denotes the number of residue–ligand interaction pairs in a mini-batch, z i and z j are the corresponding embeddings, and y i , y j ∈{0, 1} indicate whether each residue is ligand-binding. The indicator function 1 [y i = y j ] equals 1 if the two samples belong to the same class (positive pair) and 0 otherwise. sim­(z i , z j ) represents the cosine similarity, and τ is a temperature parameter controlling the sharpness of the similarity distribution. Therefore, the total loss is defined as 17 L=Lcls+λcon·Lcon where Lcls is the weighted binary cross-entropy loss and Lcon is the contrastive loss computed over residue–ligand interaction embeddings. The hyperparameter λcon controls the influence of the contrastive objective. This joint loss improves robustness to class imbalance by applying weighted supervision, and helps the model learn interaction embeddings that clearly distinguish between binding and nonbinding residues. The model was trained with a batch size of 32 and evaluated with a batch size of 64. We used the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10–4 and a weight decay of 1 × 10–5. A cosine annealing scheduler was applied to gradually reduce the learning rate over epochs. A dropout rate of 0.1 was applied during training to prevent overfitting. All training and inference were conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU. To evaluate models for ligand-binding residue prediction, where binding residues are much fewer than nonbinding ones, we used Precision, Recall, F1-score, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC). The metrics are defined as follows 18 Precision=TPTP+FP 19 Recall(Sensitivity)=TPTP+FN 20 F1−score=2·Precision·RecallPrecision+Recall 21 MCC=TP·TN−FP·FN(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN) In all equations above, TP is the number of binding residues correctly predicted as binding, FP is the number of nonbinding residues incorrectly predicted as binding, TN is the number of nonbinding residues correctly predicted, and FN is binding residues that are incorrectly predicted as nonbinding. Also, to define ligand-binding residues from the model outputs, a classification threshold is required. Therefore, the classification threshold was determined using 5-fold cross-validation on the training set. Specifically, threshold values ranging from 0.1 to 0.9 with a step size of 0.05 were evaluated, and the threshold that maximized the average F2-score on the validation folds was selected. Based on this procedure, a threshold of 0.6 was chosen and fixed for all subsequent experiments. We conducted a case study to assess the practical utility of our model in virtual screening for the EGFR target (PDB ID: 2RGP), using the DUD-E benchmark data set. The data set includes 542 active ligands and 35,050 decoys. Active compounds refer to molecules that have been experimentally confirmed to bind to the target protein, while decoys are molecules with similar physicochemical properties but dissimilar topologies that are assumed, though not experimentally validated, to be nonbinders and are widely used to assess the specificity and robustness of virtual screening methods. , All actives were used, and following common practice in virtual screening benchmarks, we randomly sampled decoys at a 1:50 active-to-decoy ratio using five different random seeds to obtain five independent replicates. For each active and decoy ligand, our model predicted the ligand-binding residues of the EGFR target, and the geometric center of the predicted residues was then defined as the docking pocket center. Docking was performed using AutoDock Vina with a 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å search box centered at the predicted pocket. For each ligand, only the best binding affinity among the generated poses was used, and the resulting scores were used to rank both active and decoy ligands. Based on the docking scores, we evaluated the virtual screening performance of our model using the enrichment factor (EF) and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC). , The enrichment factor (EF) measures how much more frequently active compounds appear in the top k percent of the ranked ligand list based on predicted binding affinities, compared to their proportion in the overall data set. We used EF@1%, EF@5%, and EF@10%. EF values greater than 1 indicate that the model is able to effectively select active compounds. Values close to 1 suggest no particular screening ability. We performed docking-based binding affinity evaluation to assess the practical utility of binding residue prediction models in virtual screening. , To perform this evaluation, we used the ligand-binding residues predicted by our model, Pseq2sites, and P2Rank. AutoDock Vina was then used to estimate binding affinities, with the docking center defined by the geometric center of the predicted ligand-binding residues. The search space was set to a grid box of 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å centered at this point, and the exhaustiveness was set to 8. We conducted a case study to assess the practical utility of our model in virtual screening for the EGFR target (PDB ID: 2RGP), using the DUD-E benchmark data set. The data set includes 542 active ligands and 35,050 decoys. Active compounds refer to molecules that have been experimentally confirmed to bind to the target protein, while decoys are molecules with similar physicochemical properties but dissimilar topologies that are assumed, though not experimentally validated, to be nonbinders and are widely used to assess the specificity and robustness of virtual screening methods. , All actives were used, and following common practice in virtual screening benchmarks, we randomly sampled decoys at a 1:50 active-to-decoy ratio using five different random seeds to obtain five independent replicates. For each active and decoy ligand, our model predicted the ligand-binding residues of the EGFR target, and the geometric center of the predicted residues was then defined as the docking pocket center. Docking was performed using AutoDock Vina with a 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å search box centered at the predicted pocket. For each ligand, only the best binding affinity among the generated poses was used, and the resulting scores were used to rank both active and decoy ligands. Based on the docking scores, we evaluated the virtual screening performance of our model using the enrichment factor (EF) and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC). , The enrichment factor (EF) measures how much more frequently active compounds appear in the top k percent of the ranked ligand list based on predicted binding affinities, compared to their proportion in the overall data set. We used EF@1%, EF@5%, and EF@10%. EF values greater than 1 indicate that the model is able to effectively select active compounds. Values close to 1 suggest no particular screening ability. As shown in Table , virtual screening using our model predicted binding centers achieved EF values of 1.49, 2.44, and 3.16 at the top 1, 5, and 10%, respectively. These results demonstrate that our model can reduce a large initial compound library to a smaller subset enriched with promising candidates in the early stage of screening. In this study, we proposed LiBRe, a ligand-aware sequence-based deep learning model for predicting protein–ligand binding residues. Unlike existing sequence-based methods that rely only on protein sequence information, LiBRe overcomes the limitations of ligand-unaware models by explicitly incorporating ligand information. Although binding residue prediction is a highly imbalanced classification task, our model demonstrates robustness by achieving higher performance than baseline methods across all evaluation metrics except Recall on the benchmark data sets. Embedding visualizations further indicate that our model is ligand-aware, as interaction embeddings vary depending on the bound ligand even for identical protein sequences. Additionally, structural visualizations reveal that the predicted binding residues form pockets that closely resemble known binding pockets, suggesting that our model can accurately identify ligand-binding regions even without structural information. Based on these findings, we conducted docking-based binding affinity evaluations using the predicted binding centers, and observed that our model enables stronger and more stable affinity predictions compared to baseline methods. To assess the practical applicability of our model, we conducted a case study on the EGFR target from the DUD-E database. Using the predicted binding residues from our model as docking centers, our model successfully prioritized active compounds over decoys in virtual screening, achieving meaningful enrichment factors and a high ROC-AUC. These results confirm that LiBRe is not only effective for binding residue prediction but also readily applicable to structure-free virtual screening pipelines. While LiBRe achieves promising results, it still leaves room for further improvement. Currently, the model does not leverage structural information, which may limit its ability to capture detailed spatial interactions. Additionally, LiBRe is designed for single protein–ligand binding scenarios, which restricts its ability to model complex interactions such as multiple ligand binding events. The proposed framework could also be extended toward protein-type-aware ligand-binding residue prediction, enabling more specialized modeling of diverse binding patterns across different classes of proteins. Future work may address these limitations by developing a hybrid architecture that leverages both sequence and structural information, and by incorporating data reflecting multiple ligand binding scenarios. These advancements are expected to further improve virtual screening performance and contribute to the acceleration of drug discovery.
