The growing threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a critical challenge to global public health, with far-reaching clinical and economic consequences. Misuse and overuse of antibiotics have accelerated the emergence of drug-resistant pathogens, diminishing the effectiveness of existing treatments [1]. In 2019 alone, AMR was estimated to have directly caused 1.27 million deaths and was associated with a total of 4.95 million deaths, making AMR one of the leading causes of infectious disease mortality worldwide [2]. If left unmitigated, AMR is projected to claim over 10 million lives annually by 2050 and result in cumulative economic losses exceeding USD 100 trillion [3]. These trends call for a fundamental shift in antimicrobial development, moving beyond conventional antibiotics toward novel therapeutic modalities with broader mechanisms of action. To address this escalating threat, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have emerged as a promising alternative to traditional small-molecule antibiotics. These short peptides exhibit broad-spectrum activity, rapid membrane-disruptive mechanisms, and a reduced likelihood of resistance development [4, 5]. However, their clinical translation remains limited, in part due to challenges in discovering potent and selective candidates. Traditional AMP discovery workflows rely heavily on high-throughput screening and rational sequence design, which are time-intensive, costly, and difficult to scale [6–8]. Moreover, the resulting peptides often exhibit suboptimal biochemical properties—such as excessive charge, instability, or high nonspecific protein-binding potential—which compromise therapeutic selectivity and safety. Consequently, there is a growing demand for efficient, scalable computational methods to accelerate AMP discovery while expanding the structural and functional diversity of peptide candidates. In recent years, a range of AI-driven approaches have emerged to accelerate the discovery and design of AMPs. Generative models such as PepGAN [9] and AMPGAN v2 [10] utilize generative adversarial networks (GANs) to optimize peptide properties. Variational autoencoders HydrAMP [11] and CLaSS [12] facilitate diverse sequence generation through latent feature representations, while deep-learning classifiers (e.g. LMPred [13] and iAMPCN [14]) predict AMP activity via CNN and Bi-LSTM architectures. Other models, such as ensemble methods (Ensemble-AMPPred [15]) and traditional Random Forest (RF) classifiers (Macrel [16]) further enhance prediction robustness. Multi-modular pipeline based AMP discovery framework is proposed with multiple activity prediction models for active peptide funnel-like filtering and search space reduction [17]. Collectively, these AI-driven tools significantly improve computational screening efficiency in AMP discovery. Despite these advances, current AI-driven AMP discovery methods face critical limitations. First, most existing models rely solely on computational predictions without experimental validation of antimicrobial potency or safety. Second, most models define antimicrobial activity using permissive thresholds, such as the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) thresholds up to 128 μg/ml. This often leads to the inclusion of peptides with weak or negligible antimicrobial effects as ‘active,’ reducing the precision and interpretability of the screening results. Furthermore, while most models achieve high predictive accuracy, they often operate as black boxes, lacking biochemical interpretability and providing limited insight into the molecular features that determine AMP activity. This makes it difficult to uncover the sequence-function relationships or rationalize model decisions in a biologically meaningful way. To overcome these limitations, we developed CVAE-BIO, a biological-knowledge-driven, multi-module pipeline for the discovery of novel AMPs against E. coli. By integrating a conditional variational autoencoder (CVAE) with biochemical constraints for AMP sequence generation and an explainable activity classifier, CVAE-BIO efficiently generates and prioritizes peptides with desirable antimicrobial and biochemical profiles. Selected candidates were validated through in vitro assays, demonstrating both high potency and narrow-spectrum activity. This framework provides a scalable and interpretable alternative to existing AMP discovery approaches. We developed a multi-module computational–experimental pipeline (Fig. 1a) for the discovery of bioactive AMPs, guided by biochemical knowledge. In Module I, a CVAE, trained by APD3 [18] peptides with documented activity against E. coli, served as a generative model, producing novel peptide candidates with conditions on high predicted activity (MIC ≤10 μg/mL) and tailored biochemical properties, including sequence length (length < 40 residues), predicted in vivo stability (instability index <40) [19], nonspecific binding potential (Boman Index <0) [20], and positive net charge at pH 7 (charge > + 2) (Fig. 1b). To further prioritize peptides with high antimicrobial potential, in Module II, the generated sequences are evaluated using an AMP classification model based on a RF, trained on an expanded panel of 30 biochemical descriptors (Fig. 1c), to enhance discrimination of active peptides and to provide interpretable guidance for the design of novel AMPs. In the final module, predicted active peptides were subjected to wet-lab validation, including in vitro antimicrobial activity assays against a panel of eight clinically relevant bacterial pathogens, and evaluated at two screening concentrations (50 μg/mL and 10 μg/mL) to confirm efficacy and functional relevance (Fig. 1d). To embed multi-label information and directly condition generation on specified biochemical properties, the CVAE projected each label stream into the latent space via a linear weighted combination of subspace projectors [21]. The effectiveness of conditional generation can be assessed by how well generated peptides satisfy the imposed biochemical constraints. Using all peptides from the APD database as initial input, the trained CVAE produced a pool of novel candidates that closely met the required criteria: 88.4% had net charge between +2 and + 8, 99.9% had length < 40 residues, and 97.4% had instability index <40. For the Boman index, 99.3% had values below 1, with 86.4% below 0 (Supplementary Table 1). The subspace-projector weights are learnable and, after convergence, the highest one was for the Boman index (2.64) compared with the one for MIC condition (1.66) (Supplementary Table 2). The AMP activity prediction model selected for this study was a RF classifier incorporating a panel of 30 peptide biochemical features. This choice was informed by a comparative analysis of multiple predictive models for identifying peptides with MIC ≤10 μg/mL against E. coli. These include RF and support vector machine (SVM) models trained on peptide handcrafted biochemical features, as well as deep-learning approaches utilizing contextualized language model embeddings from Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT [22]) and Text-To-Text Transfer Transformer (T5 [23]) processed through convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [13]. We also evaluated the HydrAMP-Classifier, which uses a recurrent long short-term memory (LSTM) architecture specifically designed for AMP prediction [11]. The performance of seven machine learning classifiers was assessed on the 20% held-out test set, with all models retrained using the same training and testing split from the APD dataset to ensure a fair comparison. Among all models, the RF classifier trained on 30 curated biochemical features consistently achieved the highest predictive performance in both receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and precision-recall (PR) analyses (Fig. 2a–b), which achieved the highest area under the ROC curve (AUROC = 0.793, 95% CI: 0.740–0.834) and the highest area under the PR curve (AUPRC = 0.634, 95% CI: 0.545–0.732). The next best-performing model was HydrAMP-Classifier (AUROC = 0.775, 95% CI: 0.724–0.825; AUPRC = 0.602, 95% CI: 0.509–0.696), followed by the CNN using T5 language model embeddings (AUROC = 0.759, 95% CI: 0.706–0.807; AUPRC = 0.596, 95% CI: 0.507–0.684). Models relying on sequence embeddings as protein language modeling, such as the RF using BERT embeddings and the CNN using BERT embeddings (CNN_BERT), resulted in lower performance. Furthermore, although also trained on biochemical features, the SVM model underperformed relative to RF model (AUROC = 0.748, 95% CI: 0.689–0.805; AUPRC = 0.582, 95% CI: 0.481–0.675). To explore the structure–activity relationships captured by the RF model, SHAP value of APD dataset peptides derived from the RF model were embedded to two dimensions by UMAP algorithm and clustered using KMeans (k = 6) (Fig. 3a). The resulting clusters exhibited clear separation in the UMAP space, with clusters 0–3 predominantly comprising inactive peptides (MIC > 10 μg/mL) and Clusters 4 and 5 enriched for active peptides (MIC ≤ 10 μg/mL). The boundary separating clusters 0–3 from 4 and 5 aligned with the classification boundary of the RF model. Scaled importance is calculated as the mean SHAP value weighted by the proportion of peptides with non-zero contributions. Projecting the testing set into the UMAP space and applying KMeans clustering showed that the cluster distribution in the testing set closely matched that of the training set (Fig. 3b). Notably, Cluster 4 contained the highest proportion of active peptides (0.72) in the testing set, indicating high potency within this group. Comparative analysis of feature importance and feature values across clusters (Fig. 3c–d) revealed that the importance of specific features in determining antimicrobial potency varies across different peptide clusters. Across clusters, charge, isoelectric point, basic and charged residue content, and hydrophobic moment significantly contributing to activity classification, although their relative contributions vary by cluster. Cluster 4 peptides exhibited strong cationic properties—such as high net charge, isoelectric point, and a high proportion of basic and charged residues—as well as pronounced amphipathic character, indicated by elevated hydrophobic moment despite low overall hydrophobicity. In terms of feature contributions, charge, isoelectric point, the percentages of basic and charged residues, and hydrophobic moment had strong positive SHAP values in Cluster 4, while low hydrophobicity also contributed significantly positively to the active classification (Fig. 3c). Both Cluster 4 and Cluster 3 exhibited similar cationic and amphipathic feature profiles (Fig. 3c–d) but were clearly separated from each other (Fig. 3a). The main distinguishing factor for Cluster 4 was its significantly lower absolute counts of tiny residues (such as A, C, and G), small residues (such as A, C, F, G, P, and V), and non-polar residues (such as A, C, F, G, I, L, M, P, V, W, and Y), compared to Cluster 3. Testing set results also showed that, compared to Cluster 4, Cluster 3’s activity profile was more ambiguous, with an active percentage of only 0.41 despite exhibiting similar amphipathic and cationic features. To evaluate the real-world effectiveness of our peptide generation models, we synthesized 54 novel peptide sequences. These sequences were generated by the trained CVAE model in Module I and selected by the trained activity prediction model in Module II of the CVAE-BIO pipeline, using a probability threshold corresponding to 0.8 precision for active classification. We evaluated the antimicrobial activity of the peptides against E. coli at 50 μg/mL and 10 μg/mL (Fig. 4a) and assessed their toxicity by measuring hemolysis of human erythrocytes at 50 μg/mL (Fig. 4b). Peptides that were active against E. coli at 50 μg/mL were further tested against the ESKAPE pathogen panel at 10 μg/mL (Fig. 5). The effective peptides at 10 μg/mL were further tested with MIC and compared with Arenicin-3-NZ17228 and chloramphenicol (Table 1). Most CVAE-BIO-generated peptides showed potent, narrow-spectrum antimicrobial activity. Differentiated from Arenicin-3-NZ17228 which targets all tested five Gram-negative peptides, CVAE-BIO-36 exhibited the widest activity spectrum, inhibiting growth of 4 bacterial strains, including both E. coli strains, E. cloacae, A. baumannii, and K. pneumoniae. No peptides showed effect on Enterococcus faecalis or Pseudomonas aeruginosa at 10 μg/mL. These peptides displayed comparable activity relative to the benchmark peptides Arenicin-3-NZ17228 and Arenicin-3-NZ17224, particularly against Gram-negative targets. In terms of narrow-spectrum antimicrobial activity, eight novel generated peptides displayed such activity at a concentration of 10 μg/mL. Five peptides; CVAE-BIO-10, CVAE-BIO-41, CVAE-BIO-46, CVAE-BIO-48, and CVAE-BIO-49, exhibited activity exclusively against E. coli. CVAE-BIO-24 was solely effective against the multidrug-resistant pathogen A. baumannii. Additionally, CVAE-BIO-9 displayed selective inhibition of the Gram-negative bacterium E. cloacae, while CVAE-BIO-11 exhibited specific inhibitory effects against S. aureus. Regarding hemolytic toxicity of the 54 CVAE-BIO–generated peptides (Fig. 4b), we applied thresholds of 10% and 5% hemolysis of human erythrocytes at 50 μg/mL to denote acceptable [24, 25] and negligible toxicity [26], respectively. Under these criteria, 42 of the 54 peptides induced <10% hemolysis (acceptable toxicity), and 38 peptides caused ≤5% hemolysis (negligible toxicity). Taking both antimicrobial activity and hemolytic toxicity into account, CVAE-BIO-8, CVAE-BIO-13, CVAE-BIO-14, CVAE-BIO-31, CVAE-BIO-35, CVAE-BIO-37, CVAE-BIO-44, CVAE-BIO-45, and CVAE-BIO-53 were identified as non-toxic, active AMP candidates (Fig. 5). Through the biochemical feature distribution comparison shown in Fig. 6, it is evident that all 54 CVAE-BIO-generated peptides satisfied the predefined biochemical constraints (Fig. 6a–d): all peptides had a length of less than 22 residues, a net charge ranging from +3.7 to +7, an instability index below 13 and 53 out of 54 peptides possess a Boman index less than 1. Additionally, this set of peptides exhibited distinct cationic-amphipathic characteristics (Fig. 6b and e–h): the net charge, isoelectric point, proportions of charged residues, and hydrophobic moment at 100° were all at significantly high levels compared to the overall APD3 peptides, while their hydrophobicity remains near zero (ranging from −0.5 to 0.85). Taking both antimicrobial activity and hemolytic toxicity into account, CVAE-BIO-8, CVAE-BIO-13, CVAE-BIO-14, CVAE-BIO-31, CVAE-BIO-35, CVAE-BIO-37, CVAE-BIO-44, CVAE-BIO-45, and CVAE-BIO-53 were identified as non-toxic, active AMP candidates (Fig. 5). Through the biochemical feature distribution comparison shown in Fig. 6, it is evident that all 54 CVAE-BIO-generated peptides satisfied the predefined biochemical constraints (Fig. 6a–d): all peptides had a length of less than 22 residues, a net charge ranging from +3.7 to +7, an instability index below 13 and 53 out of 54 peptides possess a Boman index less than 1. Additionally, this set of peptides exhibited distinct cationic-amphipathic characteristics (Fig. 6b and e–h): the net charge, isoelectric point, proportions of charged residues, and hydrophobic moment at 100° were all at significantly high levels compared to the overall APD3 peptides, while their hydrophobicity remains near zero (ranging from −0.5 to 0.85). The CVAE-BIO pipeline, a biochemical knowledge-driven peptide generation framework, shows the feasibility and advantages of artificially designing and generating novel AMPs within the space of aggregated biochemical features. Its multi-modular architecture fully integrates biochemical information; Module I employs a multi-CVAE to generate novel peptide candidates within a targeted feature space [17], ensuring key biochemical properties such as optimal charge, high predicted stability, and low nonspecific binding potential. Module II utilizes a biochemical feature-based AMP activity prediction model, leveraging a comprehensive set of biochemical descriptors to further filter and prioritize the most promising candidates. This collaborative workflow between modules efficiently streamlines the selection of high-quality AMP candidates. Finally, in vitro wet-lab validation (Module III) confirmed the practical value of the selected peptides by evaluating their antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive, Gram-negative, broad-spectrum, and narrow-spectrum targets, underscoring the pipeline’s effectiveness for AMP discovery and development. The RF classifier, trained on 30 biochemical descriptors, outperformed other models and provided interpretable insights into the characteristics of highly potent peptide clusters. The performance comparison included deep-learning approaches utilizing contextualized language model embeddings and sequence information. RF classifier also enabled SHAP-based clustering and feature importance analysis, revealing distinct biochemical profiles among peptide clusters. One active peptide cluster was characterized by strong cationic and amphipathic properties: high net charge, isoelectric point, high proportion of basic and charged residues, pronounced hydrophobic moment, and low hydrophobicity. These features enabled the model to confirm activity, supporting the conclusion that cationic-amphipathic peptides are more likely to be active [27]. Another cationic-amphipathic cluster was identified but classified as inactive, and showed ambiguous activity in testing set. Biochemical feature comparison found this cluster tends to have more tiny, small, and non-polar residues, which are unrelated to hydrophobicity or cationicity, suggesting that the cationic-amphipathic AMP phenotype may require low counts of such residues. This finding offered new insight into how the hydrophobic and polar residue balance influences activity [6] and would also further refine the cationic-amphipathic AMP phenotype [27]. The in vitro validation results (Fig. 5 and Table 1) highlight the strong performance of the CVAE-BIO pipeline in generating AMPs. These findings support the idea that narrowing the sequence search space using biochemically meaningful constraints does not impede the discovery of potent AMPs, as long as an effective filtering strategy is applied. By constraining charge, predicted stability, length, and nonspecific binding potential (Fig. 6a–d), the CVAE-BIO-generated peptides achieved a 38.9% success rate (21 out of 54 peptides) at an MIC threshold of 50 μg/mL, and an 18.5% success rate (10 out of 54 peptides) specifically against E. coli. These results are comparable to other state-of-art AMP generation frameworks that have reported wet-lab validations. For example, wet-lab validation of HydrAMP [11] showed a 38.5% success rate (10 out of 26 peptides) at 50 μg/mL and a 19.2% success rate (5 out of 26 peptides) against E. coli at 10 μg/mL. The performance of CVAE-BIO is also comparable to Joker [28] and CLaSS [12] (Supplementary Fig. 2). We have no intention of making head-to-head comparison of these models as they were developed using different datasets with wet-lab validations under different conditions. Instead, it provides an order-of-magnitude benchmark for the ability of CVAE-BIO to generate experimentally validated active AMPs. Importantly, the generated peptides displayed a wide range of antimicrobial activities, both broad-spectrum and narrow-spectrum in vitro active AMP. Specifically, those exhibiting narrow-spectrum activity may offer clinical advantages when the causative pathogen has been identified, as they enable strain-specific targeting while minimizing disruption to the host microbiome, but maybe not feasible to low-resource settings or may need longer time for treatment decision. For example, CVAE-BIO-44 and CVAE-BIO-45 showed narrow-spectrum activity at or less than 10 μg/mL specifically against Gram-negative Enterobacteriaceae pathogen E. coli and E. cloacae (Fig. 5 and Table 1). This MIC was comparable to that of the broad-spectrum antibiotic chloramphenicol and the AMP Arenicin-3-NZ17228. And combining with the toxicity assessment, these two peptides can be considered as non-toxic narrow-spectrum AMP candidates. However, we did not fully replicate reported records of the 0.125 μg/mL for Arenicin-3-NZ17228 and 0.6 μg/mL for Arenicin-3-NZ17224 on E. coli ATCC 25922 [29]. This discrepancy may likely reflect differences in assay conditions, peptide synthesis, or bacterial strain passage history, but overall supports our wet lab setup as technically consistent and within an expected range of sensitivity. Wet-lab validation further supported the characterization of highly active (MIC ≤ 10 μg/mL) cationic-amphipathic AMP phenotype with significantly low levels of tiny and small residues, providing valuable insights into the determinants of AMP activity. Previous studies have shown that misplacement of alanine or glycine can hinder activity [30, 31]. Our findings demonstrate that lower counts of tiny and small residues align with higher activity. Specifically, when these residue counts exceeded 2 and 3, respectively, peptides were much more likely to have MIC values above 10 μg/mL or be inactive. Thus, future designs of highly active cationic-amphipathic AMPs should minimize or avoid tiny and small residues, or constrain counts to a maximum of 2 and 3, to reduce false positives. The CVAE-BIO pipeline’s novelty lies in conditioning generation and prediction on a stringent MIC threshold (≤ 10 μg/mL) for E. coli, rather than binary AMP/non-AMP labels or more permissive cutoffs like 32 μg/mL used in existing models [11, 12]. This allows finer control and greater specificity for antimicrobial potency during peptide generation, guiding discovery toward peptides with high therapeutic potential. However, this conservative cutoff reduces the active peptide group’s size, increasing class imbalance during training and possibly limiting identification of moderately active peptides that could be optimized. Nonetheless, our results demonstrate high-stringency MIC conditioning is feasible and effective, though future work should address data imbalance. In summary, the CVAE-BIO pipeline offers an effective, interpretable and biochemical knowledge-driven framework for the rational design and generation of novel AMPs. But several method-level limitations of the CVAE-BIO pipeline should be acknowledged. First, the generative model is trained exclusively on APD3 peptides with known activity against E. coli at 10 μg/mL, which may limit the generalisability of the model to other bacterial species or broader pathogen classes. Future extensions of the pipeline should incorporate more diverse training datasets to enable AMP discovery targeting different pathogens. Second, while the biochemical constraints used in conditional generation, such as net charge, Boman index, and instability index, are well supported by previous studies, they remain simplifications of complex pharmacological and biological interactions. Incorporating additional layers of biological realism, such as membrane interaction modeling, and toxicity constraints could further enhance the utility of the generator. Third, although the RF classifier showed strong predictive performance and interpretability, it remains a static filtering step that does not adapt in real-time to feedback from wet-lab validation. Future iterations could consider adaptive learning to improve the efficiency of candidate selection over iterative design cycles. Exploring hybrid approaches that combine biochemical descriptors with contextual embeddings from protein language models is also an important direction for future work. The CVAE architecture comprises three core components: an encoder, a latent space sampler with reparameterisation, and a decoder. The model extends a standard VAE framework [35] by incorporating a linear conditional projector for five independent label streams, allowing fine-grained control of the generative process. The encoder is a fully connected feedforward neural network receiving a 3100-dimensional input (one-hot encoded peptide sequence, 20 digits per slot, max length 155 residues), passing through hidden layers of 512, 256, and 8 units, with ReLU activation per layer for abstraction and compression. The choice of an 8-dimensional latent space aligns with common deep-learning practice of using power-of-two layer widths [36]. Moreover, (8 = 23) is the smallest power of two that exceeds the number of conditional constraints (five), providing sufficient capacity to encode information of both constraints and sequences. The encoder’s final output computes the mean (μ) and log variance (log σ2) of the latent distribution, sampled using the standard trick: The decoder mirrors the encoder, reconstructing the input from the latent representation via dense layers of 256, 512, and 3100 units, with ReLU activations in hidden layers and a final sigmoid activation outputting value in [0, 1]. To condition the generation process on the biochemical constraints, we introduced five independent label streams and used a combination of subspace projectors [21] to project the label streams to the latent space. The RF model was trained to classify peptide sequences using 30 biochemical and biophysical features. Hyperparameters, including number of estimators (10, 50, 100, 200), minimum samples to split a node (2, 5, 10), and samples per split or leaf (auto, sqrt, log2), were optimized via 5-fold cross-validated grid search (GridSearchCV). The SVM model used the same features and was tuned using manual grid search over various kernel types and hyperparameters, also with 5-fold stratified cross-validation. The parameter grid included regularization strength (C: 0.1, 1, 10, 100), kernel type, gamma (scale, auto), and for polynomial kernels, degree (2, 3, 4); kernel types were mapped to libsvm codes. SVM input features were standardized with StandardScaler for numerical stability. To generate contextualized embeddings of peptide sequences, we employed pre-trained protein language models based on the Transformer architecture [13]. Specifically, we utilized T5 and BERT. These embeddings served as input features for a CNN classifier following the LMPred framework [13], to perform peptide activity prediction. For integration with RF and SVM classifiers, we reduced BERT embedding dimensionality while preserving key spatial and contextual information. We constructed a lightweight 1D CNN encoder: two convolutional layers (32 and 16 filters, ReLU activation, same padding), a max-pooling layer, and a final global average pooling layer, yielding fixed-length latent vectors per sequence. The resulting compressed embeddings were then used as input for training the RF and SVM classifiers. To systematically evaluate model performance, we performed random split, assigning 80% of the sequences to the training set and 20% to a held-out test set while preserving the proportion of active (MIC ≤10 μg/mL) and inactive peptides. All classification models were trained and tuned exclusively on the training set using cross-validated grid search, and their final performance was assessed on the independent 20% test partition. The held-out test set was not used at any stage for model fitting, or hyperparameter optimization. Model performance was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and area under the PR curve (AUPRC) as primary metrics. Confidence intervals for AUROC and AUPRC were estimated for each model using nonparametric bootstrapping. The final bacterial inoculum was 5 × 104 CFU/well in 100 μL. Plates were incubated for 16–24 h at 37°C and OD600 was determined to calculate the inhibition ratio. Selected peptides were subjected to MIC testing (MICs) using 96-well non-binding surface polystyrene plates. The MIC was recorded as the lowest concentration of peptide in the assay wells that show no visible bacterial growth. Assays were performed in Cation-Adjusted Mueller Hinton Broth for bacteria. The concentrations of the peptides and comparator antibiotics in the assay wells ranged from either 20 or 50 μg/mL to 0.16 or 0.4 μg/mL, following a 2-fold dilution series across the wells of the plate in triplicate for each independent biological replicate. The final cell density was 5 × 104 CFU/well for different bacteria in 100 μL per well. The MICs were determined visually after 18–20 hours of incubation at 37°C for bacteria. All experiments were performed in a minimum of triplicates (n ≥ 3). Hemolytic activity was assessed using isolated human erythrocytes from fresh blood donations, adjusted to 1 × 109 cells/mL in PBS (pH 7.4) and placed in V-bottom 96-well plates. Compounds at 50 μg/mL were added and incubated for 3 hours at 37°C. Samples were then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 minutes; 100 μL supernatant was collected for OD 540 measurement. The MIC was recorded as the lowest concentration of peptide in the assay wells that show no visible bacterial growth. Assays were performed in Cation-Adjusted Mueller Hinton Broth for bacteria. The concentrations of the peptides and comparator antibiotics in the assay wells ranged from either 20 or 50 μg/mL to 0.16 or 0.4 μg/mL, following a 2-fold dilution series across the wells of the plate in triplicate for each independent biological replicate. The final cell density was 5 × 104 CFU/well for different bacteria in 100 μL per well. The MICs were determined visually after 18–20 hours of incubation at 37°C for bacteria. All experiments were performed in a minimum of triplicates (n ≥ 3). Hemolytic activity was assessed using isolated human erythrocytes from fresh blood donations, adjusted to 1 × 109 cells/mL in PBS (pH 7.4) and placed in V-bottom 96-well plates. Compounds at 50 μg/mL were added and incubated for 3 hours at 37°C. Samples were then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 minutes; 100 μL supernatant was collected for OD 540 measurement. Pure water induced 100% hemolysis as a positive control. All toxicity assays were performed in two independent biological replicates. The proposed CVAE-BIO pipeline is a multi-module system for generating potent antimicrobial peptides, integrating a conditional variational autoencoder (CVAE) with biochemical constraints, an antimicrobial activity prediction model trained on 30 biochemical descriptors, and wet-lab validation for activity confirmation. Experimental validation showed the effectiveness of CVAE-BIO: 18.5% of generated peptides showed strong antimicrobial activity (MIC ≤10 μg/mL), and 38.9% had MIC ≤50 μg/mL while maintaining desirable biochemical properties. A SHAP-based analysis of the antimicrobial activity classifier, supported by wet-lab validation, revealed that highly active cationic-amphipathic AMPs are characterized by significantly low counts of tiny and small residues. Most validated novel peptides are narrow-spectrum AMP targeting E. coli.
