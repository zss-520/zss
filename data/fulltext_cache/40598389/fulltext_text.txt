Antimicrobial peptide (AMP) prediction has been extensively studied in recent years. However, many existing models do not fully leverage the intrinsic chemical structures of AMPs, such as atomic composition and sidechain group characteristics. Instead, these models often focus on letter composition, positional encodings, and pre-defined chemical-physical descriptors. The incorporation of non-canonical amino acids, which enhance peptide stability and reduce toxicity, is getting more attention in peptide design. Despite this, they are overlooked in predictive studies, as traditional deciphering methods and single-letter representation systems are inadequate for this task. Even though some efforts have been made to expand current alphabets, these approaches remain insufficient, impeding the development of novel AMPs. The discovery of antibiotics in the twentieth century revolutionized the treatment of bacterial infections. However, the widespread use of antibiotics in healthcare and agriculture has led to the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), a serious threat to global health. In 2019, bacterial AMR and its complications were associated with approximately 4.95 million deaths. Multidrug-resistant bacteria, particularly the ESKAPE pathogens—Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter species—are increasingly resistant to existing antibiotics [1]. With the decline of the traditional “Waksman platform” for antibiotic discovery, research has increasingly shifted toward genome mining for natural antimicrobial products, particularly antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) containing non-canonical amino acids (NCAAs). AMPs are of particular interest due to their prolonged resistance development time, potent antimicrobial efficacy, and lower incidence of resistance compared to synthetic antibiotics. Their mechanism of action involves disrupting bacterial cell membranes, altering permeability, and causing cell lysis, making them less susceptible to resistance [2–4]. Recent advancements in computer science have significantly enhanced antibiotic discovery. Models are now capable of accurately predicting the antimicrobial activity or minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of peptides, facilitating high-throughput screening of large peptide datasets obtained through genome mining or chemical space exploration. These computational approaches expedite the identification of promising AMPs, reducing the need for extensive experimental testing and accelerating the discovery process [5, 6]. Various learning methods have been introduced to address AMP prediction problems. The most common approaches are classifiers and generators. Classifiers determine whether the input sequence is an AMP, while generators, typically based on autoregressive models trained on AMP sequences, generate potential new AMPs. For instance, AmPEP by Bhadra et al. implemented a random forest classifier trained on AMP sequences encoded by amino acid distribution patterns to classify peptides [7]. AMPScanner by Veltri et al. employs a long short-term memory (LSTM) neural network to predict antimicrobial probability [8]. Huang et al. developed a machine-learning pipeline combining XGBoost and LSTM to explore possible amino acid combinations of lengths 6–9, validating the top MIC peptides [9]. Ma et al. utilized a Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) model as part of a pipeline integrated with Multi-Omics approach to mine potential AMPs in human gut microbiomes [10]. Wang et al. leveraged 3D structural and sequence information to predict and generate peptides with potent antimicrobial activity [11]. Szymczak et al. developed HydrAMP, a conditional variational autoencoder, to generate potent AMPs [12]. Despite these advances, most studies continue to rely on a standardized workflow for encoding peptides using predefined feature descriptors, such as compositions, autocorrelation methods (e.g., Moran and Geary), frequency-domain features, and physicochemical properties [7, 13–15]. Alternatively, some studies extract features through position encoding or leverage Transformer and BERT models to learn sequence information [16–18]. These approaches primarily focus on contextual classification by modeling character positions, composition, and phase relationships [19]. More recently, Yan et al. employed Graph Attention Networks (GAT) and incorporated predicted 3D structures to improve AMP activity prediction [20]. Pretrained protein language models (PLMs) and conformer prediction models, such as ProtTrans [21], the Evolutionary Scale Modeling (ESM) suite [22], and AlphaFold [23], have transformed protein deep learning technologies. Leveraging AlphaFold2’s predicted distance thresholds, Zhao et al. represented protein structures as graph data by encoding node features with a pretrained Bi-LSTM protein language model [24]. Similarly, Cordoves-Delgado et al. developed the esm-AxP-GDL framework, which integrates advanced protein structure prediction, evolutionary language models, and graph neural networks to achieve state-of-the-art performance in AMP prediction using learned features rather than physicochemical descriptors [25]. However, most studies often overlook structural variations in amino acids and terminal modifications. NCAAs are either represented by the placeholder “X” in sequence or excluded from analyses entirely. As a result, these methods are incapable of processing the extensive NCAA space [26], which represents a vast area for exploration. Peptides containing NCAAs exhibit enhanced stability and potent antimicrobial activities [27]; most are non-ribosomal peptides (NRPs) such as Brevicidine and Laterocidine [28], Teixobactin [29], Clovibactin [30], Cilagicin [31], Corbomycin [32], and the well-known Vancomycin [33]. The growing interest in incorporating NCAAs into antimicrobial peptides to improve their properties is gaining more attention. Their distinct sidechains and/or conformations can confer novel biochemical properties to newly designed or discovered peptides [34, 35]. Recent studies have successfully leveraged chemical structure-based approaches to identify small-molecule antibiotics, such as Halicin [36] and Abaucin [37], as well as to develop predictive models [38, 39]. Notably, H. Chang et al. proposed a method employing Gaussian process regression (GPR) to predict peptides represented by the Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES). Their approach calculated over 200 physicochemical descriptors for each peptide, successfully incorporated a limited NCAA dataset, and designed analogs with potent antifungal activity. Building on recent advancements, we propose a manual feature engineering-free, heterogeneous graph-based model, termed AmpHGT, to address the challenges of representing NCAAs in peptide classification. Unlike conventional PLM-based approaches [25, 40, 41], AmpHGT represents peptides as chemical compound graphs, explicitly modeling amino acid backbones, NCAA sidechains, and pretrained embeddings. This structure allows for a richer representation than single-letter encoding while being more straightforward for constructing heterogeneous graphs compared to the Hierarchical Editing Language for Macromolecules (HELM). Overall, AmpHGT enhances antimicrobial peptide classification by integrating multi-view structural information, enabling the processing of over 600 NCAAs in addition to the 20 canonical amino acids (CAAs). A novel deep learning model, termed AmpHGT, was developed based on heterogeneous graphs’ representation of peptides. AmpHGT demonstrates competitive performance against current methods on canonical amino acid benchmarks. Notably, AmpHGT is capable of efficiently classifying antimicrobial peptides with non-canonical amino acids, addressing the limitations of traditional feature extraction methods. In addition, this model is adaptable to handling different conformations, sidechains, and backbones (e.g., α, β, γ), demonstrating its potential to enhance the screening and discovery of AMPs containing non-canonical amino acids. In this study, we employed a multi-view heterogeneous graph to represent peptide structures, facilitating informative peptide structure embedding. Residue-level embeddings were extracted using the pretrained protein language model ESM2 (esm2_t33_650M_UR50D) as recommended in previous study [42]. Peptides were represented at four levels: atom, fragment, residue, and junction (see “Representing peptides as heterogeneous molecular graphs”). This multi-view approach enables the model to capture peptide structures with fine granularity, allowing it to differentiate amino acids based on their similarities, differences, and contributions to peptide properties, such as antimicrobial activity. In this study, AmpHGT enhances message passing across multiple peptide views and demonstrates superior performance, particularly in classifying peptides containing non-canonical amino acids (NCAAs), compared to ESM2. The integration of sequence embeddings with graph-based relational learning (section “Investigation to constructed NCAA dataset”) effectively addresses critical limitations of purely sequence-driven approaches. Notably, our joint training paradigm achieves higher predictive accuracy than ESM2 alone for NCAA-rich antimicrobial peptides. However, four key limitations of current methodologies warrant attention. First, our multi-view representation approach does not incorporate secondary structural features such as helices and folds. Second, the model primarily focuses on linear peptide structures, despite the prevalence of cyclic architectures in natural antimicrobial peptides, including Brevicidine [28], Nisin [49], and Vancomycin [33]. Third, dataset constraints present significant challenges, particularly the limited overlap of NCAAs between positive and negative samples and discrepancies between synthetic peptide representations and physiological structures, which may contribute to underfitting [50]. Fourth, this study entirely omits the predefined physicochemical properties of amino acids, which may impact predictive performance. Notwithstanding these limitations, a comprehensive evaluation confirms that AmpHGT’s combination of sequence embedding and HGT performs comparably to sequence embedding and 3D conformer-based approaches. Notably, AmpHGT offers a lightweight alternative by eliminating the need for 3D structure generation. Furthermore, if molecular structures—including ring formations and amino-amino interactions such as head-to-tail cyclization and disulfide bridges—are accurately annotated with correct SMILES representations, AmpHGT could theoretically be extended to accommodate these features. Most pretrained protein language models (e.g., ESM2, ProtTrans) are restricted to the 20 canonical amino acids due to their training on UniRef datasets. Although the Protein Data Bank contains proteins with over 1000 distinct NCAAs, current 3D conformer prediction models offer limited support for post-translational modifications and NCAAs. While Rosetta is flexible enough to expand its predictions to NCAAs, configuring conformational relationships for each amino acid remains highly complex and computationally demanding. Recent efforts to address these challenges include the introduction of CyclicBoltz1 by Xie et al. within the AlphaFold3 framework, enabling efficient and accurate prediction of cyclic peptide structures and complexes containing non-canonical amino acids (NCAAs) [51]. While the integration of 3D structural features—such as atomic coordinates and intra-subgraph residue distances—has shown promise in canonical peptide prediction, their incorporation into heterogeneous graph transformer (HGT)-based representations for NCAAs remains underexplored. As demonstrated by Chang et al. in their study on antifungal peptide analogs [39], integrating handcrafted features with learned embeddings can enhance predictive performance on NCAA datasets. Supporting this direction, Milchevskiy et al. extended the AAindex to include NCAAs [52], and Oeller et al. developed CamSol-PTM to accurately predict physicochemical properties of peptides containing NCAAs [53]. These advancements provide a foundation for future research to evaluate the synergistic benefits of combining handcrafted features with the HGT framework. Finally, in light of recent progress in generative models for antimicrobial peptide design [12, 54] and heterogeneous graph-based molecular generation [55], future work should move beyond the current classification capabilities of AmpHGT. Specifically, there is significant potential in exploring the de novo generation of heterogeneous graph representations for AMPs with complex secondary structures, followed by experimental validation of their antimicrobial activity. To enable fine-grained peptide representation, we tokenize the peptide structure according to the following rules: Fragmentation in molecular graphs typically involves breaking the graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{G}}_{a}$$\end{document}Ga into a set of subgraphs. For each peptide structure, we annotate the indices of sidechain connection bonds and amide bonds in RDkit’s format. By cleaving these bonds, we isolate the sidechain and backbone structures. However, simply dividing peptides into sidechains and backbones may not provide sufficient information for the model to learn effectively, given the structural similarity of most amino acids (with sidechains typically connected to the α-carbon while other parts remain nearly identical). To expose more features for effective learning, we further segment the sidechains. To possibly preserve biological meaning, this is achieved using the Breaking of Retrosynthetically Interesting Chemical Substructures (BRICS) rule where applicable [59]. If BRICS cannot be further applied, the sidechain is processed using MacFrag [60]. If neither method is applicable, the amino acid remains unfragmented. The backbone is then fragmented at the amide bonds. This process results in the peptide being divided into sidechain fragments, backbone, and backbone fragments. We refer to this strategy as"Fragmentize SideChain Even Sub-Sidechain"(FraSCESS). All molecules are processed in RDKit [61]. To enable fine-grained peptide representation, we tokenize the peptide structure according to the following rules: Fragmentation in molecular graphs typically involves breaking the graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{G}}_{a}$$\end{document}Ga into a set of subgraphs. For each peptide structure, we annotate the indices of sidechain connection bonds and amide bonds in RDkit’s format. By cleaving these bonds, we isolate the sidechain and backbone structures. However, simply dividing peptides into sidechains and backbones may not provide sufficient information for the model to learn effectively, given the structural similarity of most amino acids (with sidechains typically connected to the α-carbon while other parts remain nearly identical). To expose more features for effective learning, we further segment the sidechains. To possibly preserve biological meaning, this is achieved using the Breaking of Retrosynthetically Interesting Chemical Substructures (BRICS) rule where applicable [59]. If BRICS cannot be further applied, the sidechain is processed using MacFrag [60]. If neither method is applicable, the amino acid remains unfragmented. The backbone is then fragmented at the amide bonds. This process results in the peptide being divided into sidechain fragments, backbone, and backbone fragments. We refer to this strategy as"Fragmentize SideChain Even Sub-Sidechain"(FraSCESS). All molecules are processed in RDKit [61]. Fragmentation in molecular graphs typically involves breaking the graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{G}}_{a}$$\end{document}Ga into a set of subgraphs. For each peptide structure, we annotate the indices of sidechain connection bonds and amide bonds in RDkit’s format. By cleaving these bonds, we isolate the sidechain and backbone structures. However, simply dividing peptides into sidechains and backbones may not provide sufficient information for the model to learn effectively, given the structural similarity of most amino acids (with sidechains typically connected to the α-carbon while other parts remain nearly identical). To expose more features for effective learning, we further segment the sidechains. To possibly preserve biological meaning, this is achieved using the Breaking of Retrosynthetically Interesting Chemical Substructures (BRICS) rule where applicable [59]. If BRICS cannot be further applied, the sidechain is processed using MacFrag [60]. If neither method is applicable, the amino acid remains unfragmented. The backbone is then fragmented at the amide bonds. This process results in the peptide being divided into sidechain fragments, backbone, and backbone fragments. We refer to this strategy as"Fragmentize SideChain Even Sub-Sidechain"(FraSCESS). All molecules are processed in RDKit [61]. In this study, datasets containing only canonical amino acids were the XUAMP dataset obtained from TP_LMMSG, the XUAMP dataset is integrated from multiple datasets including APD3, DRAMP, LAMP, CAMP, dbAMP, YADAMP, and UniProt, resulting in 5536 AMPs/nonAMPs in the training set, and 1536 AMPs/nonAMPs in the independent test set. The XUAMP test set is an independent test set initially proposed by Xu et al. through rigorous filtering, served as the primary evaluation benchmark [62]. Additionally, we also collected AMPDiscover dataset proposed by Pinacho-Castellanos et al., the AMPDiscover set is comprised of 19,548 training, 5125 validation, and 15,685 test peptide sequences. The positive sequences of the AMPDiscover were obtained from starPepDB database. The negative sequences are obtained following common procedures. We have also obtained the external test created by G Cordoves-Delgado et al. for model comparison [25, 63]. Detailed information on the CAA datasets can be found in the Additional file 1: Figure S2-S3, Table 3. The NCAA dataset was sourced from DBAASP [64] and UniProt [65], to the best of our knowledge, the DBAASP is the only antimicrobial peptide database that records detailed NCAA information in peptides, including their position and category. Positive non-canonical amino acid-containing peptides (NCAA-AMP) were sourced from DBAASP with search criteria: “Complexity: Monomer,” “Target Group (Multi select): Gram +, Gram −.” For UniProt peptides, only reviewed entries containing at least one of the keywords “Antimicrobial,” “Antibiotic,” or “Amphibian defense peptide” and excluding sequences without residue modifications were included. Negative non-canonical amino acid-containing peptides (NCAA-non-AMP) were sourced using the same search criteria as the NCAA-AMP but excluded entries containing keywords related to “Antimicrobial,” “Antibiotic,” “Amphibian defense peptide,” “Antiviral protein,” and related terms. All sequences were cleaned by removing ambiguous characters and duplicates. Sequences outside the length range of 2 to 60 amino acids were excluded (some peptides in DBAASP have only 1 amino acid but a bulk terminal modification which was not incorporated in this study, e.g., LP1 ~ 6 [66]). The initial NCAA datasets comprised NCAA-AMP: 3780 and NCAA-non-AMP: 3652 sequences. All data were sourced as of July 15, 2024. Following initial data collection, CD-HIT was not implemented here control sequence similarity because of the expanded residue alphabet this dataset has, we refined the NCAA-containing sequences through two-stage filtering. First, 3036 AMPs and 2792 non-AMPs with true NCAA residues were retained after excluding sequences with solely terminal modifications (e.g., C-terminal amidation). Comparative analysis revealed significant NCAA diversity differences: AMPs contained 486 unique NCAAs versus 84 in non-AMPs. To prevent the model from relying on unique amino acids present in different sequence sets to distinguish between antimicrobial and non-antimicrobial peptides, we selected the intersection of the two sequence sets as the training sequences, creating an"overlap"training set—NCAA overlap dataset. Initial intersection contained 29 NCAA types, reduced to 26 after removing D-Thr, D-Ser, and Cys-OMe whose co-occurring residues were group-specific. This overlap set included 340 AMPs and 893 non-AMPs, with the latter downsampled to 340 to balance class distribution, the NCAA overlap dataset was then split by 8:2 to create training and validating set. Subsequent data partitioning allocated sequences with unique NCAAs per group into the NCAA-independent test set (AMPs = 2696; non-AMPs = 2452), while combining the overlap set with standard CAA training data formed the joint training dataset for zero-shot evaluation (Fig. 7A).Fig. 7Processing of NCAA data.
