The discovery of peptide therapeutics has always been a focal point of drug development. Distinguished from small molecule drugs and biological agents, peptide drugs capture researchers’ attention by their unique advantages [1, 2]. In recent years, the progress in synthesis, modification, and analytical technologies have propelled therapeutic peptide research into a new era [2]. Concurrently, the continuous evolution of machine learning models has played a pivotal role in accelerating the peptide drug discovery pipeline. Alongside the widely acknowledged antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) prediction models, the field has witnessed the successive emergence of antiviral peptides (AVPs) and anticancer peptides (ACPs) prediction models [3–5]. The AMPs have attracted widespread attention due to their broad-spectrum antibacterial activity [6, 7]. Specifically, AMPs belongs to a class of short peptides (around 6 to 100 amino acids) involved in the innate immune response among lives [8, 9]. The general therapeutic mechanism of AMPs is to bind the surface of microorganisms through the attraction effect between positive and negative charges. Specifically, most AMPs have net-positive charge, while bacterial membrane has negative charge. The interaction can directly or indirectly destroy the integrity of microbial membrane, thereby affecting the life cycle and proliferation of the microorganism [10, 11]. Moreover, evidence also suggests that the 3D structure of AMPs may also affect the antimicrobial activity of peptides [12, 13]. In addition to natural AMPs, it has been proven promising to discover new synthetic AMPs using generative models and synthetic workflows [14, 15]. Different from small molecules, peptide therapies mainly take advantage of higher specificity and lower toxicity. Hence, peptide drugs with specific targets are also adapted as antifungal, antiviral, antiparasitic or even anticancer clinical strategies [8, 16, 17]. To meet the increasing demand for model training and high-throughput screening of bioactive peptides, various public databases have been established. These databases provide different types of peptide information, allowing researchers to design machine learning models based on different objectives. The APD3 [9], dbAMP [18] and DRAMP [19] databases collect available AMP data and classify those AMPs into different categories based on their targets. Moreover, the experimental data and derived data, such as inhibition activity and molecular properties, are also included in these databases. Meanwhile, the databases such as CAMP [20], ADAM [21] and SATPdb [22] provide abundant peptide structural information. In 2021, several experimentally validated datasets were investigated [3], including APD3 [9], CAMP [20], dbAMP [18], DRAMP [19], LAMP [23] and YADAMP [24]. Based on the AMPs records in these datasets, a fusion dataset named XUAMP is constructed to thoroughly evaluate the performance of AMP identification models [3]. Meanwhile, the CD-HIT tool is utilized for rigorous sequence filtering and sampling to construct the independent test dataset [3]. The following model evaluations demonstrate that there is a potential for further enhancement in current AMP prediction models. In addition to AMPs, the APD3, CAMP, DRAMP and LAMP databases also contain diverse peptide information, including AVPs, ACPs and other theraputic peptides that target at different diseases. Benefit from the peptide information in the aforementioned datasets, multiple feature representation approaches have also been proposed. Besides the commonly used one-hot encoding and multiple sequence alignment (MSA) encoding, incorporating amino acid physicochemical properties can also effectively improve model performance [25, 26]. Meanwhile, the use of positional encoding, amino acid composition and pseudo amino acid composition features was shown to enhance the model performance by incorporating detailed composition information of amino acids [25, 27, 28]. As for the prediction models, the random forest model amPEPpy demonstrates good predictive performance in distinguishing AMPs [29]. Recently, there has been an increasing trend in utilizing deep learning frameworks to distinguish AMPs and non-AMPs [30, 31]. Besides the composition features, the recently published AMPpred-MFA model incorporates dipeptide and k-mer composition as additional input features. Although these methods combine the position, composition and physicochemical properties of peptides, they do not leverage the structural information of the peptide. To tackle this, the sAMPpred-GAT model has been proposed. The model utilizes the protein structure predicted by trRosetta for AMP identification [32, 33]. Except for AMP prediction, some works have transferred the successful experience of predicting AMPs to other types of peptide identification tasks, and have developed prediction models specifically for AVP and ACP [4, 5]. Although sAMPpred-GAT is a promising and innovative attempt, there are still limitations in its practical application. First, the model only utilizes one-hot encoding, positional encoding, hidden Markov models (HMMs) encoding and position-specific scoring matrix (PSSM) encoding as the node features. Two of these features (HMM and PSSM) solely rely on MSA and protein sequence databases [34, 35]. This indicates that the model lacks explicit representations of amino acid physicochemical properties. The two rounds of MSA also implies that the bottleneck in the model’s runtime lies in the feature extraction stage (e.g. around 15 s on single sample). Additionally, its complete deployment requires the download of the entire nonredundant protein sequence database (nrdb, 0.5 TB). Therefore, it is crucial to improve the effectiveness and storage occupancy of the model. In this study, we aim to develop a peptide prediction model, which not only predicts AMPs, but also generalizes to predict other types of peptides (i.e. AVPs and ACPs). Moreover, we aim to reduce the storage occupancy and feature encoding time in graph neural network applications. So far, protein embeddings have been demonstrated to be successful at both sequence and amino acid levels [36, 37]. Meanwhile, the hierarchical multi-scale residual network (HMSRN) has been proved promising in protein–RNA interaction prediction tasks [38]. Therefore, we adapt multiple pre-trained language models to obtain the amino acid embeddings. The HMSRN is then applied to extract informative features among the language model embeddings. Subsequently, we concatenate the extracted features into each node of peptide graph for graph learning. We name our approach as the Therapeutic Peptide Language Model Multi-Scale Graph neural network (TP-LMMSG). The experimental results reveal that our model outperforms the state-of-the-art (SOTA) models in multiple evaluation metrics. Remarkably, these achievements are attained without employing HMM features and rely solely on the lightweight nonredundant protein sequence database 90 (nrdb90) [39]. In addition to AMP prediction, we further generalize our model to the prediction tasks of AVPs and ACPs. Our model also exhibits remarkable performance, surpassing existing models in identifying the other types of peptides with imbalanced datasets. To ensure a fair evaluation and enable an objective comparison with previously proposed models, we primarily utilize the seven AMP datasets provided in previous literatures (i.e. XUAMP, APD3, CAMP, dbAMP, DRAMP, LAMP and YADAMP [3, 9, 18–20, 23, 24, 32]). Specifically, the XUAMP dataset is integrated from multiple datasets including APD3, DRAMP, LAMP, CAMP, dbAMP and YADAMP, resulting in 5536 AMPs/nonAMPs in the training set, and 1536 AMPs/nonAMPs in the independent test set. We choose to benchmark on this dataset as the primary evaluation. Besides the XUAMP dataset, we also conduct evaluations on the other six datasets mentioned above (Table 1, Figure S1). Specifically, each independent test dataset of the six datasets has undergone strict filtering rules, where CD-HIT tool is applied to ensure no sequence shares more than 40% identity in the test set [3, 40, 41]. In this manner, we can rigorously evaluate the performance and make a thorough comparison with previously proposed methods. As for the AVPs and ACPs datasets, the number of positive samples is considerably fewer than that for AMPs [17]. To ensure a fair comparison, we adapt the dataset utilized in previous publications, which are FFMAP AVPs dataset and ACP-ML ACPs dataset, respectively (Table 1) [4, 5]. Peptide sequence statistics of the nine datasets involved in this study The architecture of TP-LMMSG consists of five parts (Figure 1). Firstly, the predictive amino acids connectivity is obtained with trRosetta to construct basic peptide graph (Figure 1A). Secondly, the basic amino acid level features are extracted by conducting one-hot encoding, position encoding, PSI-BLAST and HHBlits on peptide sequences. We consider the lightweight graph construction method, where running HHBlits is optional (the portions with dash lines in Figure 1A). These features are assigned as the initial graph node features (Figure 1B). For the third step, the SeqVec and ProtTrans pre-trained protein language models are used to obtain the amino acids level embeddings, including the hidden representation of hydrophilia, electrical properties and polarity [36, 37]. Specifically, the pretrained SeqVec model and PortTrans model are adapted to extract the biophysical embeddings of each amino acid in the peptide sequence [36, 37]. For the output of each pre-trained model, an HMSRN is utilized to extract the physicochemical information at different scales (Figure 1C). In the last two steps, the latent representation is integrated for each amino acid in the peptide graph (Figure 1D). A graph attention network (GAT) followed by fully connected layers is proposed to predict whether the input peptides are positive samples or negative samples (Figure 1E). Therefore, we utilized multiple pre-trained protein language models to obtain informative amino acid feature embeddings (Figure 1C) [36, 37]. First, the pretrained SeqVec model (‘sv’ in the following description) is adapted [36]. By training EMLo on the UniRef50 dataset, SeqVec can generate continuous embeddings at amino acid level. In this step, the peptide sequences are fed into the pre-trained SeqVec model to obtain amino acid-level embeddings for each sequence. As a result, the model outputs a vector with 1024 dimensions for each amino acid. Due to the varying lengths of sequences, we extract embeddings according to the maximum sequence length (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $L_{max}$\end{document}). After obtaining the amino acid level embeddings, we adapt the HMSRN architecture to extract features at the amino acid level (Figure 1C). The aim of utilizing the HMSRN is to capture node features comprehensively while alleviating the gradient vanishing problem [44]. In this work, the HMSRN is optimized to receive a varying number of language model embeddings and conduct feature concatenation at the amino acid level. In this study, we develop a new model that resolves the time-consuming pre-processing challenge encountered by previous graph neural network models. Meanwhile, our model outperforms the current SOTA AMP prediction models across all datasets. Additionally, the TP-LMMSG also demonstrates robustness on imbalanced datasets, surpassing the current SOTA AVP and ACP prediction models. Previous studies have primarily relied on MSA and various amino acid properties as the feature representation [3, 36]. The residue features, k-mer features and overall characteristics of peptide sequences can be obtained from sequences, thus facilitating the peptide property prediction. On the other hand, graph neural networks can leverage structural information of peptide molecules, but face challenges in specific amino acid property feature representations. Moreover, due to the limited number of homologs for partial peptides, running MSA may not yield any informative sequence information. In practice, neither nrdb90 nor entire nrdb guarantees completed MSA results for all sequences. This may introduce biases in feature extraction. With the node-level language model embeddings, our model can incorporate a large number of amino acid property representations, making it possible to integrate amino acid property features into a graph neural network. In addition, the optimization in pre-processing stage enables the practical application of graph neural networks for peptide property prediction tasks. Compared with the previous graph learning model, our approach achieves a pre-processing time reduction of over 7 folds. Therefore, even when applied to larger databases with fine-tuning, our approach yields highly accurate results within reasonable time-frames. Moreover, our method could save a large amount of local storage space, enabling efficient local fine-tuning and adaptation to hardware resources at the small-scale laboratory level. The good performance and potential exhibited by the TP-LMMSG model have sparked deeper thinking for peptide property prediction. It is acknowledged that the distribution of therapeutic peptides with clinical potential is extremely imbalanced compared with negative samples in the real world. Our experimental results also explore the performance boundary. This series of experiments with imbalanced datasets demonstrates the robustness of TP-LMMSG. It also suggests that the continuous supplements of novel positive samples should be enabled and included in benchmark datasets to reflect the practical application context. Taking it a step further, with the advancement of biosynthetic technology, the introduction of non-natural amino acids is emerging in peptide drug design [46]. In the future, peptide graphs that involve non-natural amino acids can be constructed through site-directed mutagenesis and the incorporation of distinct node and edge features. By utilizing Density Functional Theory derived features or traditional small molecule representations, the limitations of protein language models could be expanded in peptide prediction tasks [47]. These avenues hold promise for further investigation. Moreover, mounting evidence suggests that covalently attached small molecules to peptides demonstrate potential drug-like properties, posing challenges in molecule-peptide graph constructions [48, 49]. Fortunately, with the development of protein prediction models, AlphaFold3 has acquired the capability to predict drug–protein interaction poses, enhancing the expansion possibilities of our workflow [50]. While AlphaFold3 is not open-sourced during the writing of this article, exploring cutting-edge protein structure prediction models provides a valuable focus for peptide property annotations in the future. A generalizable peptide prediction model, TP-LMMSG, is proposed. It outperforms current state-of-the-art models in antimicrobial peptides, antiviral peptides, and anticancer peptides predictions. TP-LMMSG effectively tackles the challenge faced by previous graph neural network-based methods, which is the inability to extract and represent amino acid level properties. The model utilizes optimized amino acid level feature extraction strategy for rapid deployment of graph neural networks in peptide prediction. TP-LMMGS can flexibly incorporate novel language model embeddings. The architecture could providing continuous contributions to the discovery process of potential peptide therapeutics.
