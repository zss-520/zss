With the emergence and spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, the effectiveness of traditional antibiotics has gradually declined, posing a major global public health challenge. The World Health Organization (WHO) lists drug-resistant infections among the top global public health threats [1]. Antibacterial peptides (ABPs) are small peptides with natural antibacterial activity [2]. They kill bacteria mainly by disrupting the cell membrane, a mechanism that reduces the likelihood of resistance [3]. ABPs exhibit high efficacy [4] and relatively low toxicity [5], making them attractive for research [6]. In recent years, progress in ABP research has been substantial. A large number of peptides with antibacterial activity have been identified from diverse organisms, and their structures and functions have been studied in depth [7]. Although traditional wet-lab methods remain important for evaluating ABP activity, they are costly and time-consuming [8]. Artificial intelligence techniques can predict ABP activity without relying on specific experimental conditions, providing an efficient pre-screening step before wet-lab evaluation. Research on predicting ABPs using artificial intelligence mainly covers two tasks: ABP classification and antibacterial activity prediction. Antibacterial activity is typically quantified by the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). A lower MIC indicates stronger inhibitory activity against pathogenic microorganisms. Accurate MIC prediction can accelerate the discovery and development of novel ABPs. With advances in computational biology and machine learning, researchers have increasingly applied these techniques to predict ABP activity [9]. Xiao et al. [10] proposed an MIC predictor based on five physicochemical properties of AMPs and pseudo-amino acid composition features, using a Gaussian kernel regression model to predict the inhibitory effect of AMPs on Escherichia coli. In addition, they observed a poor predictive correlation, which they attributed to the wide range of experimental MIC values. Li et al. [11] compared MIC predictions for Streptococcus pneumoniae isolates using traditional methods and machine learning, validating their results against broth microdilution data. They found that penicillin-binding protein sequences could effectively predict \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta $\end{document}-lactam antibiotic MIC values and exhibit high consistency. Sharma et al. [12] developed ESKAPEE-MICpred, a dual-input model integrating transfer learning and ensemble learning to predict ABP activity against five pathogens. The model takes pathogen genomic data as one input and incorporates outputs from a pre-trained model, adjusting feature-extraction weights to improve predictions. Yan et al. [13] used deep learning, specifically multi-branch convolutional neural networks and attention mechanisms, to predict the MIC against E. coli, achieving better performance than traditional machine learning approaches. Yao et al. [14] proposed AMPActiPred, a three-stage ABP predictor: first, determine whether a peptide is an ABP; second, predict its specificity toward 10 bacterial species; third, estimate its activity level. Cai et al. [15] introduced a fine-tuned BERT-based Transformer model that extracts sequence embedding features and uses transfer learning to predict ABP activity on E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus datasets. Although these methods have shown progress in predicting ABP MIC values, their overall predictive accuracy remains insufficient for reliable practical use. Most methods rely on single-modality or single-scale feature representations, and fail to integrate multi-level information, such as the ABP sequence and its corresponding 3D structure, making it difficult to capture the complex relationships among sequence, structure, function, and antibacterial mechanisms. As a result, important interactions between structural features and function may be overlooked. Moreover, many studies train and evaluate models on datasets that are small in size or limited in species diversity. This lack of diverse data hampers cross-species generalization and reduces model robustness in real-world applications, thereby constraining its potential for clinical translation. To address these challenges, we propose the geometric graph network (GGN)-ABPMIC model, integrating multi-modal peptide sequence features and multi-scale structural features. The features are processed through a GGN, and the resulting outputs are concatenated with peptide descriptors and fed into a regression head to predict MIC values. In addition, we introduce a novel multi-stage dynamic-weight hybrid loss function (MDH-loss), which combines mean squared error (MSE), the Huber loss [16], and a contrastive learning loss [17] to enhance training stability. We evaluate the model on datasets from 10 bacterial species, and GGN-ABPMIC achieves strong predictive performance across all datasets, demonstrating notable cross-species generalization. In this study, we compiled the ABP sequences targeting 10 bacterial species from AMPActiPred [14]. In addition to the sequences, we collected the corresponding MIC values and the 553-dimensional peptide feature descriptors. The data were obtained from the GRAMPA database [18], which contains ABP sequences of 10–30 amino acids and integrates several widely used ABP resources, including APD [8], DADP [19], DBaASP [20], DRAMP [21], and YADAMP [22]. To comprehensively characterize ABP activity, we designed a computational framework that integrates sequence-derived and structure-derived information, as illustrated in Fig. 1A. The pipeline starts with ABP sequences, from which ESMFold [27] predicts their 3D structure. We then extract multi-scale features from both the sequences and the predicted structures in parallel and integrate them with ProtTrans [28] embeddings and the definition of secondary structure of proteins (DSSP) [29], which provides residue-level secondary structure annotations and structure-derived descriptors. All features are then fed into the GGN framework for processing, and the resulting representation is concatenated with conventional peptide descriptors. The combined features are fed into a regression module to predict the activity value. During training, we apply MDH-loss with weights dynamically updated across epochs to balance the optimization of the regression head and the GGN encoder. Figure 1B provides a detailed illustration of the workflow of the GGN. Node features and edge features are fed in parallel into different geometric vector perceptrons (GVPs), followed by message aggregation through the message-passing layers, thereby fully capturing domain-specific information. The graph representations obtained after the message-passing layers are subsequently processed by a specialized GVP that normalizes all features into scalar form, and the output features are finally obtained through a pooling layer. Within a GVP layer, scalar and vector features are updated through tightly coupled operations. As shown in Fig. 1C, the vector features undergo a linear projection, and the L2 norms of these projected vectors are computed to capture rotation-invariant geometric information. This norm is concatenated with the input scalar features and passed through nonlinear fully connected layers to produce updated scalar features. In parallel, the projected vector features are processed by a separate linear transformation to yield candidate vector features, which are then gated and scaled using the scalar outputs to produce updated vector features. This resulting tuples preserve geometric sensitivity while remaining rotation-invariant. In the feature extraction stage, the model systematically integrates multi-scale geometric features, DSSP structural features, and ProtTrans sequence embeddings to comprehensively represent the sequence and structural information of ABPs. The multi-scale geometric features cover three levels: features between neighboring residues, features between connected residues, and intra-residue features. As shown in Fig. 2A, features between neighboring residues include \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $C_\alpha -C_\alpha $\end{document} vectors, radial basis function distance embeddings, and positional embeddings. Features between connected residues are represented by backbone direction vectors, while intra-residue features consist of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $C_\alpha -C_\beta $\end{document} vectors on side chains and dihedral angle embeddings. This design constructs a hierarchical geometric representation from local to global scales. DSSP features further provide scalar parameters such as the secondary structure type and solvent-accessible surface area for each residue. ProtTrans generates 1024-dimensional embeddings that encode deep evolutionary and semantic information. The graph neural network (GGN) framework combines GNNs and GVPs. Within this framework, the GGN first performs fine-grained feature modeling. Sequence features are derived using ProtTrans, while structural features are obtained by secondary structure parameters computed from DSSP. We further incorporate multi-scale geometric features, including inter-residue \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $C_\alpha - C_\alpha $\end{document} vectors, intra-residue \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $C_\alpha - C_\beta $\end{document} vectors, radial basis function-encoded distances, positional encodings, and backbone dihedral angles. These features are partitioned into scalar and vector components and encoded as node and edge attributes in the graph, then processed jointly through GVPs. Subsequently, all node features and edge features will undergo information aggregation via the message-passing layer, yielding the processed output features. In what follows, the working principles of GNN, GVP, and the message-passing layer will be elaborated respectively. GNNs [30] have achieved significant progress in protein structure learning, especially in capturing residue–residue interactions and topological relationships. However, conventional GNNs face fundamental limitations in handling 3D geometric features. Typically, a protein is represented as a proximity graph, and geometric information (such as atomic coordinates and spatial orientation) is encoded into rotation-invariant scalar features, often by constructing local coordinate systems to compute relative distances or angles. While this simplification preserves rotational invariance, it discards critical geometric information: Vector features carrying spatial relationships are reduced to scalars, preventing the model from perceiving directionality and shape variations in 3D space. In addition, reliance on local coordinate frames increases computational complexity and hampers learning of global geometric patterns, limiting the expressiveness for tasks such as structure prediction and design. To address these challenges, we introduce GGN [31]. Its primary advantage is the use of GVPs to directly process vector features while jointly updating scalar and vector channels. This design preserves geometric sensitivity and rotational invariance simultaneously, effectively overcoming the limitations of traditional GNNs in handling 3D geometric information. The workflow of GGN is detailed in the following sections. GVP [32] is an innovative neural network module with unique advantages for handling multi-modal geometric features in biomolecular structures. By simultaneously processing scalar and vector features, it overcomes the limitations of conventional graph neural networks in geometric reasoning. The core strength of GVP lies in its ability to directly operate on Euclidean vectors while maintaining equivariance or invariance under rotations and reflections, enabling efficient modeling of protein structures. This module can serve as a replacement for standard fully connected layers, and when integrated into GNNs, it enhances the network’s sensitivity to geometric relationships. In ABP activity prediction, GVP effectively captures the synergistic information from both sequence and structural features. The message passing layer is the core component of GGN, designed to update node features through iterative information exchange, thereby integrating relational reasoning with geometric reasoning. The workflow of this layer involves three key steps: first, generating message vectors to encode local structural information; second, aggregating messages and updating node states; and third, applying a pointwise feedforward layer for deeper feature transformation. This process ensures equivariance of geometric features and stability of scalar features, providing a foundation for protein structure modeling. In the message generation step, message vectors are formed by combining source node features and edge features, followed by processing through a GVP. The + denotes a residual connection, adding the aggregated messages to the original node features, allowing the model to learn incremental changes and alleviating the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks. Through sequential aggregation, node states are updated robustly while preserving geometric consistency. Following message passing, a pointwise feedforward layer is applied independently to each node for deeper feature transformation, further enhancing representational capacity. Scalar and vector features are then processed through an additional GVP, and the output features are pooled using a hybrid pooling layer composed of global average pooling [33] and attention pooling [34]. Average pooling ensures model robustness and prevents prediction instability, while attention pooling enhances discriminative power by amplifying important signals and suppressing noise, thereby improving overall model performance in parallel. The features processed by the GGN comprehensively represent the sequence and structural information of ABPs; however, they lack numerical features such as amino acid composition and physicochemical properties, which are often related to the activity of antimicrobial peptides. Peptide feature descriptors are a set of numerical features directly extracted from ABP sequences, capturing multidimensional information, including amino acid composition, order, and physicochemical properties. When combined with structural features processed by the GGN and other sequence features, peptide feature descriptors provide complementary information, enabling a comprehensive representation of antimicrobial peptides. In the regression task of predicting the MIC values of antimicrobial peptides, the complex data distribution and limited sample size make a single loss function insufficient to ensure both robustness and generalization. To comprehensively characterize ABP activity, we designed a computational framework that integrates sequence-derived and structure-derived information, as illustrated in Fig. 1A. The pipeline starts with ABP sequences, from which ESMFold [27] predicts their 3D structure. We then extract multi-scale features from both the sequences and the predicted structures in parallel and integrate them with ProtTrans [28] embeddings and the definition of secondary structure of proteins (DSSP) [29], which provides residue-level secondary structure annotations and structure-derived descriptors. All features are then fed into the GGN framework for processing, and the resulting representation is concatenated with conventional peptide descriptors. The combined features are fed into a regression module to predict the activity value. During training, we apply MDH-loss with weights dynamically updated across epochs to balance the optimization of the regression head and the GGN encoder. Figure 1B provides a detailed illustration of the workflow of the GGN. Node features and edge features are fed in parallel into different geometric vector perceptrons (GVPs), followed by message aggregation through the message-passing layers, thereby fully capturing domain-specific information. The graph representations obtained after the message-passing layers are subsequently processed by a specialized GVP that normalizes all features into scalar form, and the output features are finally obtained through a pooling layer. Within a GVP layer, scalar and vector features are updated through tightly coupled operations. As shown in Fig. 1C, the vector features undergo a linear projection, and the L2 norms of these projected vectors are computed to capture rotation-invariant geometric information. This norm is concatenated with the input scalar features and passed through nonlinear fully connected layers to produce updated scalar features. In parallel, the projected vector features are processed by a separate linear transformation to yield candidate vector features, which are then gated and scaled using the scalar outputs to produce updated vector features. This resulting tuples preserve geometric sensitivity while remaining rotation-invariant. The graph neural network (GGN) framework combines GNNs and GVPs. Within this framework, the GGN first performs fine-grained feature modeling. Sequence features are derived using ProtTrans, while structural features are obtained by secondary structure parameters computed from DSSP. We further incorporate multi-scale geometric features, including inter-residue \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $C_\alpha - C_\alpha $\end{document} vectors, intra-residue \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $C_\alpha - C_\beta $\end{document} vectors, radial basis function-encoded distances, positional encodings, and backbone dihedral angles. GNNs [30] have achieved significant progress in protein structure learning, especially in capturing residue–residue interactions and topological relationships. However, conventional GNNs face fundamental limitations in handling 3D geometric features. Typically, a protein is represented as a proximity graph, and geometric information (such as atomic coordinates and spatial orientation) is encoded into rotation-invariant scalar features, often by constructing local coordinate systems to compute relative distances or angles. While this simplification preserves rotational invariance, it discards critical geometric information: Vector features carrying spatial relationships are reduced to scalars, preventing the model from perceiving directionality and shape variations in 3D space. In addition, reliance on local coordinate frames increases computational complexity and hampers learning of global geometric patterns, limiting the expressiveness for tasks such as structure prediction and design. To address these challenges, we introduce GGN [31]. Its primary advantage is the use of GVPs to directly process vector features while jointly updating scalar and vector channels. This design preserves geometric sensitivity and rotational invariance simultaneously, effectively overcoming the limitations of traditional GNNs in handling 3D geometric information. GVP [32] is an innovative neural network module with unique advantages for handling multi-modal geometric features in biomolecular structures. The core strength of GVP lies in its ability to directly operate on Euclidean vectors while maintaining equivariance or invariance under rotations and reflections, enabling efficient modeling of protein structures. This module can serve as a replacement for standard fully connected layers, and when integrated into GNNs, it enhances the network’s sensitivity to geometric relationships. The features processed by the GGN comprehensively represent the sequence and structural information of ABPs; however, they lack numerical features such as amino acid composition and physicochemical properties, which are often related to the activity of antimicrobial peptides. Peptide feature descriptors are a set of numerical features directly extracted from ABP sequences, capturing multidimensional information, including amino acid composition, order, and physicochemical properties. When combined with structural features processed by the GGN and other sequence features, peptide feature descriptors provide complementary information, enabling a comprehensive representation of antimicrobial peptides. Based on this principle, the model includes five representative types of descriptors, each digitally encoding the peptide sequence from different perspectives. In the regression task of predicting the MIC values of antimicrobial peptides, the complex data distribution and limited sample size make a single loss function insufficient to ensure both robustness and generalization. To address this, we design a multi-stage mixed loss function that dynamically adjusts the weights of different loss components during training. The loss function consists of three main parts: MSE, Huber loss, and a contrastive loss (ContrastLoss). During training, the weights of these components change according to the ratio of the current epoch to the total number of epochs. This mainly arises from the model’s complex architecture, which contains millions of parameters, while the available experimental data are limited—each dataset contains only a few hundred to a few thousand peptide sequences. To address this problem, we adopt a random dropout strategy. By randomly deactivating a subset of neurons during training, we reduce co-adaptation among neurons and enhance model robustness. Under this setting, the choice of the dropout rate becomes particularly important. Additionally, the learning rate controls the step size in gradient descent, thereby affecting optimization speed and stability. We tune it to balance training efficiency and convergence accuracy due to the anisotropic and uneven curvature of the loss landscape. Temperature controls the separation between positive and negative sample pairs in the feature space, affecting feature distribution sharpness. A proper temperature enhances the discriminability and stability of the feature space. The margin defines the minimum separation between positive and negative sample pairs in metric learning, which directly influencing decision boundaries. A suitable margin helps the model learn features with large inter-class separation and small intra-class variation. A systematic evaluation across datasets from 10 bacterial species showed that GGN-ABPMIC achieves excellent predictive performance and strong generalization ability in ABP activity prediction. The GGN-ABPMIC model integrates multi-scale features from different ABP modalities. These features are processed through a GGN and then combined with multiple peptide feature descriptors for MIC prediction. We also designed an MDH-loss that incorporates MSE, the Huber loss, and a contrastive learning loss to enhance the model’s robustness in regression tasks. Experimental results demonstrate that GGN-ABPMIC outperforms the existing method AMPActiPred on the vast majority of strain categories. In addition, we validated the effectiveness of each model component through hyperparameter tuning and ablation studies. For hyperparameter tuning, we designed a systematic grid-search strategy to identify the optimal hyperparameter settings for each dataset. In the ablation experiments, we assessed the contributions of ProTrans, peptide feature descriptors, and multi-scale geometric features. The results show that these components, along with the hybrid loss function, all contribute significantly to improving prediction performance. In summary, GGN-ABPMIC demonstrates clear advantages in predicting the MIC of ABPs, providing an efficient and accurate computational approach for ABP drug development. In future work, we plan to further expand the dataset and optimize the model architecture to improve prediction performance and broaden the model’s application scope. Geometric graph network-antibacterial peptides minimum inhibitory concentration (GGN-ABPMIC) is introduced for the prediction of minimum inhibitory concentration of antimicrobial peptides based on multi-scale geometric graph networks and dynamic loss functions. GGN-ABPMIC integrates information from geometric graph at atomic- and residue-scales. It uses scalar features and vector features. By incorporating diverse peptide feature descriptors, it overcomes the limitations of traditional methods that rely on a single sequence feature. To address complex data distributions and overfitting, we design a multi-stage, dynamically weighted hybrid loss function. It combines mean squared error loss, Huber loss, and contrastive loss. The component weights are updated throughout training epochs. Case analyses show that GGN-ABPMIC exhibits high predictive accuracy for unseen sequences. Compared with existing methods, its MIC predictions are closer to the true MIC values.
