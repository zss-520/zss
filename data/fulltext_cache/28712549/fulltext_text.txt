Organophosphate compounds are a class of organic compounds containing a characteristically electrophilic phosphonyl core and are commonly found as the reactive components of agricultural pesticides and herbicides. A particularly sinister class of organophosphates is the organophosphate nerve agents (OPNAs) related to chemical warfare. These OPNAs are grouped into different series based on the ligand connectivity to the phosphonyl core. The G-series agents, for example, are shown in Figure and include tabun (GA), sarin (GB), soman (GD), and cyclosarin (GF). Though these agents are banned by the Geneva Convention, they have been used for individual attacks , and mass destruction , on several occasions. OPNAs achieve their lethality through the inhibition of acetylcholine esterase (AChE) enzymes by forming a nearly irreversible bond to the catalytic triad’s serine residue in the active site. This inhibits the breakdown of acetylcholine, which then builds up in the nicotinic and muscarinic receptors and ultimately causes death via respiratory failure. The inhibition of AChE by OPNAs can be reversed via an antidote such as an oxime, but only if administered before the aging of the phosphorylated enzyme occurs. Due to the immediate dangers associated with OPNAs in high-stress situations, rapid and user-friendly detection methods are crucial for defending against OPNA attacks and treating those who have been exposed. Instruments offer the most sensitive method for detection, − but the most convenient, portable, and user-friendly method of identification is chromogenic detection. − Importantly, in practical field scenarios, organophosphate nerve agents are disseminated primarily as aerosolized droplets rather than dilute vapor. As a result, the local surface concentration that reaches a test strip is typically far above the detection limits of most chromogenic systems. Sensitivity therefore becomes less limiting than selectivity, as environmental interferents such as solvents, lubricants, fuels, and other electrophiles can produce misleading color responses. The ideal chromogenic probe would be able to give specific, unique responses to the various families of OPNAs while avoiding false positives from interferents. Importantly, even if the interferents were to elicit visual color changes, they would be significantly different from those of the target compounds. A common binary response sensor cannot meet these crucial requirements; instead, a detector capable of producing a variety of distinct chromogenic responses from different analytes and interferents is required. G-series OPNAs and their simulants. Dirhodium­(II,II) paddlewheel complexes possess chromogenic qualities that can shift based on the nature of their axial ligands. This is because axial ligands donate into the dz2 orbital, perturbing the energy gap between the HOMO π* and the LUMO σ* molecular orbitals. , This electronic transition is the primary source of visible light absorption in the complex. This feature has been leveraged for the detection of CO and NO, and we recently discovered that dirhodium­(II,II) paddlewheel complexes are also capable sensors for diethyl chlorophosphate (DCP), a G-series OPNA simulant (Figure ). Our system consisted of dirhodium­(II,II) paddlewheel complexes with nitrogenous Lewis bases coordinated to the Rh axial sites. In the presence of DCP, the axial ligands were phosphorylated by the nerve agent simulant, displacing the chloride leaving group of DCP, which in turn would bind to the now unoccupied axial site, causing a color change. The sensitivity of detection varied by axial ligand, and LODs were as low as 0.113 μM. While the complexes resisted most false positives, acetyl chloride triggered a color response similar to that of DCP. To reduce false positives from other electrophilic analytes, we designed dirhodium­(II,II) paddlewheel complexes with thioether axial ligands that are tethered to their bridging ligands. We hypothesized that the mode of sensing for the complexes consists of axial ligand dissociation, followed by a nucleophilic attack of the tethered sulfur atom on the phosphorus center of DCP, causing the release of a chloride ion (Figure ). Like our previous system, we expected a color change as a result of the released chloride binding to the Rh axial site. The hemilability of the thioether ligands ,− also ensured that reactivity would not be completely inhibited. We have used these complexes in catalytic applications, in which the tethered axial ligands enabled better selectivity in carbene transfer reactions, thereby demonstrating that tethered thioether ligands in dirhodium paddlewheel complexes do not completely inhibit activity or binding at axial sites and could aid selective binding in other applications. − Herein, we report their use as potential OPNA sensors with enhanced selectivity. The thiocarbamoyl benzoate (TCB) scaffold 2 has been used as a dirhodium­(II,II) paddlewheel ligand by our group in past reports, with thioether moieties serving as the tethered axial coordinating ligands. , The donor capability of the sulfur atom at the axial site can be modulated by substituent modification. This will ultimately establish a structure–activity relationship between the electronic nature of the complexes and their ability to detect nerve agents and their simulants. Except for ligand 2b (R = methyl), TCB ligands were synthesized as previously reported. , Our previous ligand exchange methods typically consisted of refluxing the ligand, base, and dirhodium tetraacetate in an appropriate noncoordinating solvent. Using dirhodium tetraacetate as the precursor resulted in mixtures of singly (mono) and doubly exchanged (bis) products. In our case, the bis complexes were desired since they provided identical Rh environments for detection. To maximize yields of the bis complexes, Rh2(OAc)2(tfa)2 1 was used as the precursor instead of Rh2(OAc)4. The more facile exchange of the tfa ligands − allowed for more efficient production of the bis products, while limiting the formation of the mono species. The series of dirhodium­(II,II) paddlewheel complexes (complexes 3a-3d, Scheme ) with tethered axial coordinating thioether ligands was produced in 53–75% yield with this method and differed by thioether substituents of varying electron-donating and electron-withdrawing ability. To assess the influence of the tether in the detection of nerve agents, visual detection experiments were conducted by using the simulant DCP. The ideal candidate would be one with a unique color change from the original solution that can be easily distinguished. In our visual detection experiments, up to 75 mM (50 equiv) of DCP was added to a 1.5 mM solution of the complex in DCE, and detection was monitored at 15 mM increments (Figure ). Visual response of dirhodium­(II,II) complexes 3a–3d in the presence of varying concentrations of DCP. Rh2(OAc)2(PhTCB)2 3a started as a purple-colored solution in DCE, and after the addition of 45 mM (30 equiv) of DCP, a slight color change was observed. A discernible color change occurred after the addition of 75 mM DCP, after which the solution turned into a light brown color. The relatively electron-poor Rh2(OAc)2(p-NO2–ArTCB)2 3c was gray-green in solution, but it showed no discernible color change after 75 mM of DCP was added. The electron-rich 4-methoxy aryl thioether in complex Rh2(OAc)2(p-OMe-ArTCB)2 3d was purple in DCE solution. In contrast to 3c, we observed a color change with the solution of 3d after approximately 15 mM (10 equiv) of DCP. The solution transitioned to a yellowish color, and the color change continued with an increased concentration of DCP. Rh2(OAc)2(MeTCB)2 3b, bearing a methyl substituent on the thioether instead of an aryl group, started as a pinkish/purple color in DCE solution. After about 30 mM (20 equiv) of DCP, the solution became a brighter pink and yellow with a pink hue after 45 mM (30 equiv) of DCP. At 75 mM DCP, the solution settled as a bright yellow solution. The similar color between 3b and 3d suggests similar final products. The relatively electron-rich thioethers in both 3b and 3d, compared to the other complexes, also suggest that stronger ligand donation and greater nucleophilicity lead to more sensitive detection of DCP. Complexes 3b and 3d were further tested against common interferents encountered in laboratory environments and the field (Figure ). The complexes were exposed to organic solvents (toluene, DMSO, acetone), nonpolar compounds (gun cleaner, gasoline), the GA simulant diethyl cyanophosphate (DCNP), and chloride sources (TBACl, acetyl chloride), as shown in Figure a and b. Interferent testing for the organic solvents and nonpolar compounds was conducted by adding 5.4 μL of interferent (the amount equating to 50 equiv of DCP) to 1.5 mM of a solution of the complex. Neither the organic solvents nor the nonpolar compounds caused any significant color changes when added to DCE solutions of 3b and 3d. There was a slight change from purple to pinkish-purple when DMSO was added to complex 3d, but it did not trigger the same color response as DCP. Acetone and DMSO are known coordinating solvents, but they were inhibited by the tethered complexes, thereby validating the protection scheme of the complexes. DCNP did not trigger a significant response in either complex, demonstrating the potential of this system for distinguishing between types of G-series nerve agents. Acetyl chloride and tetrabutylammonium chloride (TBACl) were tested as interferents to verify that our designed complexes were specific to detecting DCP rather than chloride ions (Figure b). Testing acetyl chloride as an interferent was of particular interest since acetyl chloride has a similar reactivity profile as DCP and resulted in false- positive tests for our “untethered” dirhodium paddlewheel probes. While complexes 3b and 3d were both resistant to TBACl, acetyl chloride caused a color change in a solution of 3d that was similar to DCP detection. Gratifyingly, complex 3b was more resistant to acetyl chloride interference. Though there was a slight color change from the initial solution, it was distinct from the color change associated with DCP detection for that complex. Changes to axial ligands on dirhodium­(II,II) paddlewheel complexes have a significant impact on their chromogenic qualities. They can be tracked by shifts in the low-energy visible region of their UV–vis spectrum between 500 and 650 nm. This area is indicative of perturbations in the HOMO–LUMO energy gap of the Rh–Rh bond, and bathochromic or hypsochromic shifts are common when axial ligands are exchanged. Exposure of 3b and 3d to DCP was monitored by UV–vis, where we expected to observe shifts in the 500–650 nm region of the spectrum. What we observed instead were slight hypochromic shifts (a decrease in molar absorptivity) at the low-energy bands (around 560 nm), paired with prominent hyperchromic shifts at high-energy wavelengths in the visible region (around 450 nm) for both complexes (Figure a and b), with the hypochromic and hyperchromic shifts more pronounced for 3b. The hyperchromic shifts around 450 nm are likely responsible for the color change and indicate a change in the π*­(Rh2) to σ*­(Rh–Oeq) transition, − likely caused by its interaction with the titrant DCP. For 3b, as the DCP concentration increased from 0 to 10 mM, we noticed a subtle but more distinct shoulder appearing around 446 nm. This change in the electronic structure is more pronounced than what is seen at 450 nm in 3d. The band at 565 nm is related to a π*­(Rh2 4+) to σ*­(Rh2 4+) transition, which can be influenced by coordination at the axial site. The hypochromic shifts for 3b and 3d are small, indicating only slight disturbances to this transition, but they are more significant for 3d. Overall, the spectra of 3b and 3d after exposure to DCP were quite similar, with slight variations. Average UV–vis LODs arising from these shifts were 0.014 mM for 3b and 0.022 mM for 3d, thus showing that the complexes have similar reactivity. UV–vis response and LOD in the presence of DCP for (a) Rh2(OAc)2(MeTCB)2 3b and (b) Rh2(OAc)2(p-OMe-ArTCB)2 3d. We also examined UV–vis spectra of the interferents and investigated the effects of the interferents on the DCP response of complexes 3b and 3d (Figures S18 and S19). A comparison of the UV–vis trace of the complexes with and without interferents at the low-energy bands was consistent with the visual response results. Toluene, acetone, gasoline, and gun cleaner did not alter the UV–vis trace of either complex. The spectrum for complex 3b also remained unchanged at 547 nm with DMSO, DCNP, and acetyl chloride. Complex 3d, however, showed a decrease in absorbance at 565 nm when exposed to acetyl chloride, along with an increase in absorbance around 450 nm. This would indicate a false-positive detection, as observed in the visual response of 3d with acetyl chloride. Exposure of 3d to DMSO caused a small decrease in absorbance and a slight hypsochromic shift, indicative of the coordination of DMSO to the axial sites of 3d. We also conducted competitive detection experiments by adding DCP (20 equiv) to the solutions of 3b and 3d with the interferents and monitored changes in absorbance at the low-energy absorption bands of each complex. As shown in Figure a and b, toluene, gasoline, acetone, and gun cleaner did not interfere with the detection of DCP for either complex. DMSO inhibited DCP detection for both complexes, even though it did not elicit a visual color change for 3b. It is likely that DMSO still interferes with the axial sites of 3b, but its interaction is too weak to shift absorption. Impressively, 3b is still able to detect DCP in the presence of acetyl chloride, showing that it is not a competitor, although it has similar electrophilic reactivity. For 3d with acetyl chloride, the addition of DCP further reduced the absorbance at 565 nm compared to those of 3d and DCP, perhaps because of an additive effect of the two analytes. The superior resistance of 3b to interferents is attributed to the enhanced donor ability of its alkyl thioether ligand, leading to a stronger coordinative bond with the rhodium center. This results in reduced hemilability of the tethered ligand, which in turn minimizes unwanted interactions or displacement by interfering agents while retaining reactivity to DCP. In contrast, the aryl thioether in 3d experiences delocalization of the sulfur lone pair into the aromatic ring, rendering it a weaker donor and more susceptible to disruption by reactive electrophilic species such as acetyl chloride. Reduced absorbance intensity at 547 nm for 3b and 565 nm for 3d indicates a positive response. We hypothesized that the mode of action consists of a nucleophilic attack by the tethered sulfur atom on the phosphate, causing the release of the chloride ion. The chloride can then coordinate to one of the two axial sites of the dirhodium paddlewheel complex. In addition, the results suggest that donating substituents on the sulfur atom increase the likelihood of detection through phosphonyl attack. To investigate this mechanism through structural and vibrational changes, the complexes were exposed to DCP and analyzed by NMR, Raman, ATR-FTIR spectroscopy, and mass spectrometry. Standard solutions of Rh2(OAc)2(p-OMe-ArTCB)2 3d and Rh2(OAc)2(MeTCB)2 3b, after DCP exposure, were analyzed by 31P­{1H} NMR. Upon interaction with complexes 3b and 3d, the original DCP chemical shift at 4.26 ppm shifted to 4.18 ppm with 3b and 4.32 ppm with 3d (Figures S7–S11). The shifts could result from replacing the chloride in DCP with the less electron-rich sulfur of the tethered ligand at the phosphorus center. The degree of change at the phosphorus center appears to depend on the donor ability of the sulfur atom in each ligand. The methyl thioether ligand in 3b exhibits relatively stronger σ-donation due to the localized availability of its lone pair, making it more nucleophilic and reactive toward the phosphonyl center. In contrast, the para-methoxy aryl thioether ligand in 3d is a comparatively weaker donor, as aryl rings are considered weaker donors than alkyl substituents, diminishing the nucleophilicity. These differences in donor strength could explain the upfield and downfield shifts observed with 3b and 3d in the 31P­{1H} NMR spectrum. The formation of precipitates during this reaction also suggests the generation of salts, likely chloride-substituted versions of 3b and 3d, consistent with the proposed mechanism of interaction. While reports of sulfur nucleophiles binding to phosphorus centers are rare, an analogous attack of dimethyl sulfide on reactive carbonyl compounds is a common way to make sulfonium salts. Another possibility is nucleophilic attack at the methylene carbon atoms in the phosphate ester. However, that mechanism is favored for phosphate esters that do not contain a good leaving group, such as chloride groups. To further validate the interaction between the thioether and the phosphonyl, a control experiment was performed in which DCP was added to a solution of dimethyl sulfide. We found that the 31P­{1H} NMR chemical shift upon DCP exposure was 4.12 ppm, which was nearly identical to that of 3b when it was exposed to DCP (Figure S10). The proximity of the Rh atom could also influence the shift due to field and spatial effects, and this contribution cannot be ruled out. ATR-FTIR and Raman spectroscopy were used to analyze vibrational changes in the dirhodium­(II,II) paddlewheel complexes after exposure to DCP. Pre-exposure ATR-FTIR and Raman spectral assignments for dirhodium­(II,II) complexes are provided in Tables S1 and S2, respectively, while the spectra are shown in Figure a and b. The spectra were assigned using standard spectroscopy handbooks and previously published studies of similar systems. ,− Spectra were also compared with those of dirhodium­(II) tetraacetate (Rh2(OAc)4) (Figures S22–S23) for validation of the vibrational analysis. The ATR-FTIR data of the four complexes are all very similar, with differences arising from the presence of the additional phenyl ring and substitution of the additional ring. From 500 cm–1 to 1000 cm–1, there are features from the acetate functionality, deformation of a phenyl ring, and phenyl C–H wagging in all four spectra. Above 1000 cm–1, 3a and 3b exhibit similar vibrational structures, while the main differences arise from very strong −NO2 and −OMe features in 3c and 3d. Near 3000 cm–1–3500 cm–1, C–H and N–H stretching can be observed in all the complexes. Far-IR data in the range 50 cm–1–400 cm–1 were collected for the dirhodium­(II,II) complexes, but the signal was overwhelmed by noise, and no discernible features could be identified. Like the ATR-FTIR spectra, the Raman spectra of the four complexes are all very similar. The Raman spectra contain low frequency (<200 cm–1) Rh–O deformation features present in all four systems, in addition to a very intense, sharp band around 310 cm–1 representing the Rh–Rh stretch of the complexes. The feature near 360 cm–1 is attributed to Rh–O stretching. From 500 cm–1 to 900 cm–1, the complexes share features from the acetate, amidate, and sulfide moieties present in the structures. Above 1000 cm–1, there are many additional features in the Raman spectra for complexes 3a, 3c, and 3d due to the presence of a second phenyl ring and substitution of the ring, while complex 3b mainly contains vibrations arising from alkyl groups (Figure b). The substitution of the second phenyl ring with −NO2 in 3c led to the observation of more dominant vibrational bands in that Raman spectrum that do not appear in the other dirhodium­(II,II) analogues. The bands at 1575 cm–1, 1334 cm–1, 1087 cm–1, and 1109 cm–1 are vibrational modes originating from the −NO2 group, which is very Raman active. All the systems then contain phenyl ring stretching and C–H stretching in the 1570 cm–1 and 3000 cm–1 regions, respectively. The ATR-FTIR spectra (Figure a) show many DCP and dirhodium­(II,II) complex features in the postexposure products. Major changes in the postexposure spectra include the broadening and red-shifting of bands assigned to PO (1264–1270 cm–1) and N–H (3280–3290 cm–1) stretching compared to their pre-exposure assignments of 1279 cm–1 and ∼3300 cm–1, respectively, with the N–H stretching feature near 3300 cm–1 being broadened so much that it is difficult to observe postexposure. This suggests intramolecular hydrogen bonding between the PO group on the DCP portion of the postexposure product and the N–H of the amide group that was part of the tethered ligand. There were also significant changes to the bands between 950 cm–1 and 1050 cm–1 and between 750 and 800 cm–1, which all originate from DCP and are ascribed to H2C–CH3 stretching, P–O–C2H5 antisymmetric stretching, and P–O–C2H5 symmetric stretching, respectively. The shifting of these bands in this manner and the changing band intensities suggest a considerable alteration to the electron density across the DCP portion of the reaction product. Lastly, the P–Cl stretch feature (∼550–600 cm–1) is not observed in the ATR-FTIR postexposure spectra, indicating that the chloride acted as a leaving group from the phosphorus center. The Raman spectrum (Figure b) shows similar characteristics as the ATR-FTIR data, with many DCP and dirhodium­(II,II) complex features present in the postexposure products. Below 1500 cm–1, there are several changes in the Raman data. The PO stretch is slightly broadened and red-shifted (as was observed in the ATR-FTIR spectra), indicating possible hydrogen bonding, while the symmetric P–O–CH2CH3 stretch (750 cm–1–800 cm–1) has changed band shape, intensity profile, and shifted in frequency. Most importantly, the P–Cl stretch (550–600 cm–1) was no longer detected in the Raman data supporting what was observed in the ATR-FTIR spectra. There were significant changes found in the antisymmetric P–O–C2H5 stretch (1010 cm–1) in the ATR-FTIR data (Figure a), with similar changes in frequency and band shape of the symmetric P–O–CH2CH3 stretch observed in the Raman. It is strongly indicative of a change in electron density around the phosphorus center, consistent with a reaction taking place at that site. Furthermore, the absence of the P–Cl stretch in the Raman implies that the phosphorus is no longer bound to the chloride, suggesting the chloride has acted as a leaving group. There is also a feature near 690 cm–1 in the postexposure products (Figures b, S25, S27) that is in the region of P–S, P–O, or C–S stretching. P–S stretching is predicted to be slightly lower in frequency (472 cm–1–613 cm–1), and previous theoretical studies on molecular systems with similar chemical moieties also predict the P–S stretch to be lower in frequency. ,,, C–S stretching is predicted to be in this general area (590 cm–1–735 cm–1) and usually appears as a strong band in Raman and weak in IR. ,,, However, when we compare the spectra of the postexposure products to the pre-exposure dirhodium­(II,II) complexes (S25, S27, Table S1), there is only a weakly observed C–S stretch that varied in frequency among the different complexes prior to exposure. It may be possible that after exposure to DCP, the sulfur becomes bound to the phosphorus, impacting the change in dipole moment for the C–S stretch, making it appear more intense. Variations in the symmetric and antisymmetric P–O–CH2CH3 stretching bands after exposure indicate that there are changes in electron distribution around the DCP part of the molecular system that seem to impact band intensity and profile. However, this feature at 690 cm–1 is also in the region of P–O stretching and seems to be consistent with the band shape and intensity of part of the symmetric P–O–CH2CH3 stretching bands assigned in the pre-exposure DCP spectra (Figure , Table S3). Given the fact that this feature appears too high in frequency to be a P–S stretch and there is a significant inconsistency between the C–S stretch observed in the pre-exposure Raman data for the complexes, we can make a stronger argument that the feature at 690 cm–1 in the postexposure data is related to symmetric P–O–CH2CH3 stretching. There are also features that appear at 498 cm–1, 415 cm–1, and 397 cm–1 in the postexposure spectrum of 3d without corresponding features in the data for 3b. Given the complicated nature of the expected products, it is difficult to ascertain their origin. There is nothing in the pre-exposure data for 3d that stands out as responsible, while the bands at 415 cm–1, and 397 cm–1 are very close to the DCP band located at 397 cm–1 (Figure , Table S3), assigned as a torsional vibration, although it is unclear why it would be absent in the spectrum for the postexposure product of 3b. The features could be related to the additional phenyl ring and methoxy substitution, as there are certain wagging and/or deformation modes for those groups in this region. , However, there is nothing particularly strong in the pre-exposure Raman spectrum for 3d located there, which makes that assignment difficult. For now, those features will remain unassigned. Finally, there is a very strong, broad band located near 295 cm–1 in the spectra for the postexposure products, with a noticeable shoulder near 330 cm–1. Based on comparison with the pre-exposure dirhodium­(II,II) complexes, the fact the Rh–Rh bond is anticipated to be present in the product, and the hypothesized formation of the Rh–Cl (known to have a strong and broad Raman band close to 300 cm–1), the features around 295 cm–1 and 330 cm–1 are most likely the Rh–Rh stretch and Rh–Cl stretch, respectively. Dirhodium­(II,II) paddlewheel complexes with tethered thioether ligands were synthesized to improve the selectivity of DCP detection and reduce false positives associated with hydrolytically active analytes. The tethered complexes we explored exhibited increased resistance to interferents compared to their untethered derivatives, including the previously problematic acetyl chloride. However, their reduced sensitivity compared to other metal-based chromogenic systems is an area that needs further improvement (see Table S6). Furthermore, the LODs observed for 3b and 3d are above the military exposure guideline (MEP) range of 1.0 × 10–5 mM–6.8 × 10–5 mM for catastrophic exposure to G-series nerve agents (Tables S7–S10). Spectroscopic studies supported our proposed mechanism that the chloride of the phosphorus is being displaced, and an interaction is taking place involving the sulfur and the phosphorus of DCP. Disproportionation of the paddlewheel complex may also be in play; however, the predicted products were identified in the mass spectra of the post-exposure products. Overall, the ligand design tempered reactivity but improved the selectivity of the complex regarding DCP detection. The results here will provide insights into further design changes of the chromogenic system, aiming to produce sensitive and selective OPNA probes.
