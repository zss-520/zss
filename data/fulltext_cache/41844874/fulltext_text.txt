Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are small, natural molecules that are usually composed of 10–50 amino acids. They are evolutionarily conserved components of the innate immune system found in a wide range of organisms, including humans, animals, plants, and microorganisms1. AMPs serve as the first line of defense against pathogens, exhibiting broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against fungi, viruses, bacteria and even parasites. Unlike traditional antibiotics, that mostly target specific biochemical pathways like protein translation or cell wall synthesis, AMPs mainly act by binding to microbial membranes through electrostatic interactions, leading to cell membrane disruption, cell lysis, and death. Moreover, their direct antimicrobial activity, many AMPs exhibit immunomodulatory properties, immune cell recruitment, wound healing, and influencing inflammation2. These diverse properties make AMPs compelling therapeutic candidates, offering advantages over traditional antibiotics, which commonly target narrow biochemical pathways. The rise of antimicrobial resistance has necessitated the development of high-quality, curated resources to centralize experimental data. Recent efforts in database construction have led to the development of comprehensive platforms like dbAMP 3.0, which integrates vast amounts of functional and structural data to support the discovery of novel therapeutic peptides3. Such resources are critical for training robust computational models, as they provide the large-scale, high-fidelity data required for modern artificial intelligence applications. AMPs, originally identified as innate immune defense molecules, have emerged as promising therapeutic candidates in oncology due to their unique biological properties. Unlike conventional chemotherapeutics, which often lack specificity and induce systemic toxicity, AMPs can preferentially target cancer cells through mechanisms involving membrane disruption, immune modulation, and tumor microenvironment regulation. These properties make AMPs highly relevant for the development of next-generation cancer therapeutic4. Cancer cells display distinct membrane characteristics, including increased negative surface charge, altered phospholipid composition, and overexpression of anionic molecules such as phosphatidylserine. AMPs exploit these features through electrostatic interactions, leading to membrane destabilization and selective apoptosis or necrosis of tumor cells while sparing normal cells. Examples of such peptides include melittin, cecropin, and LL-37, which have demonstrated activity across multiple cancer types5. AMPs operate through direct physical interactions with cell membranes rather than specific intracellular targets, making them less susceptible to conventional resistance mechanisms. Tumor cells that have developed resistance to chemotherapy or targeted therapies often remain vulnerable to AMP-mediated cytotoxicity. This property positions AMPs as valuable candidates for treating refractory or multidrug-resistant cancers, either as monotherapies or in combination with existing treatments6. Beyond direct cytotoxicity, AMPs can modulate the immune system by recruiting dendritic cells, macrophages, and T cells to the tumor site, regulating cytokine secretion, and promoting antigen presentation. Certain peptides, such as LL-37, defensins, and LTX-315, induce immunogenic cell death (ICD), stimulating adaptive anti-tumor immunity. These dual cytotoxic and immunomodulatory effects make AMPs excellent candidates for cancer immunotherapy and vaccine development. Several AMPs influence the tumor microenvironment by inhibiting angiogenesis, modulating stromal cell activity, reducing pro-tumor inflammation, and affecting extracellular matrix interactions. For example, cathelicidins and defensins can inhibit endothelial cell proliferation and angiogenesis, restricting tumor growth and metastasis, and creating a more therapeutically favorable microenvironment7. AMPs can enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. By increasing cancer cell membrane permeability, AMPs improve drug uptake and sensitize tumor cells to radiation-induced DNA damage. In combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors, AMPs may also enhance T-cell infiltration and anti-tumor immune responses, providing a synergistic therapeutic effect8. Advances in multi-omics technologies, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and lipidomics, allow the identification of tumor-specific AMP targets and facilitate the design of patient-tailored peptide therapies. AI-driven generative peptide models can optimize AMP sequences for cancer selectivity, stability, and reduced toxicity. These strategies align AMPs with the principles of precision oncology, enabling targeted and mechanism-driven interventions. It is essential to predict AMPs accurately. The rise of artificial intelligence has made computational approaches for predicting AMPs a key area of research. In the last decade, multiple machine learning (ML)-based tools have been introduced, including AmpGram9, and TP-MV10. More recently, deep learning (DL) has spurred further progress, enabling the creation of tools that utilize Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks. Prominent examples are Deep-AmPEP3011, sAMP-PFPDeep12, and AMPlify13. Many of these methods predict potential AMPs using only amino acid sequences. Further advancements have been made with models like iAMP-Attenpred14 and AMP-BERT15, which incorporate BERT-based16 semantic embeddings. These innovations have substantially improved prediction accuracy, driving the field of AMP research forward. Beyond standard sequence classification, new computational paradigms have emerged, such as the use of Contrastive Learning and Multi-view fusion frameworks to better characterize the diverse functional profiles of peptides17. These methods allow models to learn more discriminative representations by comparing active and inactive sequences in high-dimensional latent spaces. The above methods contributed well to the prediction of AMP. However, these approaches primarily rely on traditional ML techniques (i.e., Random Forest) and conventional DL architectures like CNNs and LSTM networks. The existing predictors exhibit several limitations that hinder their overall performance and generalizability. Firstly, a significant limitation is the lack of incorporation of advanced protein language models. These language models capture rich contextual and evolutionary information from protein sequences, which is essential for understanding complex biological patterns within peptides. Without these models, existing approaches are unable to fully exploit long-range dependencies and semantic embeddings crucial for accurate AMP prediction. Secondly, these methods often do not implement feature ensembling strategies and evolutionary-based features, which could enhance the discriminative power of predictive models. By relying mostly on amino acid sequences alone, they miss integrating complementary information from multiple feature descriptors. Furthermore, many existing tools struggle to capture the multi-functional nature of peptides. Recent studies have highlighted that predicting specific activities (e.g., anti-cancer vs. anti-bacterial) requires more sophisticated feature engineering that accounts for the delicate balance of hydrophobicity and charge distribution18. This lack of granular physicochemical modeling often results in poor performance when attempting to differentiate between therapeutically relevant peptides and general amphipathic sequences. Additionally, several existing predictors do not employ feature selection approaches adequately. As a result, redundant, irrelevant, or noisy features may remain in the data, leading to overfitting, increased computational cost, and reduced predictive accuracy. The absence of systematic feature selection limits model interpretability and efficiency. Moreover, the field is moving toward foundation models and large-scale pre-training on protein universes, yet many current AMP predictors remain restricted to small, task-specific datasets that limit their generalizability across different microbial taxa19. Finally, current models frequently avoid the application of explainability methods such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), which are crucial for understanding model decision-making processes. Without SHAP or similar interpretability frameworks, it is difficult to identify which features most influence predictions, limiting biological insights and confidence in model outputs. These limitations highlight a gap for improvement in AMP prediction performance. Addressing these shortcomings requires integrating protein language models with evolutionary features, rigorous feature selection, and explainability approaches that could advance the accuracy, robustness, and interpretability of computational AMP predictors. In this work, we propose an improved predictive model using an advanced DL framework. First, we computed the evolutionary features via Position Specific Scoring Matrix (PSSM) against each sequence. Pseudo approach is embedded into each PSSM to calculate the local region information. To remove the noisy and less informative patterns, Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) is applied to each segment. The newly designed feature descriptor is named Pseudo Position Specific Scoring Matrix–Discrete Cosine Transform (PsePSSM-DCT). To extract more meaningful features, we applied protein language models, i.e., Evolutionary Scale Modeling (ESM-2), Universal Representation (UniRep), and ProtTrans-T5 which extracts contextualized sequence features, capturing amino acid embeddings, long-range dependencies, evolutionary insights, and physicochemical properties. It learns functional and structural representations by modeling protein sequences in a transformer-based framework, enabling applications in binding prediction, structure modeling, and protein function annotation. Each feature set is fed into a Generative Adversarial Capsule Bidirectional Temporal Convolutional Neural Network (GAC-BiTCNN). GAC-BiTCNN is an advanced hybrid DL framework proposed for sequential data modeling in this work. It integrates the generative capabilities of Generative Adversarial Network (GAN), the feature-rich representations of Capsule Network (CapsNet), and the temporal modeling strength of Bidirectional Temporal Convolutional Neural Network (BiTCNN). This hybrid architecture enables the model to capture hierarchical and spatial dependencies in sequence data while enhancing generalization through adversarial training. The incorporation of bidirectional temporal convolutions ensures effective context learning by processing forward and backward temporal dependencies. By integrating AI, and biological knowledge, GAC-BiTCNN-AMP aligns with the objectives of precision medicine and healthcare innovation, offering a robust, interpretable, and scalable solution for peptide-based therapeutic discovery. AMP data were compiled from six public antimicrobial peptide (AMP) databases: CAMPR420, DBAASPv321, dbAMP222, DRAMP323, LAMP224, and YADAMP25, which contain experimentally validated peptide entries with associated antimicrobial activity data reported as Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) values. The individual datasets were merged into a unified collection where duplicate sequences were removed, and in cases of conflicting MIC values for the same peptide, the lowest MIC was retained to reflect the strongest experimentally reported potency. Peptides were classified as the positive class (AMP) based on biologically motivated criteria consistent with curation standards in major AMP databases, specifically demonstrating antimicrobial activity against at least one pathogen, maintaining an MIC ≤ 100 μg/ml, and possessing a sequence length between 6 and 50 amino acids. Following these filters, the positive dataset comprised 4821 sequences active against Pseudomonas aeruginosa, 2545 against Candida albicans, and 1875 against Salmonella spp. For the negative class (non-AMP), a set of 9190 sequences was retrieved from UniProtKB using a keyword search query designed to select short peptides (length 6–50) unlikely to exhibit antimicrobial function by excluding terms such as antimicrobial, antibiotic, and various secretory markers. These sequences were cross-referenced against the compiled AMP databases to exclude documented activity and were filtered to retain only the 20 canonical amino acids. To ensure dataset quality and minimize the risk of overfitting, the combined sequences were clustered using the CD-HIT tool26 at a sequence identity threshold of 40%, a standard threshold for balancing diversity and independence. Any sequences with non-standard characters or lengths outside the 6–50 residue range were excluded during this stage. To prevent data leakage and ensure model generalization, a temporal splitting strategy was applied where training and validation sequences were restricted to entries published before 2022, while the independent test set comprised sequences added in 2023–2024 or newly curated entries. Effective separation was confirmed through pairwise BLASTp alignments between the sets, showing no significant hits with an E-value threshold < 10−5. The final processed dataset consisted of 8141 AMP and 8290 non-AMP sequences, which were subjected to an 80/20 stratified split to yield a training set of 6513 AMP and 6632 non-AMP sequences, alongside an independent test set of 1628 AMP and 1658 non-AMP sequences. This rigorous pipeline ensures a high-quality, non-redundant, and leakage-resistant dataset suitable for unbiased performance evaluation. Feature extraction approaches27 are used to transform the features possessed by primary sequences into numerical forms as machine/deep learning frameworks are unable to deal directly with it. In this context, efficient methods are required to effectively extract the meaningful features. We use PsePSSM-DCT, ProtTrans-T5, UniRep, and ESM-2. ProtTrans-T528 is a modern protein language model that uses the T5 (Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer) architecture, which was developed in the natural language processing space, and extends it to the protein sequence domain. ProtTrans-T5 is pretrained on millions of protein sequences using the power of large-scale self-supervised learning, on comprehensive databases like UniRef50. This pretraining allows the model to learn rich, contextual representations of protein sequences, including evolutionary, structural, and functional information that is essential to many bioinformatics applications29. In contrast to conventional sequence-based approaches, in which amino acids are analyzed in isolation or with little context, ProtTrans-T5 produces embeddings that take into consideration the context of each amino acid in the full protein sequence. This allows the model to pick up fine biological patterns, long-range dependencies, and evolutionary signals that are important in elucidating protein structure and function30. ProtTrans-T5 is a flexible architecture that allows many downstream bioinformatics applications. It can be modified to perform protein family classification, subcellular localization prediction, and even protein sequence to three-dimensional structure mapping. Such flexibility renders ProtTrans T5 a useful and widely applicable tool in computational biology31. ProtTrans-T5 translates protein sequences into numerical vectors of high dimensions. The most common variant, commonly known as ProtT5-XL-UniRef50, generates embeddings whose dimension is 1024 per sequence. When a peptide or protein sequence is passed through ESM-2, it undergoes a series of transformations that encode each amino acid in a high-dimensional latent space. Feature selection39 is a fundamental step in machine learning that involves identifying and selecting the most relevant features from a dataset. Its primary goal is to improve model performance by removing redundant, irrelevant, or noisy features that may lead to overfitting or reduced generalization capability. In high-dimensional datasets, many features may carry overlapping or minimal information. Eliminating such features helps in reducing computational cost, enhancing model interpretability, increasing accuracy, and preventing the curse of dimensionality. By preserving only the most informative predictors, feature selection streamlines the learning process and often results in more robust and computationally efficient models. In this work, we used XGBoost-Forward Feature Selection (XGB-FFS). XGB-FFS is a hybrid approach that combines the gradient boosting power of XGBoost with the step-wise strategy of forward feature selection. XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting) is a highly efficient and scalable machine learning algorithm based on decision trees, known for its superior performance on structured data. Forward feature selection is a greedy search method that starts with no features and iteratively adds the most beneficial one at each step, based on a performance metric such as accuracy. By integrating XGBoost into the forward selection process, XGB-FFS evaluates the contribution of each feature using XGBoost’s intrinsic ability to rank features based on gain or frequency. This ensures that only those features that improve the predictive performance are retained. The process begins with an empty feature set. At each iteration, one new feature is considered from the remaining pool, and a temporary model is trained using the current selected features plus the candidate one. XGBoost evaluates this combination and measures model performance accuracy. The feature that results in the highest improvement is permanently added to the feature set. This process repeats until no further gain is observed or a stopping condition (e.g., predefined number of features or threshold improvement) is met. Among all algorithms, GAC-BiTCNN achieved the highest predictive results. The detailed explanation of GAC-BiTCNN is listed below. The GAC-BiTCNN is a hybrid deep learning architecture designed to address the challenges of complex sequence modeling, particularly in biological domains such as protein and peptide classification. It effectively integrates four powerful neural network components, including Generative Adversarial Network45, Capsule Networks, Bidirectional Temporal Convolutional Network46, and Convolutional Neural Network47 into a unified framework. This model is tailored to capture local patterns, long-range dependencies, and spatial hierarchies, while simultaneously addressing class imbalance and enhancing feature diversity through adversarial learning. To exploit the rich biological information embedded in protein sequences, GAC-BiTCNN employs a multi-source feature representation strategy. In this study, we utilized four feature sets: ProtTrans-T5, UniRep, ESM-2, and PsePSSM-DCT. Each of these encodings contributes unique information about the protein’s structure, function, and evolutionary profile. Each feature space is passed for model training via GAC-BiTCNN and performs prediction. To improve generalization, input feature is passed through a Generative Adversarial Network. The goal of a GAN is to train two separate neural networks: a Generator and a Discriminator against each other. A Generator that learns to produce realistic synthetic samples from random noise vectors, attempting to replicate the statistical characteristics of the real feature distributions. A Discriminator that distinguishes real samples from generated (fake) ones, thus encouraging the generator to improve48. Importantly, GANs are applied at the feature level rather than directly at the raw sequence level. This design choice is motivated by both biological and computational considerations. Protein and peptide functionality is governed by complex, non-linear physicochemical and evolutionary patterns, such as amphipathicity, net charge distribution, hydrophobic clustering, secondary-structure propensity, and conserved motifs, which are already embedded within pretrained protein language model representations and evolutionary descriptors. Feature-level embeddings thus reside in a continuous, biologically meaningful latent space that captures high-level semantic information not explicitly observable at the amino-acid sequence level. In contrast, sequence-level GANs operating on discrete amino-acid tokens often suffer from instability, mode collapse, and biologically implausible outputs, particularly for short sequences such as peptides. Such models may generate unrealistic residue transitions, disrupt critical functional motifs, or produce invalid length distributions. Feature-level augmentation avoids these issues by operating within a learned manifold where biological constraints are implicitly encoded, enabling the generation of synthetic samples that preserve functional realism while expanding the diversity of the training data. Furthermore, GAN-based augmentation is performed independently for each feature representation, ensuring that the structural, functional, and evolutionary signals specific to ProtTrans-T5, UniRep, ESM-2, and PsePSSM-DCT are preserved without cross-feature interference. This targeted augmentation strategy enriches each feature space individually, increasing intra-class diversity and alleviating class imbalance, common challenges in protein and peptide datasets. Compared to simpler augmentation strategies such as random oversampling or conservative amino-acid substitution, feature-level GAN augmentation enables exploration of a broader and more informative functional space. As demonstrated in our comparative experiments, this approach yields superior generalization performance while maintaining biologically feature distributions. Consequently, the use of GANs at the feature level enhances both the robustness and predictive stability of the downstream deep learning components. Following augmentation, each individual feature stream (real + synthetic) is passed through dedicated CNN blocks. These blocks serve as local feature extractors to learn low-level patterns such as sequence motifs, residue interactions, and structural sub-patterns. Each CNN operates independently on its respective feature set, preserving the unique semantic and statistical properties of that feature type. This parallel CNN architecture ensures specialized learning, where each feature stream benefits from customized convolutional filters suited to its characteristics. The outputs from the CNN layers are subsequently passed into BiTCN, where each feature set continues to be processed independently. BiTCN builds upon the foundation of standard TCN by introducing enhancements that make it particularly well-suited for modeling sequential biological data such as protein or peptide sequences49. One of the key strengths of BiTCN lies in its ability to capture long-range dependencies through the use of dilated convolutions. Effective separation was confirmed through pairwise BLASTp alignments between the sets, showing no significant hits with an E-value threshold < 10−5. ProtTrans-T528 is a modern protein language model that uses the T5 (Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer) architecture, which was developed in the natural language processing space, and extends it to the protein sequence domain. Feature selection39 is a fundamental step in machine learning that involves identifying and selecting the most relevant features from a dataset. Its primary goal is to improve model performance by removing redundant, irrelevant, or noisy features that may lead to overfitting or reduced generalization capability. In high-dimensional datasets, many features may carry overlapping or minimal information. Eliminating such features helps in reducing computational cost, enhancing model interpretability, increasing accuracy, and preventing the curse of dimensionality. By preserving only the most informative predictors, feature selection streamlines the learning process and often results in more robust and computationally efficient models. In this work, we used XGBoost-Forward Feature Selection (XGB-FFS). XGB-FFS is a hybrid approach that combines the gradient boosting power of XGBoost with the step-wise strategy of forward feature selection. XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting) is a highly efficient and scalable machine learning algorithm based on decision trees, known for its superior performance on structured data. Forward feature selection is a greedy search method that starts with no features and iteratively adds the most beneficial one at each step, based on a performance metric such as accuracy. By integrating XGBoost into the forward selection process, XGB-FFS evaluates the contribution of each feature using XGBoost’s intrinsic ability to rank features based on gain or frequency. This ensures that only those features that improve the predictive performance are retained. The process begins with an empty feature set. At each iteration, one new feature is considered from the remaining pool, and a temporary model is trained using the current selected features plus the candidate one. XGBoost evaluates this combination and measures model performance accuracy. The feature that results in the highest improvement is permanently added to the feature set. This process repeats until no further gain is observed or a stopping condition (e.g., predefined number of features or threshold improvement) is met. After feature extraction, the next step in model development is the construction/selection of the most efficient model training algorithm. In connection, we implemented several popular deep learning algorithms, including CNN28, TCN40, BiLSTM41, and GAC-BiTCNN. Among all algorithms, GAC-BiTCNN achieved the highest predictive results. The detailed explanation of GAC-BiTCNN is listed below. The GAC-BiTCNN is a hybrid deep learning architecture designed to address the challenges of complex sequence modeling, particularly in biological domains such as protein and peptide classification. It effectively integrates four powerful neural network components, including Generative Adversarial Network45, Capsule Networks, Bidirectional Temporal Convolutional Network46, and Convolutional Neural Network47 into a unified framework. This model is tailored to capture local patterns, long-range dependencies, and spatial hierarchies, while simultaneously addressing class imbalance and enhancing feature diversity through adversarial learning. To exploit the rich biological information embedded in protein sequences, GAC-BiTCNN employs a multi-source feature representation strategy. In this study, we utilized four feature sets: ProtTrans-T5, UniRep, ESM-2, and PsePSSM-DCT. Each of these encodings contributes unique information about the protein’s structure, function, and evolutionary profile. Each feature space is passed for model training via GAC-BiTCNN and performs prediction. To improve generalization, input feature is passed through a Generative Adversarial Network. The goal of a GAN is to train two separate neural networks: a Generator and a Discriminator against each other. A Generator that learns to produce realistic synthetic samples from random noise vectors, attempting to replicate the statistical characteristics of the real feature distributions. A Discriminator that distinguishes real samples from generated (fake) ones, thus encouraging the generator to improve48. Importantly, GANs are applied at the feature level rather than directly at the raw sequence level. This design choice is motivated by both biological and computational considerations. Protein and peptide functionality is governed by complex, non-linear physicochemical and evolutionary patterns, such as amphipathicity, net charge distribution, hydrophobic clustering, secondary-structure propensity, and conserved motifs, which are already embedded within pretrained protein language model representations and evolutionary descriptors. Feature-level embeddings thus reside in a continuous, biologically meaningful latent space that captures high-level semantic information not explicitly observable at the amino-acid sequence level. In contrast, sequence-level GANs operating on discrete amino-acid tokens often suffer from instability, mode collapse, and biologically implausible outputs, particularly for short sequences such as peptides. Such models may generate unrealistic residue transitions, disrupt critical functional motifs, or produce invalid length distributions. Feature-level augmentation avoids these issues by operating within a learned manifold where biological constraints are implicitly encoded, enabling the generation of synthetic samples that preserve functional realism while expanding the diversity of the training data. Furthermore, GAN-based augmentation is performed independently for each feature representation, ensuring that the structural, functional, and evolutionary signals specific to ProtTrans-T5, UniRep, ESM-2, and PsePSSM-DCT are preserved without cross-feature interference. This targeted augmentation strategy enriches each feature space individually, increasing intra-class diversity and alleviating class imbalance, common challenges in protein and peptide datasets. Compared to simpler augmentation strategies such as random oversampling or conservative amino-acid substitution, feature-level GAN augmentation enables exploration of a broader and more informative functional space. As demonstrated in our comparative experiments, this approach yields superior generalization performance while maintaining biologically feature distributions. Consequently, the use of GANs at the feature level enhances both the robustness and predictive stability of the downstream deep learning components. Following augmentation, each individual feature stream (real + synthetic) is passed through dedicated CNN blocks. These blocks serve as local feature extractors to learn low-level patterns such as sequence motifs, residue interactions, and structural sub-patterns. Each CNN operates independently on its respective feature set, preserving the unique semantic and statistical properties of that feature type. This parallel CNN architecture ensures specialized learning, where each feature stream benefits from customized convolutional filters suited to its characteristics. The outputs from the CNN layers are subsequently passed into BiTCN, where each feature set continues to be processed independently. BiTCN builds upon the foundation of standard TCN by introducing enhancements that make it particularly well-suited for modeling sequential biological data such as protein or peptide sequences49. One of the key strengths of BiTCN lies in its ability to capture long-range dependencies through the use of dilated convolutions. This allows the model to access a broader temporal context without increasing computational complexity. Additionally, BiTCN preserves the temporal order of sequence data, which is critical in biological sequences where the arrangement of amino acids determines functional and structural properties. Importantly, BiTCN processes input in both forward and backward directions, enabling the model to extract contextual information from both preceding and succeeding residues. The output of each BiTCN is computed by combining the results of forward and backward TCN passes, typically as follows:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$BiTCN_{output} = TCN_{forward} \left( x \right) + TCN_{backward} \left( x \right)$$\end{document} This bidirectional formulation allows the network to model inter-residue dependencies across the entire sequence, which is essential for capturing long-range interactions that may be spatially distant but functionally important. As a result, BiTCN provides a robust and context-aware representation of sequence data, enhancing the model’s ability to make biologically meaningful predictions. The outputs generated by the BiTCN modules are subsequently fed into Capsule Networks, processed independently for each feature stream. Unlike traditional CNNs that rely on scalar neurons, Capsule Networks utilize vector-based capsules to capture spatial hierarchies and inter-feature dependencies. This structure is architecturally designed to model hierarchical relationships among learned sequence features, which can enhance the representation of complex dependencies in protein and peptide sequences, as reflected by improved predictive performance in this study50. The GAC-BiTCNN is a hybrid deep learning architecture designed to address the challenges of complex sequence modeling, particularly in biological domains such as protein and peptide classification. It effectively integrates four powerful neural network components, including Generative Adversarial Network45, Capsule Networks, Bidirectional Temporal Convolutional Network46, and Convolutional Neural Network47 into a unified framework. This model is tailored to capture local patterns, long-range dependencies, and spatial hierarchies, while simultaneously addressing class imbalance and enhancing feature diversity through adversarial learning. To exploit the rich biological information embedded in protein sequences, GAC-BiTCNN employs a multi-source feature representation strategy. In this study, we utilized four feature sets: ProtTrans-T5, UniRep, ESM-2, and PsePSSM-DCT. Each of these encodings contributes unique information about the protein’s structure, function, and evolutionary profile. Each feature space is passed for model training via GAC-BiTCNN and performs prediction. To improve generalization, input feature is passed through a Generative Adversarial Network. The goal of a GAN is to train two separate neural networks: a Generator and a Discriminator against each other. A Generator that learns to produce realistic synthetic samples from random noise vectors, attempting to replicate the statistical characteristics of the real feature distributions. A Discriminator that distinguishes real samples from generated (fake) ones, thus encouraging the generator to improve48. Importantly, GANs are applied at the feature level rather than directly at the raw sequence level. This design choice is motivated by both biological and computational considerations. Protein and peptide functionality is governed by complex, non-linear physicochemical and evolutionary patterns, such as amphipathicity, net charge distribution, hydrophobic clustering, secondary-structure propensity, and conserved motifs, which are already embedded within pretrained protein language model representations and evolutionary descriptors. Feature-level embeddings thus reside in a continuous, biologically meaningful latent space that captures high-level semantic information not explicitly observable at the amino-acid sequence level. In contrast, sequence-level GANs operating on discrete amino-acid tokens often suffer from instability, mode collapse, and biologically implausible outputs, particularly for short sequences such as peptides. Such models may generate unrealistic residue transitions, disrupt critical functional motifs, or produce invalid length distributions. Feature-level augmentation avoids these issues by operating within a learned manifold where biological constraints are implicitly encoded, enabling the generation of synthetic samples that preserve functional realism while expanding the diversity of the training data. Furthermore, GAN-based augmentation is performed independently for each feature representation, ensuring that the structural, functional, and evolutionary signals specific to ProtTrans-T5, UniRep, ESM-2, and PsePSSM-DCT are preserved without cross-feature interference. This targeted augmentation strategy enriches each feature space individually, increasing intra-class diversity and alleviating class imbalance, common challenges in protein and peptide datasets. Compared to simpler augmentation strategies such as random oversampling or conservative amino-acid substitution, feature-level GAN augmentation enables exploration of a broader and more informative functional space. As demonstrated in our comparative experiments, this approach yields superior generalization performance while maintaining biologically feature distributions. Consequently, the use of GANs at the feature level enhances both the robustness and predictive stability of the downstream deep learning components. Following augmentation, each individual feature stream (real + synthetic) is passed through dedicated CNN blocks. These blocks serve as local feature extractors to learn low-level patterns such as sequence motifs, residue interactions, and structural sub-patterns. Each CNN operates independently on its respective feature set, preserving the unique semantic and statistical properties of that feature type. This parallel CNN architecture ensures specialized learning, where each feature stream benefits from customized convolutional filters suited to its characteristics. The outputs from the CNN layers are subsequently passed into BiTCN, where each feature set continues to be processed independently. BiTCN builds upon the foundation of standard TCN by introducing enhancements that make it particularly well-suited for modeling sequential biological data such as protein or peptide sequences49. One of the key strengths of BiTCN lies in its ability to capture long-range dependencies through the use of dilated convolutions. This allows the model to access a broader temporal context without increasing computational complexity. Additionally, BiTCN preserves the temporal order of sequence data, which is critical in biological sequences where the arrangement of amino acids determines functional and structural properties. Importantly, BiTCN processes input in both forward and backward directions, enabling the model to extract contextual information from both preceding and succeeding residues. The output of each BiTCN is computed by combining the results of forward and backward TCN passes, typically as follows:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$BiTCN_{output} = TCN_{forward} \left( x \right) + TCN_{backward} \left( x \right)$$\end{document} This bidirectional formulation allows the network to model inter-residue dependencies across the entire sequence, which is essential for capturing long-range interactions that may be spatially distant but functionally important. As a result, BiTCN provides a robust and context-aware representation of sequence data, enhancing the model’s ability to make biologically meaningful predictions. The outputs generated by the BiTCN modules are subsequently fed into Capsule Networks, processed independently for each feature stream. Unlike traditional CNNs that rely on scalar neurons, Capsule Networks utilize vector-based capsules to capture spatial hierarchies and inter-feature dependencies. This structure is architecturally designed to model hierarchical relationships among learned sequence features, which can enhance the representation of complex dependencies in protein and peptide sequences, as reflected by improved predictive performance in this study50. To gain insights into the decision-making behavior of the proposed GAC-BiTCNN, we employed the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). SHAP53 is a game-theoretic approach that quantifies the contribution of each input feature to a model’s prediction, enabling both local (instance-level) and global (model-level) analysis of feature relevance. In this study, SHAP is used to examine feature contribution patterns and representation-level importance, rather than to assign explicit biochemical meaning to individual feature dimensions. Because protein language model (PLM) embeddings encode biological information in a distributed latent space, individual embedding dimensions do not correspond directly to specific physicochemical or structural properties. Accordingly, the SHAP analysis is designed to assess how strongly different latent dimensions and feature groups influence predictions, to identify dominant or redundant subspaces, and to validate whether the model relies on compact and discriminative representations, rather than diffuse noise. The SHAP-based analysis was performed on the four most effective feature descriptors including ESM-2, UniRep, ProtTrans-T5, and PsePSSM-DCT, as shown in Fig. 2a–d. For ESM-2 (Fig. 2a), SHAP values are relatively balanced (approximately −1.0–+1.5), indicating stable and evenly distributed contribution patterns across latent dimensions. Similarly, UniRep (Fig. 2b) exhibits consistent SHAP contributions with comparable ranges, reflecting robust and distributed encoding of sequence-level information. In contrast, ProtTrans-T5 (Fig. 2c) shows a broader spread of SHAP values (approximately −1.5–+1.5), suggesting that while many latent dimensions contribute moderately, a subset exerts stronger influence on the classification outcome.Fig. 2SHAP-based representation-level interpretation of the proposed GAC-BiTCNN using the top 20 ranked latent features from four different descriptors: (a) ESM-2, (b) UniRep, (c) ProtTrans-T5, and (d) PsePSSM-DCT. The binarization of Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) values into binary AMP (positive) vs. non-AMP (negative) labels is a standard and biologically motivated practice in the AMP prediction field20. AMPs are defined as peptides with demonstrated antimicrobial activity at low concentrations, typically MIC ≤ 100–200 μM against at least one tested microbial strain, reflecting their role as effectors in innate immunity with selective cytotoxicity toward pathogens. Conversely, peptides with MIC > 200–500 μM, or those with no measurable activity, are considered inactive or non-AMP in the context of therapeutic potential. This binary classification aligns with the functional definition used by major AMP databases, such as CAMPR4, DBAASP, dbAMP, DRAMP, and LAMP2, which provide curated binary labels based on experimental assays. Utilizing a threshold (e.g., MIC ≤ 100 μM) enables seamless cross-database integration and comparison. Furthermore, binarization simplifies the learning task, focusing the model on discriminative patterns associated with true antimicrobial function, such as cationic charge, amphipathicity, and membrane disruption, while avoiding the high variability inherent in continuous MIC regression due to differences in strains and assay conditions. For precision oncology applications, as emphasized in this study, the binary framework is particularly relevant because it prioritizes peptides with strong, broad-spectrum activity, a prerequisite for evaluation as immunomodulatory or tumor-targeting agents. The proposed GAC-BiTCNN-AMP framework addresses a critical need in antimicrobial research by offering a robust computational approach for identifying AMPs, which are vital alternatives to conventional antibiotics in combating multidrug-resistant pathogens. By leveraging the therapeutic potential of AMPs, this study contributes to advancing peptide-based drug discovery and supports the development of novel macromolecular therapeutics for infectious diseases, wound healing, and cancer treatment. A major strength of this work lies in its integration of multiple discriminative feature representations. The combination of transformer-based protein language models (ProtTrans-T5, UniRep, and ESM-2) with a novel evolutionary descriptor (PsePSSM-DCT) captures both contextual sequence information and evolutionary constraints, enabling the model to learn richer and more discriminative peptide representations than conventional approaches. Another strength is the innovative hybrid architecture that combines Generative Adversarial Networks, Capsule Networks, and Bidirectional Temporal Convolutional Neural Networks with attention mechanisms. This multi-model design enhances feature extraction, improves generalization, and achieves superior predictive performance compared to existing AMP predictors. Furthermore, the application of XGB-Forward Feature Selection reduces redundancy and focuses on informative attributes, while SHapley Additive exPlanations provide interpretability, ensuring that the model’s predictions are not only accurate but also biologically meaningful. Together, these contributions make GAC-BiTCNN-AMP a powerful, interpretable, and generalizable computational platform for high-throughput AMP prediction, with direct implications for therapeutic peptide discovery and biomedical applications. Despite the strong performance observed, several limitations inherent to current AMP benchmark datasets should be acknowledged. First, the datasets compiled from CAMPR4, DBAASP v3, dbAMP 2.0, DRAMP 3.0, LAMP2, and YADAMP16–21 primarily contain experimentally validated sequences from relatively well-studied organisms and pathogens. This may introduce selection bias toward peptides with broad-spectrum or high-potency activity, potentially over-representing certain structural motifs (e.g., cationic and amphipathic sequences) while under-representing rare or niche AMPs. Second, negative (non-AMP) sequences are often sourced from general protein databases (e.g., UniProtKB) using keyword exclusion, which may inadvertently include peptides with weak or context-dependent antimicrobial activity not yet experimentally characterized. Third, even after strict CD-HIT clustering at 40% identity and temporal/source-aware splitting, distant homology or convergent functional evolution in short peptides cannot be entirely eliminated, which could contribute to optimistic cross-validation estimates. Finally, the binary classification framework (MIC ≤ 100 μg/ml as positive) simplifies a continuous activity spectrum and may not fully capture nuanced potency differences relevant for therapeutic applications. While our temporal split and BLASTp homology checks (see Sect. "Datasets collection") minimize direct data leakage, these dataset characteristics likely contribute to the high reported metrics. Future work should incorporate more diverse negative controls (e.g., scrambled or shuffled sequences with preserved physicochemical properties), multi-task regression on continuous MIC values, and validation on emerging large-scale AMP screening datasets to better assess generalizability. The proposed GAC-BiTCNN-AMP framework addresses a critical need in antimicrobial research by offering a robust computational approach for identifying AMPs, which are vital alternatives to conventional antibiotics in combating multidrug-resistant pathogens. By leveraging the therapeutic potential of AMPs, this study contributes to advancing peptide-based drug discovery and supports the development of novel macromolecular therapeutics for infectious diseases, wound healing, and cancer treatment. A major strength of this work lies in its integration of multiple discriminative feature representations. The combination of transformer-based protein language models (ProtTrans-T5, UniRep, and ESM-2) with a novel evolutionary descriptor (PsePSSM-DCT) captures both contextual sequence information and evolutionary constraints, enabling the model to learn richer and more discriminative peptide representations than conventional approaches. Another strength is the innovative hybrid architecture that combines Generative Adversarial Networks, Capsule Networks, and Bidirectional Temporal Convolutional Neural Networks with attention mechanisms. This multi-model design enhances feature extraction, improves generalization, and achieves superior predictive performance compared to existing AMP predictors. Furthermore, the application of XGB-Forward Feature Selection reduces redundancy and focuses on informative attributes, while SHapley Additive exPlanations provide interpretability, ensuring that the model’s predictions are not only accurate but also biologically meaningful. Together, these contributions make GAC-BiTCNN-AMP a powerful, interpretable, and generalizable computational platform for high-throughput AMP prediction, with direct implications for therapeutic peptide discovery and biomedical applications. Despite the strong performance observed, several limitations inherent to current AMP benchmark datasets should be acknowledged. First, the datasets compiled from CAMPR4, DBAASP v3, dbAMP 2.0, DRAMP 3.0, LAMP2, and YADAMP16–21 primarily contain experimentally validated sequences from relatively well-studied organisms and pathogens. This may introduce selection bias toward peptides with broad-spectrum or high-potency activity, potentially over-representing certain structural motifs (e.g., cationic and amphipathic sequences) while under-representing rare or niche AMPs. Second, negative (non-AMP) sequences are often sourced from general protein databases (e.g., UniProtKB) using keyword exclusion, which may inadvertently include peptides with weak or context-dependent antimicrobial activity not yet experimentally characterized. Third, even after strict CD-HIT clustering at 40% identity and temporal/source-aware splitting, distant homology or convergent functional evolution in short peptides cannot be entirely eliminated, which could contribute to optimistic cross-validation estimates. Finally, the binary classification framework (MIC ≤ 100 μg/ml as positive) simplifies a continuous activity spectrum and may not fully capture nuanced potency differences relevant for therapeutic applications. While our temporal split and BLASTp homology checks (see Sect. "Datasets collection") minimize direct data leakage, these dataset characteristics likely contribute to the high reported metrics. Future work should incorporate more diverse negative controls (e.g., scrambled or shuffled sequences with preserved physicochemical properties), multi-task regression on continuous MIC values, and validation on emerging large-scale AMP screening datasets to better assess generalizability. This study introduces GAC-BiTCNN-AMP, a novel hybrid framework for the accurate prediction of antimicrobial peptides. By integrating GAN-based data augmentation, capsule networks, and bidirectional temporal convolutions with attention guidance, the model effectively captures local, global, and hierarchical sequence features. The incorporation of protein language models (ProtTrans-T5, UniRep, and ESM-2) alongside the handcrafted PsePSSM-DCT descriptor and feature selection via XGB-FFS enhanced predictive power while reducing redundancy. Extensive evaluations across training, testing, and independent datasets demonstrated that GAC-BiTCNN-AMP surpasses existing machine learning and deep learning predictors in terms of accuracy, robustness, and interpretability. Importantly, SHAP analysis served as effective representation-level validation of the fused feature contributions, reinforcing the model’s reliability and alignment with learned discriminative patterns. While the SHAP analysis in this study operates on abstract latent dimensions of the fused embeddings and selected evolutionary descriptors (rather than direct residue- or motif-level attribution), it still confirms that the model relies on biologically relevant representation patterns for accurate AMP classification. Future work will focus on advanced deep learning strategies, such as transfer learning and multi-task learning to further expand the scope of antimicrobial peptide prediction. By leveraging pretrained protein language models on massive biological datasets, the framework could be fine-tuned not only for AMP identification but also for related tasks such as predicting peptide toxicity, stability, hemolytic activity, and functional subclass categorization. Similarly, incorporating generative models within this architecture could accelerate the de novo design of novel peptide sequences with optimized therapeutic properties. Such advancements would extend the utility of GAC-BiTCNN-AMP from classification toward a comprehensive platform for peptide discovery and rational drug development.
