Achieving human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) epidemic control by 2030 requires identifying recent HIV infections to enhance surveillance and enable prompt, targeted interventions. Understanding the pattern of recent versus long-term infections provides critical insight into the dynamics of HIV transmission in specific populations and geographic areas. This study aimed to characterize health indices among newly diagnosed HIV-positive patients at the Lagos University Teaching Hospital HIV Clinic based on their recency test classification as either “recent” or “long-term” infections, and to examine the relationship between socio-demographic factors and infection patterns. A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among 456 newly diagnosed people living with HIV (PLHIV) attending the APIN Clinic at Lagos University Teaching Hospital (LUTH) over three months in 2024. Participants were selected using a systematic sampling technique. HIV testing followed a parallel testing algorithm, and all reactive samples were subsequently tested with the Unigold HIV test for confirmation and the Asante HIV-1 Rapid Recency Assay for infection recency classification. The Asante assay displays three distinct bands - control, verification, and long-term infection lines - allowing for differentiation between recent and long-term HIV infections. These recency results were used to support clinical decision-making, epidemiological surveillance, and targeted prevention interventions. The study population comprised male and female clients aged 15 years and above who were newly diagnosed as HIV-positive and met the inclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria were: a reactive Determine test result, no prior HIV diagnosis or antiretroviral therapy (ART) use, and no opt-out from recency testing, in line with the National HIV testing guidelines. Exclusion criteria included newly diagnosed clients presenting with WHO Clinical Stage 3 or 4 disease or those who reported prior or current ARV use at the time of HIV testing. Data were collected using pre-tested, interviewer-administered structured questionnaires and were analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2009 and IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee of Lagos University Teaching Hospital (LUTH). A total of 456 respondents participated in the study (100% response rate), with a mean age of 44.9 ± 11.8 years and a female predominance (63.2%). Overall, 23.5% were classified as having a recent HIV infection. Recent infections were significantly associated with younger age (p < 0.001), being single (p = 0.001), and lower educational attainment (p = 0.009). Recent infection was more common among participants with same-gender or bisexual sexual preferences (p = 0.006), those with multiple sexual partners (p = 0.001), alcohol users (p = 0.002), and people who inject drugs (p = 0.034). Clinical factors showed strong associations: WHO stage 1 (p < 0.001) and CD4 ≥ 200 cells/mm³ (p < 0.001) were strongly predictive of recent HIV infection. Multivariate analysis showed that older age groups, post-secondary education, and WHO stage 2 significantly reduced the odds of recent infection, while CD4 ≥ 200 cells/mm³ increased it. Other sociodemographic and behavioral variables were not significantly associated. A total of 456 questionnaires were administered, yielding a 100% response rate. Table 1 shows that the mean age of respondents was 44.9 ± 11.8 years. The sex ratio was 1.7, with a higher proportion of females (63.2%) compared to males (36.8%). The majority of respondents (53.9%) were married or cohabited. Most (77.0%) practiced Christianity, and the predominant ethnic group was Yoruba (46.9%). A small proportion (11.4%) reported smoking, with 63.5% of smokers using cigarettes. Up to 41.7% of respondents had attained post-secondary education. More than two-thirds (77.6%) were employed, with the largest proportion (36.4%) classified as semi-skilled workers. It is also observed that 23.5% of respondents had a recent HIV infection. Approximately half of the participants were classified as WHO HIV clinical stage 2, and over half (63.8%) had CD4 cell counts ≥ 200 cells/mm³. As seen on Table 2, there was a statistically significant association between age and recent HIV infection (χ² = 22.680, p < 0.001). The proportion of recent infections decreased with increasing age, from 50.0% in those under 20 years to 14.9% in those aged 51 years and above. Marital status was also significantly associated with recent HIV infection (χ² = 18.605, p = 0.001), with singles (34.4%) more likely to have recent infections compared to those who were married or cohabiting (20.7%). There was also a statistically significant relationship between level of education and timing of HIV infection. A statistically significant association was observed between the highest level of education and recent HIV infection (χ² = 13.486, p = 0.009). The proportion of respondents with recent infections was highest among those with no formal education (32.3%) and those who completed senior secondary school (31.8%), compared to only 15.8% among those with post-secondary education. This suggests that individuals with lower educational attainment were more likely to have recent HIV infections than those with higher education. There was no statistically significant association between employment status and recent HIV infection (χ² = 0.008, p = 0.931). Among employed respondents, 23.2% had recent HIV infections compared to 22.7% among the unemployed, indicating minimal difference based on employment status. Similarly, occupation type was not significantly associated with recency test results (Fisher’s exact p = 0.655). The proportion of recent infections was highest among unskilled workers (32.1%) and professionals (31.3%), followed by self-employed (28.6%), semi-skilled (22.9%), and skilled workers (20.0%). No significant association was found between sex and recency status (p = 0.412). There is a statistically significant association found between gender inclination for sex and recency of HIV infection (χ² = 10.353, p = 0.006). Respondents with opposite-gender preference had a lower proportion of recent infections (21.6%) compared to those with same-gender (50.0%) or both-gender (37.5%) preferences. The number of sexual partners also showed a significant relationship with recent HIV infections (χ² = 14.290, p = 0.001), with individuals reporting multiple partners having a higher proportion of recent infections (36.4%) compared to those with one partner (18.9%). Other variables such as current relationship status, sexual activity, last sexual encounter, sexual practices, and condom use were not significantly associated with recency status. Alcohol ingestion was statistically significantly associated with recent infection (χ² = 9.708, p = 0.002), with 31.9% of drinkers having recent infections compared to 18.9% of non-drinkers. Drug injection was also significantly associated (χ² = 4.490, p = 0.034), with 57.1% of injecting drug users (PWIDS) having recent infections versus 22.9% among non-users. No statistically significant associations were found for blood transfusion status and timing of HIV infection, although a trend was noted toward higher recency among those transfused within the past year. WHO clinical stage showed a strong association with recency (χ² = 45.791, p < 0.001). Stage 1 clients had the highest proportion of recent HIV infections (92.6%). CD4 count was also significantly associated (χ² = 56.605, p < 0.001), with 34.7% of those with CD4 ≥ 200 cells/mm³ classified as recently infected, compared to only 3.6% among those with CD4 < 200 cells/mm³. Table 3 shows that individuals aged 21–30, 31–40, and 41–50 years were 11, 4, and 4 times less likely, respectively, to have a recent infection compared to those under 20 years (OR = 0.095, 0.237, and 0.251, respectively). Single individuals were five times less likely to have a recent infection than those who were divorced (OR = 0.223). Respondents with post-secondary education were 31 times less likely to have a recent infection than those with no formal education (OR = 0.507). Using WHO Clinical Stage 1 as the reference category (OR = 1.00), individuals in WHO Clinical Stage 2 had significantly lower odds of having a recent HIV infection as determined by the recency test (OR = 0.575. Respondents with CD4 counts ≥ 200 cells/mm³ were 10 times more likely to have a recent infection than those with CD4 counts < 200 cells/mm³ (OR = 10.098). Other variables were not statistically significant. These findings highlight the need for intensified, targeted prevention strategies—particularly for adolescents and young adults, individuals with low education, key populations, and people engaging in high-risk behaviors. Integrating recency testing into routine HIV services, expanding youth-friendly and community-based testing, and strengthening harm-reduction interventions are crucial for interrupting ongoing transmission and improving early diagnosis. This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the socio-demographic and clinical profiles of newly diagnosed PLHIV, revealing key differences between those with recent and long-term infections. It successfully identified patterns and predictors associated with HIV recency, offering insights for tailored interventions. The presence of a significant number of recent infections, particularly in specific LGAs, indicates ongoing community transmission and highlights the need to intensify prevention strategies. The findings also affirm the effectiveness of existing interventions in supporting long-term HIV-positive individuals, as evidenced by their relatively stable health indices. Future research should include viral load results and explore longitudinal outcomes to better understand the trajectory of HIV infections in different demographic groups. Finally, this study reinforces the value of recency testing and demographic mapping in shaping HIV response strategies and advocates for sustained, multisectoral action to curb new infections and improve health outcomes. Supplementary Material 1 Supplementary Material 1 Supplementary Material 2 Supplementary Material 2 Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection remains a significant public health challenge worldwide, despite considerable progress in treatment and prevention. The virus targets the immune system, particularly CD4 cells, rendering individuals susceptible to opportunistic infections such as tuberculosis, fungal diseases, and certain cancers [1]. Although HIV infection is incurable, antiretroviral therapy (ART) has enabled people living with HIV (PLHIV) to achieve viral suppression, prolonging life expectancy and improving quality of life [2]. While HIV infection can affect individuals across all demographics, certain populations are at higher risk due to behavioral, biological, and structural factors. These include individuals engaging in unprotected sex, people with multiple sexual partners, persons with untreated sexually transmitted infections (STIs), people who inject drugs (PWIDs), and men who have sex with men (MSM). Additional risk is posed to HIV exposed infant (HEI), that is, infants of HIV-positive mothers during childbirth and breastfeeding, especially in the absence of appropriate preventive measures [3–5]. Recent HIV infection - defined as infection acquired within the previous six to twelve months - is particularly critical from a public health standpoint due to its association with high viral load and infectivity. Individuals in this stage often remain undiagnosed and continue to engage in high-risk behaviors, increasing the likelihood of further transmission. Recency assays, also known as Rapid Tests for Recent Infections (RTRIs), employ one or more biomarkers to differentiate between recent and long-term HIV infections among individuals newly diagnosed with HIV. The Recent Infection Testing Algorithm (RITA) integrates one or more laboratory assays with clinical data - typically viral load measurements - to classify HIV infection as either recently acquired or long-term, the latter referring to infections acquired more than one year prior. Antibody avidity, which reflects the binding strength and functional maturation of antibodies, increases progressively following seroconversion. This characteristic enables the use of surrogate markers to estimate the duration of infection. In long-term infections, high-avidity antibodies exhibit strong antigen binding, whereas in recent infections, low-avidity antibodies demonstrate weaker or incomplete binding. Differentiating recent from long-term infections among newly diagnosed persons provides valuable epidemiological insights into HIV transmission patterns and supports the design and implementation of targeted prevention and control strategies [6–9]. Recent infection testing among newly diagnosed people living with HIV (PLHIV) utilizes existing diagnostic platforms to enhance public health surveillance and programmatic response. This strategy has been implemented in several countries, including Nigeria, where the Asante HIV-1 Rapid Recency Assay is incorporated alongside the Unigold test within a parallel testing algorithm. The testing process commences with the Determine HIV test as the initial screening tool. Individuals with reactive results subsequently undergo confirmatory testing using both the Unigold and Asante test kits. The Asante HIV-1 Rapid Recency Assay displays three distinct lines: a control line, a positive verification line, and a long-term infection line. Interpretation of these lines allows differentiation between recent and long-term HIV infections, thereby supporting clinical decision-making, epidemiological surveillance, and targeted prevention interventions [6–9]. Globally, HIV has claimed over 40 million lives and continues to affect millions, with an estimated 39 million PLHIV at the end of 2022 [10]. In 2021 alone, approximately 1.3 million people acquired new infections. Key populations - such as sex workers, men who have sex with men, people who inject drugs, and transgender individuals - account for over half of new infections [4, 11]. The burden of HIV infection remains disproportionately high in sub-Saharan Africa, where women and girls constitute a significant proportion of new infections [12–14]. Nigeria, with an estimated 1.9 million PLHIV, ranks third globally in HIV burden. Although prevalence has declined to 1.4% among adults aged 15–49, substantial gaps persist, particularly in early diagnosis and treatment access [15]. Lagos State, a high-burden area, has approximately 120,000 PLHIV, with an estimated 60,000 yet to initiate care [16, 17]. Routine surveillance of newly diagnosed HIV infections remains a cornerstone for effective prevention, care, and treatment strategies. Recent advances have introduced rapid tests for recent infection (RTRI) as part of the Recent Infection Testing Algorithm (RITA), allowing real-time surveillance and response [8, 18]. These data are essential for identifying transmission “hotspots,” evaluating the effectiveness of interventions, and informing resource allocation. The integration of recency testing into national surveillance systems has been supported by initiatives such as the U.S. President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR), which began implementing recency testing in Nigeria in 2020 [8, 18]. In Lagos State, recency testing is operational in 11 facilities, including the LUTH, AIDS Prevention in Nigeria (APIN) Clinic. Despite ongoing interventions, there is limited published data on the distribution of recent versus long-term infections in Lagos State. Understanding the epidemiology of recency among newly diagnosed individuals at the LUTH APIN Clinic will support the identification of populations at heightened risk, facilitate prompt interventions, and optimize resource use. This study, therefore, aimed to characterize health indices among newly diagnosed HIV-positive patients at the Lagos University Teaching Hospital HIV Clinic based on their recent or long-term infection status, and to examine the relationship between socio-demographic factors and infection patterns. This was a descriptive cross-sectional study aimed at characterizing HIV infections among newly diagnosed clients accessing care at the Lagos University Teaching Hospital (LUTH) APIN Clinic, with a focus on characterizing health indices between recent and long-term infections and to examine the relationship between socio-demographic factors and infection pattern. LUTH is a tertiary health facility with a 761-bed capacity, serving a diverse population and hosting an APIN-supported HIV clinic. It is the largest healthcare facility in Lagos State, Nigeria, receiving numerous referrals and offering comprehensive HIV care and treatment services [19–21]. The study population comprised newly diagnosed HIV-positive clients aged 15 years and above, accessing care at LUTH APIN Clinic over a 3-month period: April - June 2024. The inclusion criteria for this study comprised clients who tested HIV positive via HIV Testing Services (HTS) using the Determine test kit, with no prior history or documentation of antiretroviral therapy (ART) use. Eligible participants were those who did not opt out of recency testing and were aged 15 years and above, in accordance with the national HIV testing guidelines. Clients were excluded from the study if they were diagnosed with AIDS at presentation, WHO Clinical Stage 3 or 4 in a newly diagnosed PLHIV, or reported any previous ART use during the HTS visit. The Asante HIV-1 Rapid Recency Assay was incorporated alongside the Unigold test within a parallel testing algorithm, as shown in Fig. 1 below. The testing process commenced with the Determine HIV test as the initial screening tool (those referred from spoke sites for confirmation and further management skipped the Determine testing stage). Individuals with reactive results subsequently underwent confirmatory testing using both the Unigold and Asante test kits. The Asante HIV-1 Rapid Recency Assay displays three distinct lines: a control line, a positive verification line, and a long-term infection line. Interpretation of these lines allowed differentiation between recent and long-term HIV infections, thereby supporting clinical decision-making, epidemiological surveillance, and targeted prevention interventions. If only the control line and the positive verification line were present, the infection was classified as recent; however, if the control line, positive verification line, and a third line were present, the infection was classified as long-term. During the Rapid Test for Recent Infection (RTRI) stage, all results were reported irrespective of viral load testing; viral load assessment was subsequently applied within the Recent Infection Testing Algorithm (RITA) to confirm recent infections. A recent RTRI result indicated that the individual was likely infected within the preceding year and could be confirmed with viral load testing, whereas a long-term RTRI result indicated infection likely acquired more than one year prior. Long-term results did not require additional confirmatory testing [6–9]. In accordance with the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) guidance for the HIV Testing Services – Recent Infection (HTS_RECENT) indicator, all Rapid Test for Recent Infection (RTRI) results are reported at the point of testing, irrespective of viral load availability. Although viral load testing forms part of the more comprehensive Recent Infection Testing Algorithm (RITA) and improves classification accuracy, programmes are not required to delay reporting RTRI results while awaiting viral load confirmation. Consequently, recency results are reported immediately to support real-time or near–real-time surveillance, particularly in settings where viral load testing capacity may be limited [22]. This yielded a minimum sample size of 138.3, which was adjusted to 152 to account for a 10% non-response rate. However, a total of 456 newly diagnosed clients were ultimately recruited during the study period. The Lagos University Teaching Hospital (LUTH) APIN Clinic operates from Monday to Friday and attends to an average of 20 newly diagnosed clients per day, largely due to frequent referrals from private health facilities. This results in approximately 80 new clients weekly and about 320 monthly. Over the three-month data collection period, an estimated 960 newly diagnosed clients were expected to present at the clinic. A systematic sampling technique was employed for respondent recruitment. The sampling interval (K) was determined using the formula K = N/n, where N represents the estimated number of eligible clients over the three months (960) and n is the minimum sample size (456). Thus, K = 960/456 ≈ 2.1, and a sampling interval of 2 was adopted. The starting point was determined by simple random sampling using a coin toss to select the first eligible participant, after which every second client was recruited until the target sample size was reached. Data collection was carried out using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire adapted for this study [24]. The instrument comprised three sections: Section A captured socio-demographic characteristics; Section B recorded recency test results; and Section C assessed health indices, including HIV clinical staging and CD4 + cell counts. Two trained research assistants, each with a Bachelor of Science degree, assisted with the data collection process following a one-week training focused on research ethics, study objectives, and questionnaire administration. To ensure the tool’s reliability and clarity, a pretest was conducted with 46 clients - representing 10% of the minimum sample size - at the HIV clinic in Lagos State University Teaching Hospital (LASUTH), a facility with similar characteristics to the study site. The questionnaire was refined based on feedback from this exercise. HIV testing adhered to the national algorithm, employing Determine and Unigold test kits, with Stat-Pak used as a tiebreaker where necessary. Clients who tested positive were subsequently assessed for recency of infection using the Asante HIV-1 Rapid Recency Assay, with preliminary classifications as either ‘Recent’ or ‘Long-Term’ infections. For those initially identified as recent, viral load samples were collected to complete the Recent Infection Testing Algorithm (RITA); however, results were not available during the study timeframe. This study was not a clinical trial; therefore, trial registration was not applicable. Data were entered and cleaned using Microsoft Excel and analyzed with SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to summarize findings. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used to explore associations between categorical variables. Multinomial logistic regression was employed to identify independent predictors of recent HIV infection. Variables with p ≤ 0.20 in bivariate analysis and those with known epidemiological relevance were included in the model. Recency status (recent vs. long-term) served as the dependent variable. Dummy coding was applied with appropriate reference categories. Predictors entered included socio-demographic characteristics, sexual behaviors, substance use, WHO clinical stage, and CD4 count (point of care test for immune status using a VISITEC test kit). Adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were generated. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Clients who tested positive were subsequently assessed for recency of infection using the Asante HIV-1 Rapid Recency Assay, with preliminary classifications as either ‘Recent’ or ‘Long-Term’ infections. For those initially identified as recent, viral load samples were collected to complete the Recent Infection Testing Algorithm (RITA); however, results were not available during the study timeframe. This study was not a clinical trial; therefore, trial registration was not applicable. Data were entered and cleaned using Microsoft Excel and analyzed with SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to summarize findings. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used to explore associations between categorical variables. Multinomial logistic regression was employed to identify independent predictors of recent HIV infection. Variables with p ≤ 0.20 in bivariate analysis and those with known epidemiological relevance were included in the model. Recency status (recent vs. long-term) served as the dependent variable. Dummy coding was applied with appropriate reference categories. Predictors entered included socio-demographic characteristics, sexual behaviors, substance use, WHO clinical stage, and CD4 count (point of care test for immune status using a VISITEC test kit). Adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were generated. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. This study aimed to characterize health indices among newly diagnosed HIV-positive patients at the Lagos University Teaching Hospital HIV Clinic based on their Recency test result of either being ‘recent’ or ‘long-term’ infection status, and to examine the relationship between socio-demographic factors and infection patterns, offering detailed insights into socio-demographic patterns, behavioral risk factors, clinical indicators, and the determinants of recent versus long-term HIV infection. The findings contribute significantly to the understanding of HIV transmission dynamics within this population and have implications for designing targeted interventions. The mean age of respondents indicates that HIV remains a significant burden among adults in their economically productive years. Although females constituted a larger proportion of participants, sex was not significantly associated with recent infection, suggesting that both men and women continue to acquire HIV at comparable rates within this setting. However, age demonstrated a strong and consistent relationship with recency: the proportion of recent infections declined markedly with increasing age. Young people under 20 years had the highest proportion of recent infections, aligning with national and regional evidence that adolescents and young adults remain disproportionately affected by new HIV acquisitions. This highlights the urgent need for intensified youth-focused prevention and early detection interventions, including comprehensive sexuality education, youth-friendly services, and strategies addressing structural vulnerabilities [23, 25–28]. Marital status was also significantly associated with recency. Single respondents had a higher proportion of recent infections compared with those who were married or cohabiting. This finding is consistent with studies showing increased HIV vulnerability among unmarried individuals due to factors such as partner change, transactional sex, and inconsistent condom use [29, 30]. Ethnically, most respondents were Yoruba, reflecting the geographic demographics of Lagos State and the clinic’s catchment population. Regarding religion, most participants identified as Christian, a factor potentially influencing health-seeking behaviors and adherence to treatment. Similarly, education level played a significant protective role. Individuals with post-secondary education were substantially less likely to have a recent infection, whereas those with no formal education or only secondary education had the highest burden. This gradient suggests that improved literacy and access to information may enhance risk perception, promote safer sexual practices, and facilitate earlier testing [23, 29]. Employment status and occupation type were not associated with recency, indicating that socioeconomic characteristics alone may not predict recent HIV infection in this population. However, the occupation pattern showing higher proportions of recent infections among unskilled workers and professionals suggests that socioeconomic extremes might influence risk differently, possibly through limited access to information or higher mobility and social exposure. Sexual and behavioral factors demonstrated strong associations with recent infection. Same-gender and bisexual preferences were linked to significantly higher proportions of recent infections compared to opposite-gender preference. This finding reflects the increased biological and structural risks affecting key populations such as men who have sex with men (MSM), who often face stigma, discrimination, and limited access to tailored prevention services. The number of sexual partners also showed a strong relationship with recent HIV infection, with individuals reporting multiple partners being more likely to have a recent infection. This aligns with well-established evidence on the role of partner concurrency and network factors in accelerating HIV transmission [3–5]. Alcohol use and injecting drug use were both significantly associated with recent infection. Alcohol consumption may impair judgment and reduce condom use, while injecting drug use presents a direct transmission route, consistent with global trends showing elevated HIV incidence among people who inject drugs (PWID). Although blood transfusion was not statistically associated with recency, the trend toward higher recent infections among those transfused within the previous year warrants further investigation to rule out residual risks in blood safety practices [1–3]. Clinical characteristics showed the strongest associations with recency. WHO clinical stage and CD4 count at diagnosis were powerful predictors, with individuals in Stage 1 and those with CD4 ≥ 200 cells/mm³ significantly more likely to have a recent infection. This is expected, as recent HIV infection is typically characterized by minimal clinical symptoms and preserved immune function, whereas chronic infection is associated with progressive immunosuppression. The very high proportion of recent infection among those in Stage 1 further validates the reliability of the recency assay in distinguishing new from longstanding infections. Multivariate analysis reinforced these findings: younger age, lower education, higher CD4 count, and earlier WHO stage were strong independent predictors of recent HIV infection. Notably, patients with CD4 ≥ 200 cells/mm³ were ten times more likely to have a recent infection, while those in Stage 2 had significantly lower odds than those in Stage 1, reflecting the expected trajectory of HIV disease progression [7]. Geographic analysis revealed heterogeneity in HIV infection rates across Lagos LGAs. One major limitation of this study was the reliance on self-reported data, which may have introduced recall and social desirability bias. To mitigate this, interviewers were trained to build rapport and create a trusting, non-judgmental environment that encouraged honest responses. In addition, viral load results for participants identified with recent infections were not available during the study period, limiting the ability to apply the full Recent Infection Testing Algorithm (RITA) classification. Future longitudinal studies incorporating viral load and biomarker data are recommended to enhance the accuracy and robustness of recency testing outcomes. Finally, the findings of this study may have limited generalizability beyond the study setting, as data were obtained from a single tertiary hospital in urban Lagos and may not reflect HIV infection patterns in rural or community-based populations. Based on the study findings, several multi-level recommendations are proposed to strengthen HIV prevention, diagnosis, and care. At the community level, there is a need for sustained awareness campaigns to promote HIV testing, combat stigma, and encourage early diagnosis, particularly among adolescents, young adults, and women. Community-led initiatives should also promote safer sexual practices and support for people living with HIV/AIDS. At the local government level, targeted interventions should be implemented in high-burden areas including mobile HIV testing, support groups, and integration of HIV services into primary healthcare. The state government should invest in healthcare worker training on recency testing and ensure that policies prioritize early diagnosis and treatment, while strengthening surveillance systems to monitor infection trends. At the federal level, there is a need to scale up the implementation of the Recent Infection Testing Algorithm (RITA), improve access to viral load testing, and allocate adequate funding for national HIV programmes. Policy reforms should address the structural and socio-economic determinants of HIV, promote universal health coverage, and ensure equitable access to HIV prevention and treatment services across all population groups.
