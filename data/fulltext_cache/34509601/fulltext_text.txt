Microbial antagonism, a crucial competitive strategy, is ubiquitous within natural microbial communities [1]. It refers to the process where one microorganism inhibits or kills others through secreted metabolites or environmental modulation, serving as a core mechanism for maintaining community dynamic equilibrium with significant ecological implications [2, 3]. This substance can suppress pathogen proliferation. They also safeguard microbial diversity and stabilize decomposer communities and biogeochemical cycling [4]. In agricultural biocontrol, antagonistic yeasts offer alternatives to chemical pesticides, mitigating residues and resistance risks [5]. In medicine, antagonistic products like antibiotics remain pivotal anti-infective agents, with recent expansions into skin microbiota modulation [6, 7]. While antagonism in conventional environments is well-studied, its mechanisms in extreme settings present unique challenges and distinctive features [8]. Hypersaline environments widely distributed on the planet (e.g., salt pans, salt lakes and saline-alkali soils; NaCl > 15%) impose severe osmotic stress, permitting survival only for halophilic/halotolerant bacteria, archaea and a few highly adapted eukaryotic species [9]. Competition for limited resources and niches drives the evolution of specialized antagonistic strategies [10]. Critically, hypersaline systems represent rare ecosystems where members of all three domains of life—Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya—coexist and interact [11]. This three-domain coexistence positions them as ideal natural models for investigating inter-domain microbial interactions being in the same ecological niche, particularly antagonism. Current research on hypersaline antagonism primarily focuses on intra-domain interactions (bacteria vs. bacteria, or archaea vs. archaea). Haloarchaea, the key players in these habitats, employ unique antimicrobial strategies. For instance, halocins (H1, H2, H3, H4, H5, H6/H7, A4, C8, G1, R1, S8, KPS1, Sech7a, SH10, HA1, HA3 and H17), a class of antimicrobial peptides secreted by archaea, disrupt target cell membrane integrity via pore formation or nuclease activity [12]. Additionally, Haloferax mediterranei, a model organism in domain archaea, secretes proteases like HlyR4, which were involved in the formation of antagonistic substances [13]. Similarly, some of these halophilic or salt-tolerant bacteria living in hypersaline environments may also produce potent antagonistic agents [14]. Three decades ago, an indole-derived natural product, vibrindole A, was isolated from the toxic mucus of the boxfish Ostracion cubicus showing a clear inhibition effect to Staphylococcus aureus and S. albicans [15]. Since then, numerous substances with antibacterial activities have been continuously discovered [14]. Moreover, the semi-synthetic derivatives of the strain Pseudonocardia endophytica isolated from the sediment of Indian mangroves exhibit broad-spectrum inhibitory effects [16]. Subsequently, the 7-deoxy-8-O-methyltetrangomycin from actinomycetes Nocardiopsis sp. HR-4 derived from an Algerian salt lake can effectively inhibit the growth of MRSA (Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus) [17]. Recently, antimicrobial peptides designed based on metagenomic data from hypersaline environments have demonstrated significant inhibitory activity against multidrug-resistant pathogens, with neither cytotoxicity nor hemolytic activity observed [18]. However, the phenomenon of halophilic or salt-tolerant bacteria inhibiting haloarchaea remains poorly documented. In 2013, researchers discovered that the extremely halophilic bacterium Salicola sp. s3-1 inhibited the growth of the haloarchaeon Halorubrum sp. s1-2, though the active compound(s) remain unidentified to date [19]. Additionally, Fadoua Ghanmi and colleagues demonstrated that Pseudomonas halophila ES1D1 and Salicola sp. ES1R9, isolated from a solar saltern, exhibited a growth inhibition towards the Halorubrum tebenquichense SS1R14 occupying the same ecological niche [20]. Although there are some reports on the antagonistic effect of halophilic bacteria on halophilic archaea, substances produced by bacteria that can cross-domain inhibit archaea have not yet been identified. In this study, we isolated a halophilic bacterium from saline-alkali soil exhibiting potent cross-domain inhibition against halophilic archaea and bacteria, and we aimed to explore the specific substances responsible for executing this cross-domain inhibitory effect. Initially, we optimized the culture medium and cultivation condition for growth to enhance the production of antimicrobial substances. Subsequently, we effectively extracted these antimicrobial substances from supernatants using ethyl acetate extraction followed by a further purification with a silica gel column. For the structural identification of these antimicrobial substances, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was adopted. In addition, whole-genome sequencing of the antimicrobial substance-producing-strain was performed to facilitate comparative genomics analysis for elucidating the biosynthetic gene clusters and pathway corresponding to antimicrobial metabolite synthesis. This work provides unprecedented insights into bacteria-archaea antagonism in hypersaline environments, as well as potential applications in both biocontrol and pharmaceutical industry. Strain AD133 was isolated from a saline-alkali soil sample (37.80583 N, 107.75920E) collected from Dingbian county, Shaanxi province, China (Fig. S1) using the neutral oligotrophic haloarchaeal growth medium (NOM) [21] which containing (per liter) 0.05 g yeast extract, 0.25 g fish peptone, 1 g sodium pyruvate, 5.4 g KCl, 0.29 g CaCl2, 0.27 g NH4Cl, 2 g MgSO4·7H2O, 184 g NaCl and 2 mL of phosphate buffer (prepared by mixing equal volumes of 0.5 M KH2PO₄ and 0.5 M K2HPO4). The pH of the medium was adjusted to 7.5 using 1 M NaOH. The modified NOM (MNOM) consisted of (per liter) 5 g yeast extract, 4.5 g fish peptone, 3 g sodium pyruvate, 80 g NaCl, 4 g KCl, 0.35 g CaCl2, 0.25 g NH4Cl, 1 g MgSO4·7H2O and 2 mL of phosphate buffer. The optimized MNOM medium optimized by subsequent experiments, significantly increases cell biomass and shortens the cultivation period. Luria-Bertani Broth (LB) [22] and Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) [23] were used to cultivate other Bacteria and plant pathogenic fungi, respectively. To prepare solid media, agar powder was added at a ratio of 2.0% (wt/vol) prior to autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min. After inoculation, cells were cultivated at 37 °C or with shaking at 180 r·min− 1 for liquid medium. The saline-alkali soil sample underwent a 10-fold serial dilution (10− 1 to 10− 3) with sterile 20% NaCl (w/v) and then was spread onto NOM solid medium in 200 µL for each dilution according to the procedure described by Tu et al. [24]. Colonies with distinct morphologies were purified through successive streaking [25]. Archaeal and bacterial 16 S rRNA genes were amplified using primers F8/R1462 [26] and 27 F/1492R [27], respectively, under standard PCR procedure. DNA sequencing was performed by a biotechnology company (General Biol, Chuzhou, China) after DNA electrophoresis and gel extraction using AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen, Union City, USA). The taxonomic position was determined by homology search against the public nucleotide database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Strain AD133 has been deposited in the Marine Culture Collection of China (MCCC) under the number of MCCC 1K09297. Multiple sequence alignment of the 16 S rRNA gene sequences was performed using ClustalW with default parameters, and the resulting alignments were manually corrected. Phylogenetic reconstruction based on the 16 S rRNA genes was constructed using MEGA X [28] with maximum likelihood (ML), maximum parsimony (MP), and neighbor-joining (NJ) algorithms, with Halorhodospira halophila DSM 224ᵀ as the outgroup. Node support was assessed via 1,000 bootstrap replicates. To determine the cell morphology of strain AD133, a single colony was picked and streaked onto the NOM supplemented with skim-milk. Colony morphology and proteolytic activity were directly recorded after cultivation. Gram staining was performed according to the method described by Dussault [29], with subsequent cell size and morphology observations using optical microscopy (Olympus CX23, Tokyo, Japan). Cell morphology was also checked by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (HITACHI UHR FE-SEM SU8000, Tokyo, Japan) with a standard sample preparation procedure (ZHONG KE BAI CE, Beijing, China). In order to display the antimicrobial activity, the indicating plate was made by cultivating indicator strains Halorubrum sp. LN72 (haloarchaeon, hereinafter referred to as LN72) and Halobacillus sp. AD240 (bacterium, hereinafter referred to as AD240) to logarithmic phase (OD600≈0.6) in NOM, respectively. Then, 10 mL of cell suspension was poured into 100 mL corresponding medium warmed at 55 °C after autoclaving. Subsequently, indicating plates were made by adding nearly 20 mL above mixture for each Petri-dishes. Agar diffusion assay was performed by punching wells (Ø 6 mm) agar plates to accommodate 200 µL testing samples with potential inhibitory activity [30]. Oxford cup assay was conducted according to the method described by Humphries et al. [31]. Dual culture assay was constructed to test the inhibitory activity against the plant pathogenic fungi [32]. The purified pyoluteorin was dissolved in methanol to a concentration of 1 mg/mL. An aliquot of 200 µL of this solution, corresponding to 200 µg of the compound, was added per well (agar well or Oxford cup). To probe the antimicrobial spectrum of strain AD133, the strain was screened against 12 haloarchaeal and 8 bacterial genera (Table 1) as well as a plant pathogenic bacterium (Dickeya zeae WH1) and two plant pathogenic fungi (Pyricularia oryzae and Valsa spp.). All these strains for screening antibacterial spectrum were used to make the indicator plates. A 20-µL cell suspension of strain AD133 (OD600≈0.6) was inoculated onto these indicator plates. Inhibition activity was recorded, and the diameter of the inhibition zone was measured accordingly. Variables including pH (5.0–9.5 in 0.5-unit increments), NaCl (0–160 g·L− 1 in 10 g·L− 1 increments), temperature (25, 30, 37, 42 °C), sodium pyruvate (2–26 g·L− 1 in 2 g·L− 1 increments) were independently adjusted. The growth curve was plotted after optimization compared with the original medium and condition. Response surface optimization was conducted by selecting yeast extract, fish peptone, NH4Cl and MgSO4 as four pivotal influential factors using OD600 as the response [33]. All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, each consisting of three technical replicates. Inhibition zone diameters were measured using a vernier caliper, and differences between two groups were compared using Student’s t-test. For growth curve analysis, OD₆₀₀ values were measured and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and growth curves were plotted accordingly. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Tangential flow filtration (TFF): The clarified bacterial supernatant was concentrated using a Vivaflow 50 TFF module (Sartorius, Germany; Molecular Weight Cut-off (MWCO): 10 kDa) driven by a LongerPump BT100-2 J peristaltic pump (Baoding Longer Precision Pump Co., China) equipped with a silicone pump tubing. The system was operated at a recirculation flow rate of 300 mL/min (shear rate ≈ 4,300 s⁻¹) under a transmembrane pressure (TMP) of 1.2 psi. A 10-fold volume reduction was achieved within 45 min for 500 mL initial supernatant. Ammonium sulfate precipitation: Solid ammonium sulfate was gradually added to cell-free supernatant (CFS) under continuous stirring (4 °C) to achieve stepwise saturation of 30% (removing contaminating proteins) and 70–80% (targeting antimicrobial peptides). After each addition, the mixture was stirred for 6 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C for complete precipitation. Precipitate was collected by centrifugation (12,000×g, 30 min, 4 °C), and the supernatant was discarded. Solid plate concentration method: The AD133 bacterial suspension was uniformly spread on the surface of a solid medium and incubated at 37 °C for 7 days. The plate surface was then rinsed with liquid medium to completely remove adherent bacteria. The solid medium was cut into approximately 5 × 5 mm pieces, transferred into sterile centrifuge tubes, and centrifuged at 5,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C to facilitate liquid extraction from the agar block. Equal volumes of the collected liquid and the original culture supernatant were subjected to an agar well diffusion assay to compare differences in antibacterial activity. Organic solvent extraction: To harvest the antimicrobial substance(s) from the supernatants of AD133, the strain was pre-cultured in optimized NOM to prepare seed culture, and the seed culture was inoculated (1% v/v) into 10 L fresh medium and fermented under identical conditions for 96 h. Cell suspension was centrifuged (5,000 ×g, 20 min) after fermentation, and the resulting supernatant was filtered through a microfiltration membrane (Ø 0.22 μm). The filtrate underwent triple extractions (1:1 v/v, 2 h oscillation per extraction) with ethyl acetate, or n-butanol, or petroleum ether. Combined organic phases were rotary-evaporated (50 °C, reduced pressure) to dryness. The residues were dissolved in HPLC-grade methanol to yield crude antibacterial extracts (Fig. 1) [34]. For fraction analysis, a secondary extraction procedure was performed. 50 mL crude extract was diluted 1:1 with deionized water. This diluted solution was used to replace the bacterial supernatant in the primary extraction, and the steps from “c” to “f” in Fig. 1 were repeated for re-extraction with ethyl acetate. Three fractions, diluted crude extract (pre-extraction), secondary ethyl acetate extract, residual aqueous phase (post-extraction), were prepared for bioactivity assessment. Each fraction was diluted 1:8 (v/v) in methanol and analyzed by UV-Vis spectroscopy (Shimadzu UV-1800, Kyoto, Japan). Full-wavelength scans (200–800 nm) employed a 1-cm quartz cuvette with methanol as reference, using parameters: medium scan speed, 2 nm bandwidth, 1 nm data interval [35]. To test the thermal stability, crude extract aliquots were heated at 60–120 °C (10 °C intervals) for 20 min. After cooling, antibacterial activity was assessed against an untreated control. Extract was also stored at 37, 25, 4, and − 20 °C for 7, 30, and 60 days prior to activity detection. In order to figure out the nature of the antibacterial substances, extract was treated with proteinase K (37 °C, 30 min) (Transgen, Beijing, China), with activity compared pre- and post-treatment. To initially determine its molecular weight, extract was dialyzed with a dialysis bag (Solarbio, Beijing, China) with MWCO of 12.0–14.4 kDa against deionized water with hourly water changes. After 12 h, dialysate was collected and activity compared to the original extract. Ultrafiltration was also performed sequentially using 3, 10, and 30 kDa spin columns (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Antibacterial activity of retentates and filtrates was analyzed at each cutoff. To determine the substance(s) with antimicrobial activity from the supernatant of strain AD133, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra was adopted. NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker ASCEND AVIIIHD600 spectrometer (600 MHz) (Germany) using tetramethylsilane (TMS) as internal standard. The mass spectrometry (MS) analysis was performed using an Agilent 6546 ultra-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q/TOF-MS) system equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source, the mobile phase was composed of 50% water and 50% acetonitrile, the flow rate was set as 0.2 mL/min, and the sample size was 2 µL. All solvents (EtOAc, CH2Cl2, MeOH) were analytical grade. The crude extract (0.942 g after EtOAc extraction and solvent removal) was fractionated by silica gel column chromatography. Elution with CH2Cl2 followed by CH2Cl2/MeOH (20:1 → 10:1, v/v) yielded three fractions (Fr1–Fr3). Fr1 (68 mg) was further purified by Sephadex LH-20 column (GE Healthcare, Fairfield, USA) chromatography (MeOH eluent) with controlled flow rate and fraction collection. Elution profiles were monitored by TLC; like fractions were pooled to afford compound 1 (2.1 mg), which was immediately characterized by NMR [36]. Genomic DNA of strain AD133 was extracted using TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) according to manufacturer’s instruction. DNA quality was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis before sequencing on Illumina HiSeq 4000 (Biomarker, Beijing, China). Raw reads were quality-controlled with the fastp v0.24.0 [37] and assembled using the SPAdes v3.15.5 [38]. Contigs < 500 bp were filtered using a Python script (https://www.python.org/). Assembly quality was assessed with the CheckM2 v1.2.4 [39], and the final genome was deposited in GenBank under accession number GCA_051195215.1. Subsequently, the genomes of AD133 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa M18 (reference pyoluteorin producer) were annotated with the Prokka v1.14.6 [40]. Top hits were analyzed for genomic location and gene size. Synteny with strain M18’s plt cluster was visualized through multiple sequences alignment [41]. Strain AD133 was harvested at late-log phase (OD600≈0.6) by centrifugation (5,000 ×g, 10 min, 4 °C). Cell pellets were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus kit (Takara, Japan) according to the instruction followed by DNase I (Ambion, Austin, USA) treatment to eliminate genomic DNA [42]. RNA integrity was confirmed by the NanoDrop 2000/2000c (USA) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Santa Clara, USA). Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was depleted using Ribo-Zero (Illumina), and enriched mRNA was fragmented (200–300 bp). First-strand cDNA was synthesized with SMARTer PCR cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Japan), followed by Illumina TruSeq library preparation (end-repair, adapter ligation, 12–15 cycle PCR amplification). Purified libraries underwent paired-end sequencing (PE150) on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (≥ 6 Gb/sample). Raw reads were quality-filtered using the fastp v0.24.0 to remove adapters and low-quality reads, yielding clean reads. The clean reads were then aligned to the reference genome of Arhodomonas sp. AD133 using HISAT2 v2.2.1 [43]. Subsequently, featureCounts v2.0.1 was employed to quantify the number of reads mapped to each gene [44]. Gene expression levels were normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Biosynthetic pathways of pyoluteorin originated from these testing carbon sources were predicted through combining of metabolic pathway matching against the KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) [45] and homology search against the encoding genome of AD133. The AD133 genome was interrogated for enzymes involved in carbon metabolism. A 10-fold volume reduction was achieved within 45 min for 500 mL initial supernatant. Ammonium sulfate precipitation: Solid ammonium sulfate was gradually added to cell-free supernatant (CFS) under continuous stirring (4 °C) to achieve stepwise saturation of 30% (removing contaminating proteins) and 70–80% (targeting antimicrobial peptides). After each addition, the mixture was stirred for 6 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C for complete precipitation. Precipitate was collected by centrifugation (12,000×g, 30 min, 4 °C), and the supernatant was discarded. Solid plate concentration method: The AD133 bacterial suspension was uniformly spread on the surface of a solid medium and incubated at 37 °C for 7 days. The plate surface was then rinsed with liquid medium to completely remove adherent bacteria. The solid medium was cut into approximately 5 × 5 mm pieces, transferred into sterile centrifuge tubes, and centrifuged at 5,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C to facilitate liquid extraction from the agar block. Equal volumes of the collected liquid and the original culture supernatant were subjected to an agar well diffusion assay to compare differences in antibacterial activity. Organic solvent extraction: To harvest the antimicrobial substance(s) from the supernatants of AD133, the strain was pre-cultured in optimized NOM to prepare seed culture, and the seed culture was inoculated (1% v/v) into 10 L fresh medium and fermented under identical conditions for 96 h. The filtrate underwent triple extractions (1:1 v/v, 2 h oscillation per extraction) with ethyl acetate, or n-butanol, or petroleum ether. Combined organic phases were rotary-evaporated (50 °C, reduced pressure) to dryness. The residues were dissolved in HPLC-grade methanol to yield crude antibacterial extracts (Fig. 1) [34]. We actively explored and attempted various methods for crude extraction of antibacterial substances, including ammonium sulfate precipitation, tangential flow concentration of the supernatant, and concentration of substances in solid culture media. However, none of these methods effectively enriched the corresponding antibacterial active substances, and the concentrated solutions obtained exhibited little to no antibacterial activity (Fig. S4). Ultimately, we found that the organic solvent extraction method could effectively obtain a crude extract with strong antibacterial activity. Three organic solvents with significantly different polarities (petroleum ether, ethyl acetate, and n-butanol) were used to extract the antibacterial substances. The extraction efficiency of the active substances by the non-polar solvent petroleum-ether was significantly limited, and no inhibition zone was observed in the antibacterial experiment. The medium-polarity solvent ethyl acetate showed the optimal extraction performance. It produced the largest inhibition zone diameter (25 mm ± 0.3 mm) with the best edge clarity, indicating the strongest antibacterial activity. Although the strong-polarity solvent n-butanol could produce an inhibition zone, its antibacterial effect (16 ± 0.5 mm) was significantly weaker than that of ethyl acetate under the same experimental conditions (Fig. 5a). These results indicate that medium-polarity solvents are more favorable for the extraction of antibacterial substances from AD133. It appears as a colorless oil. The 1H-NMR (600 MHz, Methanol-d4) data shows δH values as follows: 7.07 (1H, t, J = 8.2 Hz), 6.54 (1H, s), 6.36 (2 H, d, J = 8.2 Hz). The δH 7.07 (t, J = 8.2 Hz) corresponds to H-4′ on the resorcinol ring, coupling with two adjacent protons (H-3′ and H-5′). The δH 6.36 (d, J = 8.2 Hz) represents H-3′ and H-5′ of the resorcinol ring, coupling with H-4′. The δH 6.54 (s) corresponds to H-3 on the pyrrole ring. The 13C-NMR (150 MHz, Methanol-d4) data revealed eleven resonances corresponding to a carbonyl group (δC 185.6), substituted benzene (δC 157.9, 132.7, 115.2, 108.0) and pyrrole ring (δC 132.2, 121.6, 119.1, 111.8). The δC 157.9 corresponds to the carbon atoms (C-2′ and 6′) that are substituted by hydroxyl groups on the aromatic ring. The δC 132.7 corresponds to C-4′, while 108.0 corresponds to C-3′ and 5′. The δC 115.2 corresponds to C-1′ which is connected to the carbonyl group. Additionally, the δC values of 132.2, 119.1, 111.8 and 121.6 correspond to C-2, 3, 4 and 5, respectively. The NMR data is consistent with the literature, confirming that the compound is pyoluteorin (Fig. 7d). High-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HR-ESI-MS) analysis revealed that the purified compound exhibited a prominent [M + H]⁺ ion peak with an observed m/z of 271.9878, which is highly consistent with the theoretical m/z of 271.9881 for the molecular formula C₁₁H₇Cl₂NO₃ of pyoluteorin (mass error < 2 ppm), further confirming its identity (Fig. S6). However, high expression of the negative regulator pltZ was also observed, suggesting the existence of a fine-tuned regulatory balance within the cell. The low expression levels of pltN and pltO indicated that they may possess specialized functions or are subject to different regulatory mechanisms (Table S2). Through bioinformatics alignment and analysis, among currently known pyoluteorin-related gene clusters, the highest sequence identity between the AD133 genome and the M18 gene cluster was found to be 72.83%, and the lowest was 15.1% (Table 2). These gene clusters in strain AD133 showed complete collinearity with M18 in the synthesis segment (Fig. 9). The physical lengths of all homologous gene clusters on the genome were highly correlated with that of the M18 gene cluster. Genes related to biosynthesis in the homologous gene clusters maintained the same transcription direction (5′→3′) as M18, while genes related to transmembrane transport exhibited an opposite arrangement pattern (Fig. 9). This bidirectional arrangement pattern may imply that the transport genes have an independent regulatory mechanism. Intriguingly, proteins PltI and PltJ in M18 correspond to the same protein AD133_01951 in AD133 (Table 2) which might be caused by gene redundancy. Notably, among all homologous gene clusters, the regulatory factors pltZ and pltM maintained the same transcription direction as M18, while the pltR regulatory factor showed an inverted complementary arrangement (Fig. 9). This asymmetry in the regulatory network may be related to the environmental response mechanism of secondary metabolite synthesis. The principal carbon metabolic pathways in AD133 were inferred (Fig. 10). ABC transporters mediating the active transmembrane transport of xylose, glucose, fructose, sucrose, and maltose were identified within the AD133 genome. Regarding xylose metabolism, a series of key enzymes were elucidated. Glycerol kinase (GlpK) was implicated in the utilization of extracellular glycerol. Xylose isomerase (XylA) catalyzes the isomerization of xylose to xylulose. Xylulokinase subsequently phosphorylates xylulose to D-xylulose-5-phosphate. Collectively, these enzymes constitute the metabolic pathway directing xylose into the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), ultimately leading to pyruvate formation. Furthermore, xylose catabolism via the Weimberg pathway was also suggested, with key participating enzymes identified. These include xylose-1-dehydrogenase (XylB), which converts xylose to xylonate; xylonate dehydratase (XylD), oxidizing xylonate to 3-keto-oxaloacetate; and a decarboxylase (XylX) involved in subsequent decarboxylation reactions. Enzymes associated with the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway and the Entner-Doudoroff (ED) pathway were also detected. Additionally, enzymes involved in glycerol and acetate utilization were identified. Glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase participates in the further metabolism of glycerol. Acetyl-CoA synthetase (ACSS2 or ACSS1) facilitates acetate utilization by converting it to acetyl-CoA for entry into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Ultimately, through this complex network of metabolic pathways, diverse carbon sources are progressively converted to proline. Proline serves as the biosynthetic precursor for the dichloropyrrole moiety of the biological pesticide pyoluteorin (Fig. 10). We actively explored and attempted various methods for crude extraction of antibacterial substances, including ammonium sulfate precipitation, tangential flow concentration of the supernatant, and concentration of substances in solid culture media. However, none of these methods effectively enriched the corresponding antibacterial active substances, and the concentrated solutions obtained exhibited little to no antibacterial activity (Fig. S4). Ultimately, we found that the organic solvent extraction method could effectively obtain a crude extract with strong antibacterial activity. Three organic solvents with significantly different polarities (petroleum ether, ethyl acetate, and n-butanol) were used to extract the antibacterial substances. The extraction efficiency of the active substances by the non-polar solvent petroleum-ether was significantly limited, and no inhibition zone was observed in the antibacterial experiment. The medium-polarity solvent ethyl acetate showed the optimal extraction performance. It produced the largest inhibition zone diameter (25 mm ± 0.3 mm) with the best edge clarity, indicating the strongest antibacterial activity. Although the strong-polarity solvent n-butanol could produce an inhibition zone, its antibacterial effect (16 ± 0.5 mm) was significantly weaker than that of ethyl acetate under the same experimental conditions (Fig. 5a). These results indicate that medium-polarity solvents are more favorable for the extraction of antibacterial substances from AD133. The δC 115.2 corresponds to C-1′ which is connected to the carbonyl group. Additionally, the δC values of 132.2, 119.1, 111.8 and 121.6 correspond to C-2, 3, 4 and 5, respectively. The scale bar corresponding to the length of the gene is shown on the right In the results of the utilization of 16 readily available carbon sources, strain AD133 showed extremely strong utilization ability for sugars and grew vigorously in glucose, fructose, xylose, maltose, and sucrose. Strain AD133 could also efficiently utilize non-sugar carbon sources, including glycerol, mannitol, and acetate. It could effectively metabolize eight carbon sources, covering three biochemical categories: carbohydrates, organic acids, and alcohols (Fig. S8). The principal carbon metabolic pathways in AD133 were inferred (Fig. 10). ABC transporters mediating the active transmembrane transport of xylose, glucose, fructose, sucrose, and maltose were identified within the AD133 genome. Regarding xylose metabolism, a series of key enzymes were elucidated. Glycerol kinase (GlpK) was implicated in the utilization of extracellular glycerol. Xylose isomerase (XylA) catalyzes the isomerization of xylose to xylulose. Xylulokinase subsequently phosphorylates xylulose to D-xylulose-5-phosphate. Collectively, these enzymes constitute the metabolic pathway directing xylose into the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), ultimately leading to pyruvate formation. Furthermore, xylose catabolism via the Weimberg pathway was also suggested, with key participating enzymes identified. These include xylose-1-dehydrogenase (XylB), which converts xylose to xylonate; xylonate dehydratase (XylD), oxidizing xylonate to 3-keto-oxaloacetate; and a decarboxylase (XylX) involved in subsequent decarboxylation reactions. Enzymes associated with the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway and the Entner-Doudoroff (ED) pathway were also detected. Additionally, enzymes involved in glycerol and acetate utilization were identified. Glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase participates in the further metabolism of glycerol. Acetyl-CoA synthetase (ACSS2 or ACSS1) facilitates acetate utilization by converting it to acetyl-CoA for entry into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Ultimately, through this complex network of metabolic pathways, diverse carbon sources are progressively converted to proline. Proline serves as the biosynthetic precursor for the dichloropyrrole moiety of the biological pesticide pyoluteorin (Fig. 10). Additionally, species Burkholderia pyrrocinia may also conceive the ability of producing pyoluteorin [49]. Although the genome of the strain Streptomyces sp. JCM9888 contains partial genes for pyoluteorin synthesis, there is no evidence proving that it can actually synthesize pyoluteorin [50]. Strain AD133 belonging to the species in genus Arhodomonas (Fig. 2) is a pyoluteorin producing strain, however, the close relatives (A. aquaeolei DSM 8974, A. aquaeolei M3R-1517 and A. sp. KWT) of strain AD133 don’t contain reliable pyoluteorin-encoding gene cluster on their genome. Thus, we propose that the production of pyoluteorin is strain-specific, not species-specific, also founding expands the spectrum of pyoluteorin producing strains. The plant growth-promoting and pyoluteorin producing rhizosphere bacteria showed a broad spectrum of antifungal and antibacterial activities. The P. protegens MP12 exhibited inhibitory effects on Botrytis cinerea, Alternaria alternata, Aspergillus niger, Penicillium expansum and Neofusicoccum parvum [51]. The P. aurantiaca PB-St2 showed significant antibacterial activity against Gram-positive pathogen Bacillus cereus and Gram-negative pathogens P. aeruginosa, Salmonella enterica and Klebsiella oxytoca [52]. The exometabolites of strain AD133 displayed an antimicrobial activity against bacteria (S. aureus ATCC 6538, B. subtilis DSM10, B. cereus ATCC14579 and D. zeae WH1), plant pathogenic fungi (P. oryzae and Valsa spp.) and haloarchaea (Halorubrum sp. LN72) (Fig. 8). It is obvious that strain AD133 shows a cross-domain antimicrobial activity, it can inhibit microorganisms from all three domains (Bacteria, Eukaryotes and Archaea). Haloarchaeal strains, Halobacterium sp. AD33, Haloferax sp. AD503, Halomicrobium sp. AD321, Haloparvum sp. AD34 and Halorubrum sp. AD26, and halotolerant bacterial strains, Alkalibacillus sp. AD475, Fodinibius sp. AD704 and Halobacillus sp. AD240 (Table 1), were isolated from the same niche as the strain AD133 [53]. Hypersaline environments represent an underexploited special habitat harboring vast untapped microbial resources [8]. Archaea and Bacteria, as two phylogenetically distinct domains of life, commonly coexist in various natural and artificial ecosystems, potentially engaging in complex physiological interactions and signaling exchanges [53, 54]. Despite significant differences in their metabolic pathways, cellular structures, and genetic regulatory mechanisms, both domains often occupy overlapping ecological niches in the utilization of key nutrients such as carbon and nitrogen, leading to a dynamic balance in resource competition [55, 56]. Strain AD133 described in this study exhibits the ability to inhibit the growth of both Archaea and Bacteria, a phenomenon scarcely reported in microbial systems. Microorganisms with such antagonistic properties may play crucial roles in regulating ecosystem structure by restraining the overexpansion of dominant species, thereby promoting niche differentiation and functional replacement, and ultimately contributing to the maintenance of microbial community diversity and stability [57]. The production strains of pyoluteorin originate from diverse habitats and belong to distinct species. Their optimal growth conditions and production parameters vary accordingly. Strain AD133 belonging to genus Arhodomonas is the first halotolerant bacterium which can synthesize pyoluteorin. It was isolated from saline-alkali soli using NOM medium. However, the biomass of the cells of strain AD133 was very low (OD600≈0.2–0.3) without optimization on cultivation conditions and culture medium components (Fig. 4). Through optimizing, the biomass of strain AD133 has been significantly increased, cell density (OD600) rising from 0.2 to 0.3 to 1.4–1.5 (Fig. 4), which has significantly enhanced production efficiency and saved time costs. In this study, we attempted to crudely extract an unknown exoproducts of strain AD133 exhibiting antimicrobial activity using various methods. To more effectively isolate the bioactive component, it is essential to determine the type of exometabolites with antimicrobial activity, as different types of substances require tailored separation strategies. Our results demonstrated that the antibacterial activity of this component remained stable even after treatment with high temperature and proteinase K, preliminarily ruling out the possibility that it is a proteinaceous substance (Fig. 6a, c) [58, 59]. Furthermore, the bioactive component was able to pass through ultrafiltration membranes with a molecular weight cutoff of 3 kDa and dialysis membranes with a cutoff of 12 kDa, indicating a relatively low molecular weight, which further supports the inference that it is a non-proteinaceous compound (Fig. 6b, d) [60]. Notably, the substance retained strong antibacterial activity even after prolonged storage at room temperature, suggesting favorable physicochemical stability as a biological pesticide (Fig. 6e). In combination with our preliminary experiments, in which ammonium sulfate precipitation effectively removed proteins but simultaneously led to the loss of antibacterial activity (Fig. S4d) [61], we reasonably hypothesize that the active antibacterial component is most likely a bioactive small-molecule compound. This finding provides a crucial direction for subsequent strategies aimed at its extraction and characterization. The effectiveness of organic solvents in extracting the antibacterial substances from AD133 suggests that certain small molecular compounds are likely responsible for the observed antimicrobial activity (Fig. 5). As an efficient technique for the extraction of small molecules, organic solvent extraction has been widely applied in the isolation and purification of natural products [62]. This method not only enables effective enrichment of target exometabolites, but also demonstrates strong adaptability in complex systems with high salt and high ion concentration, without being significantly affected by ionic interference [63]. Moreover, the solvent extraction process itself facilitates a transition from a high-salt to a low-salt environment, which is beneficial for subsequent functional studies conducted under physiological or standard experimental conditions. The antibacterial activity of the exometabolites can thus be evaluated in a low-salt environment, offering a more accurate reflection of their biological activity in practical application scenarios. This characteristic is of great significance for assessing the potential of natural products in the fields of pharmaceutical development and agricultural applications. The pyoluteorin in the exometabolites of strain AD133 was determined NMR analysis after acetate ethyl ester extraction and thin-layer chromatography separation (Figs. 5 and 7). It is intriguing that microorganisms originating from hypersaline environment and those from conventional habitats are both capable of synthesizing the same pyoluteorin, they exhibit notable differences at the level of the corresponding gene clusters [41, 64]. Pyoluteorin biosynthetic gene cluster has been identified in P. fluorescens Pf-5 [46], and was also found in other Pseudomonas species such as P. aeruginosa M18 [65]. Compared to the pyoluteorin synthesis gene cluster in model P. aeruginosa strain M18 through homology search, a corresponding pyoluteorin biosynthetic gene cluster consisting of eight genes (pltL and pltA-G) was obtained on the genome of AD133 (Table 2). Furthermore, it has been supported by the transcriptome data (Table S2). Interestingly, no pyoluteorin-related genes were identified in the available genomes of the genus Arhodomonas (Table S3). The pyoluteorin synthesis gene cluster in strains M18 and AD133 shared identical gene synteny (Fig. 9). Notably, although microorganisms from distinct ecological niches ultimately produce the same secondary metabolite, their underlying genetic foundations may be fundamentally different. However, other Plt related genes, including pyoluteorin transport genes pltH, pltI, pltJ, pltK, pltN and pltO which are presumably involved in transcription, as well as regulatory genes such as pltM, pltR, and pltZ [65, 66], displayed significant sequence divergence or structural variation (Fig. 9; Table 2). Through protein homology search, it was revealed that pyoluteorin transport-related proteins PltJ and PltK from strain M18 correspond to a single protein, AD133_01951, in the genome-encoded proteins of strain AD133 (Table 2). These differences may reflect distinct evolutionary pressures or events of horizontal gene transfer during adaptation. Such genetic diversity may further lead to variations in the biosynthetic mechanisms of pyoluteorin under different environmental contexts, thus providing novel insights into the study of its biosynthetic pathways. This phenomenon—“similar phenotype, divergent genotypes”—is particularly common among extremophiles and reflects the multiple adaptive strategies that organisms have evolved in response to different ecological pressures [67–69]. Single carbon source experiments indicated that the strain AD133 can efficiently utilize a variety of carbon sources to support growth and proliferation (Fig. S8). This characteristic enables its potential application in the resource recovery of specific industrial wastes. Under high-salt culture conditions (e.g., seawater or saline wastewater), AD133 maintains a high level of pyoluteorin production. The strain can utilize nutrients present in saline wastewater to synthesize pyoluteorin, thereby transforming waste into valuable products, reducing production costs, and mitigating environmental pollution. These features confer significant ecological and economic benefits [70, 71]. Within the annotated genome of AD133, several enzymes associated with the transmembrane transport of nutrients, as well as those involved in various carbon source utilization pathways, have been identified. Thus, we propose the possible pyoluteorin biosynthesis pathways in strain AD133 (Fig. 10). Future studies may focus on targeted gene knockout of rate-limiting steps in the synthesis pathway to optimize production yields, thereby providing a novel strategy for the industrial-scale biosynthesis of pyoluteorin by halophilic bacteria under high-salt conditions. Pyoluteorin, a naturally derived antibiotic secondary metabolite, has demonstrated broad application potential in the fields of plant disease control and human medicine. It effectively reduces crop diseases such as root rot, showing promising prospects in biological control [46]. Moreover, it has been suggested as a potential candidate for novel anti-tumor drugs [72]. The strain AD133 isolated from hypersaline environment affiliating to the genus Arhodomonas showed a clear inhibition activity against microorganisms from three domains. This halotolerant bacterium was determined to synthesize and secrete the biological pesticide pyoluteorin. The ability of producing pyoluteorin was strain specific rather than species specific. Xylose, glucose, fructose, sorbitol, sucrose, maltose, glycerol and acetate can be utilized as single carbon sources for pyoluteorin production. The pyoluteorin biosynthetic gene cluster was uncovered on the genome of AD133 and supported by transcription data. The biosynthesis pathway of pyoluteorin was summarized. This work has established a comprehensive pipeline spanning from strain isolation to bioactive compound identification and ultimately to the characterization of biosynthetic gene cluster. It will undoubtedly provide an instructive reference case for research on natural bioactive compounds derived from microorganisms in extreme environments, particularly hypersaline environments.
