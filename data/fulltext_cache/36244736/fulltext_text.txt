Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are gaining attention as adjunctive agents in periodontal therapy due to their antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and host-modulatory properties. Their potential adjunctive applications include use as local drug delivery agents, topical pastes, gels or mouthrinses, reinforcements of membranes or implant surfaces, and other applications. While numerous in vitro and animal studies have investigated their utility, the evidence remains scattered, with no comprehensive synthesis available. This scoping review aimed to map the existing available evidence on AMPs in periodontal therapy, focusing on AMP types, delivery systems, and reported biological, microbiological, and clinical outcomes. A systematic literature search was conducted across Medline-PubMed, Cochrane, and Google Scholar for studies published in the past 20 years. Studies were included if they evaluated AMPs for periodontal therapy in vivo or in vitro. The key data extracted included the type of AMP, study model, outcomes, and conclusions. The review followed PRISMA-ScR guidelines. This prospective scoping review was conducted in accordance with the original guidance of Arksey and O’Malley[19] and more recent guidelines from Munn et al.[20] The focused question used for the current literature search was: “What antimicrobial peptides have been utilized for periodontal therapy and what are their reported clinical, microbiological, and biological outcomes?” The secondary questions were as follows: What types and formulations of AMPs have been evaluated for periodontal therapy and what delivery systems have been employed?What specific clinical (e.g., pocket probing depth [PD] and clinical attachment level [CAL]), microbiological (e.g., bacterial load), and biological (e.g., cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases) outcomes have been assessed in studies using AMPs for periodontal therapy? A comprehensive search strategy was developed and applied across multiple electronic databases (viz., PubMed/Medline, Embase, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, ClinicalTrials.gov). The search terms included combinations of: (“periodontitis” OR “periodontal disease”) AND (“antimicrobial peptides” OR “AMPs” OR “LL-37” OR “defensins” OR “nisin”) AND (“local delivery” OR “controlled release” OR “drug delivery” OR “gel” OR “scaffold”). A manual search of bibliographies of included studies was also conducted. Keywords used were a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, which were then employed to adapt to the various databases. The publication period was restricted to the last 20 years with limited to the literature published in English. All articles included for the review were in accordance with the scoping review methodology. Records were collected using reference management software, and the titles were screened for duplicates. Two independent reviewers (NP and SH) screened the titles and abstracts based on the preagreed focused question and eligibility criteria. When uncertainty arose, full texts were reviewed to determine eligibility. Disagreements, if any, were resolved through the consensus or consultation with the third and fourth reviewers (AS and GS). Additional manual search was performed of the references from the included full-text articles (NP, SH, and AS). These selected full texts were read for suitability as per the eligibility criteria. The entire process was overseen by AS. Data were extracted independently from the studies and tabulated using Microsoft Excel spreadsheets. The standardized and preagreed data collection sheet included the author title, year of publication, place of study, type and form of AMP used, study design, brief methodology, study results, and conclusion. As this is a scoping review, all studies meeting the eligibility criteria were subject to qualitative research. All reviewers independently verified the collected data. A total of 21 studies were included, comprising 13 in vitro studies, 5 animal studies, and 3 clinical studies. The commonly investigated AMP was LL-37. Delivery systems ranged from gels, spays, nanoparticles, and conjugates. Included studies reported significant antimicrobial activity against various periodontal pathogens. Periodontal disease is a complex antimicrobial inflammatory disorder, characterized by the formation of plaque biofilm, leading to subsequent periodontal tissue destruction.[1] Based on individual disease susceptibility, the degree of destruction may vary among individuals. At least, 50% of adults exhibit some level of periodontal disease.[23] The oral cavity harbors numerous microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and protozoa. As a complex habitat, the mouth offers several niches – gingival sulcus, tongue, cheek, soft and hard palate, floor of the mouth, throat, saliva, and the teeth for plaque colonization.[4] Of the 700 resident species of the oral cavity, only eight bacterial species have consistently been associated with periodontal disease, including Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Treponema forsythia, Treponema denticola, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Eubacterium nodatum Prevotella intermedia, and Prevotella nigrescens.[567] The innate immune system plays a principal role in defending these microorganisms. It augments the physical and chemical barriers by producing anti-microbial peptides (AMPs). AMPs have attracted significant interest as potential adjuncts in the management of periodontal disease due to their dual role in antimicrobial activity and host modulation. While several AMPs have demonstrated promising outcomes in modulating inflammatory responses and suppressing periodontopathogens, most of the available evidence stems from in vitro research and animal models.[89101112131415] These studies have explored the incorporation of AMPs into various formulations for use as local delivery agents, topical gels, or mouthrinses, all aimed at achieving periodontal health. However, the diversity in experimental setups, AMP types, and outcome measures reported across the literature pose a challenge in synthesizing current knowledge and identifying promising directions for clinical translation.[9161718] A scoping review is therefore necessary to map the breadth and nature of existing evidence, clarify the current state of preclinical research on local AMP delivery in periodontology and highlight research gaps that must be addressed. By consolidating findings from the available literature, this review will also help guide the design of future translational research to ascertain the potential use of AMPs as viable therapeutic agents in periodontal therapy. AMPs are a group of small peptides that exist naturally and play a crucial role in the innate immunity of different organisms.[30] AMPs offer a wide range of inhibitory activity against various microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites.[30] These peptides are known to block the inflammatory effects of bacterial toxins.[31] The peptides have co-evolved with oral bacteria and several serve as templates for the development of therapeutic peptides. Several distinct classes of AMPs, including defensins, cathelicidins, protectins, and histatins, have been extensively characterized in the oral cavity. Upon exposure to periodontopathogenic bacteria, these AMPs exhibit a coordinated and synergistic response, mediating the release of key signaling molecules such as interleukins, chemokines, and cytokines. This cascade facilitates neutrophil chemotaxis to the site of infection and orchestrates a robust and regulated host immune response aimed at maintaining periodontal homeostasis.[32] Given their dual antimicrobial and immunomodulatory properties, AMPs have emerged as promising candidates for therapeutic application in periodontal disease. However, their clinical translation is challenged by factors such as proteolytic degradation, short half-life, and difficulties in achieving effective concentrations at the target site.[33] Advances in local drug delivery (LDD) systems – including hydrogels, nanoparticles, and bio-adhesive scaffolds – have opened new avenues to overcome these limitations by ensuring sustained peptide release and enhanced stability within the periodontal environment.[34] This has led to an increasing body of research investigating AMP-based local delivery systems as adjuncts to conventional periodontal therapy. Accordingly, this scoping review aimed to map the existing evidence on the LDDs of AMPs as a therapeutic modality in periodontal therapy. A total of 21 studies were included, comprising 13 in vitro trials, 5 animal model trials, and 3 clinical trials. The findings reveal a growing interest in the use of AMPs due to their broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties, host-modulatory potential, and low propensity for resistance development. However, the distribution of data was skewed toward preclinical data, with limited clinical validation. In vitro studies constituted the majority (62%) of included literature and predominantly investigated the antibacterial activity, cytotoxicity, and biocompatibility of various AMPs both synthetic and natural against periodontal pathogens. Among the peptides studied, LL-37, in both its natural and conjugated forms, emerged as the most frequently investigated. All studies consistently demonstrated promising antibacterial properties, particularly against biofilm-forming organisms and periodontopathogens.[891011121415182122232435] Animal studies further supported the therapeutic potential of AMPs. Locally administered AMPs in experimental periodontitis models were associated with reduced inflammation, decreased alveolar bone loss, and significant positive shifts in microbial composition. These findings provide valuable insights, particularly regarding the in vivo behavior of the peptides, their local tissue effect, and response and feasibility of the delivery method.[18252627] One study incorporated AMPs into veterinary toothpaste and assessed the biofilm count in dogs and reported favorable reduction of colony counts.[28] The findings from in vitro and animal models support the biological plausibility and potential mechanisms of action of AMPs. However, limitations such as study design heterogeneity, small sample sizes, short study duration, and species-specific immune responses must be considered when extrapolating these results to human settings. Despite encouraging preclinical data, clinical evidence remains sparse. Only three clinical studies investigating AMP-based adjunctive therapy were identified, all of which had limited follow-up periods.[161729] While improvements in clinical parameters such as PD and CAL were reported, heterogeneity in study design, peptide type, concentration, and delivery vehicles preclude definitive conclusions. Furthermore, long-term safety, patient-related outcomes, and cost-effectiveness data are lacking. A notable strength of the current review is the diversity of AMPs evaluated and the variety of delivery systems explored, including gels, microspheres, nanoparticle-based carriers, and conjugate systems. These approaches may enhance peptide stability, bioavailability, and controlled release for periodontal therapy. However, standardization is lacking in terms of peptide formulations, study designs, and outcome assessments. In addition, the conflict-of-interest statement and the source of funding, which are essential for assessing research transparency and potential biases, were missing in a few studies.
