Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease that occurs when the body's immune system fails to respond properly to microbial biofilms. Peptides exhibiting anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties are promising as biomarkers and therapeutic agents; however, identifying functional peptide signatures remains a significant challenge due to sequence variability and limited data availability. The aim of this study was to develop a graph-contrastive convolutional neural network (GCCNN) framework for accurately categorizing periodontitis-related peptides into immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory classes, to enhance model interpretability, and facilitate customized peptide-based therapies. Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease of the gum tissues characterized by a dysregulated immune response to bacterial plaque, leading to the destruction of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone, and ultimately resulting in tooth loss. In the pathogenesis of periodontal disease, various host-derived peptides play critical roles as mediators of inflammation.1, 2, 3 In particular, certain anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory peptides can modulate the host immune response and have been implicated in controlling periodontal inflammation. For example, the human cathelicidin LL-37 is known to exhibit antimicrobial activity against periodontal pathogens while also possessing immunomodulatory and tissue-regenerative effects. Elevated levels of LL-37 in saliva and gingival crevicular fluid have been identified as a potential biomarker distinguishing periodontal health from disease.4 These peptide biomarkers, which can either suppress harmful inflammation or modulate the behavior of immune cells, are of great interest for diagnostics and therapeutics in periodontology. A recent study has demonstrated that the ANXA1-FPR2.5 signaling pathway is crucial in the development of periodontal disease. In vitro tests showed that WRW4, an FPR2 antagonist, significantly enhanced the IL-1β-induced release of IL-8 and GM-CSF. This also affected periodontal tissue damage, as WRW4 increased alveolar bone loss, osteoclast activity, and dilated blood vessels, while Ac2-26 reduced these effects. Identifying peptide signatures associated with immunomodulatory or anti-inflammatory activity is challenging due to the multifunctional nature of many peptides and the subtle sequence determinants of their functions. Traditional experimental screens are labor-intensive, and purely sequence-based rules are elusive. Machine learning approaches offer a promising avenue for recognizing complex sequence patterns that correlate with biological function. Recent advances in deep learning have shown success in classifying multifunctional therapeutic peptides by learning rich representations of amino acid sequences. In particular, contrastive learning frameworks such as SimCLR have demonstrated the ability to learn informative latent representations from unlabeled data by maximizing agreement between augmented ‘views’ of the same input. Applying contrastive representation learning to peptide sequences can leverage unlabeled or weakly labeled data to enhance the encoder’s ability to capture meaningful biochemical motifs before fine-tuning on specific labels.6 Prior studies have applied deep learning to peptide classification; however, key gaps remain in leveraging contrastive learning and addressing specialized domains, such as periodontal peptides. Veltri et al. (2018)6 introduced one of the first deep neural networks (combining CNN and LSTM) for antimicrobial peptide (AMP) recognition, achieving improved accuracy over traditional models and even deriving a reduced amino acid ‘alphabet’ from the model. However, that work focused on general AMP vs. non-AMP classification and did not incorporate contrastive representation learning or tackle multi-functional peptide classes. Recent advances have extended peptide classifiers: Zhao et al. (2024)7 developed deepAMPNet, which integrates AlphaFold2-predicted 3D structures with a graph neural network and Bi-LSTM language model. This structure-aware approach significantly outperformed previous AMP predictors in terms of accuracy and AUC. Still, it targets broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and relies on extensive structural data, without utilizing contrastive pre-training. Another recent work, Li et al. (2025)’s8 MultiPep-DLCL, addressed multifunctional therapeutic peptides via a transformer with label-sequence contrastive learning and a specialized focal-dice loss to mitigate class imbalance. MultiPep-DLCL demonstrated superior multi-label classification performance across peptide functions. Nevertheless, its contrastive strategy is supervised (aligning sequences with label embeddings), and it does not explicitly leverage unlabeled data or focus on the interpretability of sequence motifs. Despite these advances, no prior model has specifically tackled periodontitis-related peptides (anti-inflammatory vs. immunomodulatory) using unsupervised contrastive learning. The functional classes in this domain are highly imbalanced and subtle, which previous models did not handle in a structure-aware contrastive manner. Moreover, most deep models for peptide classification offer limited transparency – for instance, deepAMPNet and earlier CNN/RNN models are largely ‘black boxes’ optimized for accuracy. MultiPep-DLCL included an attention mechanism that highlights important residues correlating with known motifs; however, the field generally lacks robust sequence-level interpretability. In light of these gaps, our study introduces a Graph-Contrastive CNN (GCCNN) framework that uniquely combines a lightweight 1D CNN encoder with SimCLR-style.9 Contrastive pre-training on peptide sequences. This approach learns invariant motif-level features from unlabeled or weakly labeled data, enhancing the encoder’s ability to capture meaningful sequence patterns before fine-tuning on the limited labeled set. We specifically focus on periodontal immunomodulatory vs. anti-inflammatory peptides, addressing the class imbalance via weighted loss and providing model transparency via SHAP interpretation of learned motifs. By doing so, we bridge the gap between general peptide predictors and the niche of periodontal bioactive peptides, introducing contrastive representation learning into peptide classification for the first time. We tackle the small, imbalanced dataset with a novel strategy that improves feature extraction and interpretability. This contrasts with prior models that lacked structure-informed contrastive learning, struggled with skewed functional classes, or failed to provide explanations for their predictions. Our GCCNN offers a fresh approach to identify peptide-based signatures in periodontitis, leveraging contrastive learning to overcome data limitations and shining light on the sequence motifs driving model decisions. This research aims to develop a graph-contrastive convolutional neural network (GCCNN) framework capable of reliably categorizing periodontitis-related peptides into functional classes that are immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory. The specific objectives are to: (1) apply contrastive learning to extract strong motif-level representations from sparse peptide sequence data; (2) implement weighted supervised fine-tuning to address class imbalance; and (3) enhance model interpretability by identifying conserved sequence motifs that assist in classification. The ultimate goal is to facilitate the development of customized peptide-based therapies by allowing accurate functional annotation of periodontal peptides. In this research, we introduce a Graph-Contrastive Convolutional Neural Network (GCCNN) framework for classifying peptide sequences relevant to periodontal health into anti-inflammatory peptides (AIPs) and immunomodulatory peptides (IMPs). Our model combines a 1D CNN encoder with SimCLR-style contrastive learning to obtain motif-level representations from peptide sequences that remain unchanged. To fix a big class imbalance, pre-trained representations are fine-tuned using supervised learning with class-weighted binary cross-entropy. We constructed our dataset from a published multifunctional peptide library (MultiPep-DLCL),10 focusing on peptides annotated with anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory functions. The source data were provided in a FASTA-like two-line format: a descriptor line containing a 21-bit multi-hot label vector (preceded by ‘>’), followed by the amino acid sequence line. We filtered this corpus to isolate peptides labeled as anti-inflammatory (AIP) or immunomodulatory (IMP). Specifically, we retained any peptide for which the third bit (index 2) of the label vector indicated anti-inflammatory activity or the eleventh bit (index 10) indicated immunomodulatory activity. All other peptides lacking these labels were excluded. This filtering yielded a dataset of 961 peptides for training and 206 peptides for testing. Each retained peptide is thus associated with a binary label in {AIP, IMP}, noting that some sequences carry both labels (i.e., multifunctional). We preserved the original train/test split provided by the source to evaluate generalization. For each peptide sequence, we performed tokenization and padding to prepare inputs for the CNN. An amino acid vocabulary of size V = 20 (the standard amino acids) plus an extra symbol for unknown or ambiguous residues (‘X’) was defined. We mapped each amino acid character to an integer index (1–20 for actual residues, and zero reserved for padding). The maximum sequence length in the dataset was 50 amino acids, so we fixed the input length, Lmax, to 50 amino acids. Peptide sequences shorter than 50 were right-padded with the padding index 0, and any longer sequences (none in our dataset) would be truncated to 50. This yielded a uniform input tensor shape of N × 50, where N represents the number of sequences, and each row corresponds to an index-encoded peptide. The binary labels for AIP and IMP were encoded as a length-2 target vector for each peptide (with one indicating the presence of the activity, zero indicating its absence). We designed a lightweight convolutional neural network (CNN) encoder to transform fixed-length sequence inputs into latent feature representations. The encoder comprises an embedding layer followed by a 1D convolution and global pooling. The embedding layer maps each amino acid index to a trainable dense vector of dimension de = 32. Thus, the embedding layer E ∈ ℝ^((V+1) × 32) (with +1 for the padding index) produces a 32-dimensional learnable embedding for each amino acid type, and inserts a zero-vector for padded positions (we set padding_idx=0 so the padding token has no contribution). The embedded sequence (shape 50 × 32 per peptide) is then fed into a 1-dimensional convolutional layer that scans for local motifs. We used 64 convolutional filters (output channels) with a kernel size of 5, applied with a stride of 1 and ‘same’ padding, such that the output length remained 50. A rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation is applied to each filter’s output, introducing nonlinearity. This convolutional layer effectively detects any informative 5-residue subsequence patterns (such as short motifs or epitopes) along the peptide. We chose kernel width five as a balance to capture pentapeptide-scale motifs; this can be extended to multiple kernel sizes in future work for detecting patterns of varying lengths. The convolutional outputs (64 feature maps of length 50) are then aggregated by global max pooling along the sequence length axis to obtain a fixed-length representation. The global max pool takes the maximum activation value of each filter across all positions, yielding a 64-dimensional feature vector h for each peptide. Intuitively, this retains the most salient motif activation for each filter, regardless of its position in the sequence. The result h can be viewed as a peptide’s latent signature vector in ℝ^64. This CNN encoder, up to the pooling layer, is denoted f(·) and will be used for both contrastive pre-training and downstream classification. We employed a self-supervised contrastive learning stage to pre-train the CNN encoder f(·) on the unlabeled sequence data. The goal of this stage was to learn an embedding space where the peptide representations are invariant to simple stochastic transformations, such that peptides with similar content map to nearby points. We adopted the SimCLR paradigm, in which the model is trained to maximize agreement between two randomly augmented ‘views’ of the same input sequence in latent space. Stochastic Augmentations: For each peptide in a batch, we generated two independent augmented sequences (View 1 and View 2) through simple sequence perturbations. In one augmentation, we performed random residue dropout, in which a random 15% of the amino acid positions were removed (and the sequence was later re-padded). This simulates a deletion or skip of some residues. In the other augmentation, we performed random masking and substitution of 15% of residues. For those positions, the amino acid was either replaced with the special ‘X’ token or with a randomly chosen amino acid. These augmentations are analogous to the cropping and jittering used in image contrastive learning, providing two different ‘corrupted’ versions of the same peptide that should still share the essential sequence features. Each augmented sequence was processed by the CNN encoder f(·) to produce latent vectors hi(1) = f(view1i) and hi(2) = f(view2i) for peptide i. Projection Head: Following SimCLR, we added a small neural network, g(·), on top of the encoder during pre-training to project the representation, h, to a space where the contrastive loss is applied. The projection head was a 2-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) that takes the 64-dimensional encoder output and produces a 64-dimensional projection. Specifically, g consists of a fully connected layer (64 • 64) with ReLU, followed by a second fully connected layer (64 • 64). We then applied ℓ2-normalization to the output to obtain the final 64-d projected vectors zi(1) = g(hi(1)) and zi(2) = g(hi(2)), with ||z||=1. The normalization encourages the model to utilize direction (angle) in representation space rather than magnitude for comparing samples. Contrastive Loss: We used the NT-Xent (normalized temperature-scaled cross-entropy) loss as introduced in SimCLR. For a given batch of B peptide samples, after augmentation, we have 2B resulting encoded projections. The NT-Xent loss treats each pair of projections (zi(1), zi(2)) from the same peptide i as positive pairs, and all other 2*(B-1) combinations, zi(v) vs. zj(w) (where i ≠ j), as negative pairs. Moreover, the total loss is the average of Li over all i, including both views. Here, sim(u, v) = ⟨u, v⟩ is the cosine similarity (since u and v are unit-normalized), and τ is a temperature hyperparameter that controls the concentration level of the distribution. We set τ = 0.1, which was found to give stable gradients for the 64-dimensional projections. Intuitively, this loss trains the encoder so that each peptide’s two augmented versions have a high inner product, while the similarity between different peptides’ representations is low. Pre-training Setup: We optimized the contrastive loss using the Adam optimizer (β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999) with a learning rate of 1 × 10³. Batches of size B = 128 peptides were sampled from the training set without labels, and each batch was processed to compute the NT-Xent loss. The encoder and projection head were trained for 10 epochs over the 961 training peptides in this self-supervised manner. We observed that by ∼10 epochs, the contrastive loss had largely converged (further epochs yielded diminishing returns on this small dataset). Contrastive Training Outcome: The contrastive pre-training successfully decreased the NT-Xent loss from an initial value of ∼4.4 to around 1.86 by the final epoch, indicating that the model learned to cluster augmented views of the same peptide in the latent space. Figure 1 shows the contrastive loss curve over epochs. The steadily falling loss indicates that the encoder has become progressively better at producing similar embeddings for augmented versions of each peptide while distinguishing between different peptides. After contrastive pre-training, we discarded the projection head g(·) and focused on the 64-dimensional encoder outputs h = f(sequence) as peptide embeddings. We then trained a supervised classifier on top of these embeddings to predict the two target labels (AIP and IMP). The classifier head is a simple feed-forward layer: a fully connected linear layer mapping the 64-dimensional encoder output to two outputs, followed by a sigmoid activation to produce probabilities for the two labels. This formulation treats the task as multi-label binary classification, since a peptide can potentially have one, both, or neither of the two activities. (In our dataset, ‘neither’ does not occur by construction, and ‘both’ is possible for a small subset.) We initialized the classifier’s weights randomly and fine-tuned the entire model end-to-end, i.e., the CNN encoder weights from pre-training were not frozen but continued to update during supervised training. All layers were optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻10^³ (the same as used in pre-training). We used a smaller batch size of 64 for supervised training and trained for 10 epochs over the labeled training set. Given the severe class imbalance (far more AIP-positive peptides than IMP-positive), we employed loss weighting to give extra emphasis to the minority class. The loss function was binary cross-entropy, computed independently for each of the two outputs, with weights of 1.0 for the AIP class and 5.0 for the IMP class. Thus, errors in predicting the IMP label contributed five times as much loss as errors in AIP, counteracting the skew, where only 147 of 961 training peptides (15%) carry the IMP label. This weight factor of 5 was tuned via a grid search (testing values 1, 3, 5, 10); it was found that higher weights (e.g., 10) led to over-fitting and degraded AIP performance, while 5 provided the best balance. During fine-tuning, the model learned to output a probability for each peptide being anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory. A peptide was considered predicted positive for a class if the sigmoid probability exceeded 0.5. The training loss steadily decreased from ∼1.89 at epoch 1 to ∼0.98 by epoch 10, indicating the model was fitting the data. No explicit early stopping was necessary, given the small epoch count and the observation that the loss continued to decline gently. The selection of methods and hyperparameters for a model was based on prior domain knowledge and empirical validation. Initial architectures, such as the 1D CNN encoder and SimCLR-style contrastive learning, were chosen based on their effectiveness in the literature. We evaluated the trained model on the hold-out test set of 206 peptides (which were not seen during contrastive pre-training or fine-tuning). The study focuses on a multi-label task and includes measures such as exact-match accuracy, per-class precision, recall, and F1-score. It also presents ROC curves and AUC values for each label, highlighting the trade-off between the true positive rate and the false positive rate. PR curves and Average Precision are also used to analyze imbalanced data. Confusion matrices are presented for each class to illustrate the counts of true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives in the classification process. All metrics were first computed on the training set to verify learning, and then on the test set to evaluate generalization. Below, we primarily report the test set outcomes unless otherwise specified. On a curated peptide dataset, GCCNN performed well with AIPs (F1 = 0.94, ROC-AUC = 0.98), but struggled to generalize IMP predictions due to insufficient data (F1 = 0.04, ROC-AUC = 0.54). Using t-SNE to visualize the latent space revealed that AIP clusters were closely grouped, whereas IMP embeddings were more widely dispersed. SHAP-based interpretation revealed conserved sequence motifs that contribute to AIP predictions, highlighting the model's transparency. After fine-tuning, the model achieved high accuracy in identifying anti-inflammatory (AIP) peptides, but it struggled with immunomodulatory (IMP) peptides due to the imbalance. The overall exact-match classification accuracy on the training set was ∼85.6%, indicating that in ∼86% of peptides, the model correctly assigned both labels. The model exhibited consistent performance on the independent test set with an exact-match accuracy of around 84%, indicating no severe overfitting. For anti-inflammatory peptides (AIP), precision was approximately 0.88, and recall was perfect at 1.00 during training, leading to a high F1-score of around 0.94. On the test set, AIP precision remained between 0.85 and 0.90. In contrast, for immunomodulatory peptides (IMP), the model had a training precision of 1.00 but a very low recall of 0.02, identifying only three true IMPs out of a larger set. The F1-score for IMP was extremely low at 0.04, and on the test set, the model captured almost no IMPs, maintaining a precision of 100% based on almost negligible predictions. The classifier effectively learned to identify AIP labels, maximizing sensitivity for these instances, while minimizing the prediction of IMP labels to reduce false positives. Despite some detection of IMP cases encouraged by loss weighting, recall remained very low, resulting in a macro-averaged F1-score of approximately 0.49 during training, largely influenced by the weak performance of IMP. To examine the model’s discrimination power in a threshold-independent manner, we plot the ROC and Precision–Recall curves for each class in Fig. 4, Fig. 5. The ROC curve for AIP is near the top-left corner, with an AUROC ≈ of 0.98, indicating excellent separability of AIP-positive vs AIP-negative peptides. In contrast, the ROC curve for IMP lies close to the diagonal (AUROC ≈ 0.54), barely above random guessing. This slightly above-chance performance for IMP suggests the model’s ranking of peptides by IMP score contains minimal signal – essentially, only a few true IMP peptides receive higher scores than the negatives, and most others are indistinguishable from negatives. A Graph-Contrastive CNN framework has been developed to classify peptide-based immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory signatures in the context of periodontal disease. The model uses contrastive self-supervised learning to equip a CNN encoder with robust peptide representations and fine-tune it to predict specific functional labels. The results show that the contrastive pre-training significantly benefits the majority class (anti-inflammatory peptides), enabling the model to achieve high precision and recall for identifying anti-inflammatory candidates. However, identifying immunomodulatory peptides remains difficult when there are few positive examples. Future work could include data augmentation, more sophisticated loss functions, expanding the neural architecture, and transferring knowledge from large pre-trained protein language models. The identification of immunomodulatory peptides can be improved through several strategies. These include data augmentation or up-sampling, which involves synthetically augmenting IMP-positive samples to increase their effective number.11,12 Adjusting the loss function, which can be done by focusing on hard positives or reducing easy negatives, can help the model learn from mistakes rather than being overwhelmed by easy non-IMP examples. Architecture enhancements can be made to the CNN, which has a single kernel size and captures relatively local patterns. Including multiple convolution kernel sizes, recurrent layers, or transformer encoders can help capture more complex sequence dependencies. Transfer learning from large protein language models, such as ESM-2 or ProtT5, can also be utilized to give the network a head start in understanding peptide properties. Despite these advantages, our current approach failed to achieve a satisfactory recall for IMP peptides. The model’s virtual inability to recall IMPs (detecting only 1–2% of them) indicates a classification threshold that is essentially set too high for that class – it only labels a peptide as IMP if it is extremely confident (hence the few positives it does predict are correct). This behavior is partly due to the overwhelming class imbalance: with 850 AIPs vs 147 IMPs in training, the model can minimize overall loss by focusing on AIP. Even with a 5 × loss weight on IMP, the absolute number of IMP samples may have been too low for the model to learn generalizable features. The precision of 1.0 for IMP suggests that the model identified a very specific pattern present in exactly those few peptides it marked as positive (perhaps a short motif or composition that coincidentally only those IMPs had). Everything outside that narrow pattern was treated as negative. In other words, the model likely overfitted to a tiny subset of the IMP class (achieving perfect precision on that subset) and effectively ignored the rest, resulting in almost no coverage of the true IMP peptides.15,16 The contrastive CNN model showed varied outcomes compared to earlier methods, demonstrating strengths in recognizing anti-inflammatory peptides (AIP) with high metrics (F1-score ≈0.94, ROC-AUC 0.98), but performed poorly on the minority immunomodulatory peptide (IMP) class (F1 ≈0.04, AUC 0.54). This disparity underscores the difficulty of generalizing to underrepresented categories. In comparison, previous peptide predictors achieved consistently high accuracy across categories, such as deepAMPNet, which reached excellent external metrics (AUC > 0.95) without the challenges of class imbalance. The MultiPep-DLCL study addressed this issue by utilizing focal and cross-entropy loss, thereby enhancing its ability to recognize rare functional labels. Although a class-weighted training was also employed in the current model, the IMP class remained problematic, indicating a need for advanced strategies or more data to improve performance in minority classes. Notably, our contrastive pre-training enhanced the model’s latent space and AIP performance, despite the small dataset. Compared to Veltri et al.’s 2018 CNN/LSTM model, which required hundreds of peptides to reach ∼91% accuracy, our method maintained high AIP accuracy with far fewer samples by learning robust representations from unlabeled augmentations. This indicates better generalization to limited data: an advantage over purely supervised models from earlier studies. However, the IMP outcome also reveals the limits of contrastive learning when positive examples are extremely scarce – an area where MultiPep-DLCL’s label-informed contrastive approach had some success in recovering ‘tail’ labels.17,18 In terms of interpretability, our model provides clearer insights than most prior approaches. We employed SHAP value analysis to uncover conserved sequence motifs that drive AIP predictions, providing a transparent view of what the CNN learned. This level of motif-based explanation is generally lacking in deepAMPNet and Veltri et al.’s model, which focused on predictive performance without pinpointing sequence features (apart from Veltri’s coarse-grained reduced alphabet insight). MultiPep-DLCL utilized an attention mechanism to highlight important amino acids correlated with known functional motifs, aligning with our emphasis on transparency. Nonetheless, our approach’s ability to directly link motifs to model output via SHAP adds a layer of interpretability that pure attention or feature importance analyses in earlier studies did not fully achieve.19,20 Overall, compared to the three prior works, our GCCNN demonstrated competitive accuracy for the dominant class and introduced greater interpretability; however, it underperformed on the extremely limited IMP class. This contrast highlights a trade-off: methods like deepAMPNet and MultiPep-DLCL excel in overall accuracy (with ample data or multi-label training), whereas our contrastive CNN, designed for a small specialized dataset, enhances feature learning and explainability but requires further refinement to boost minority-class predictions. Importantly, our results suggest that self-supervised contrastive learning can be a powerful tool in peptide classification, enabling generalization on small or imbalanced datasets when combined with targeted strategies (e.g., two-stage models or data augmentation) to address the hardest-to-predict classes. This study highlights the clinical and biological significance of identifying anti-inflammatory peptides in periodontitis research, aiding in the discovery of biomarkers for early diagnosis and therapeutic approaches. It reveals insights into inflammatory regulation and suggests a framework for computational classification of peptides, which could expedite therapeutic development. The model struggles with class imbalance and may benefit from a multi-task learning approach and a two-stage classification pipeline for improved accuracy. In summary, the graph-contrastive CNN approach successfully extracted a meaningful signature for anti-inflammatory periodontal peptides, demonstrating the utility of contrastive pre-training in biochemical sequence classification. However, it underscored the need for advanced strategies to handle rare functional classes, such as immunomodulatory peptides. The insights gained, such as the near-perfect precision but low recall for IMP and the latent space clustering, guide us toward targeted improvements as outlined above.
