The overuse of antibiotics has precipitated a global health crisis, as antibiotic-resistant bacteria increasingly compromise the efficacy of conventional therapies [1, 2]. Antibiotic resistance, confirmed as a major threat, causes millions of deaths annually [3]. Among the most concerning are the ESKAPE pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Escherichia coli (E. coli), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), which are characterized by their multidrug resistance and frequent association with severe healthcare-related infections such as pneumonia and bacteremia [4, 5]. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have emerged as promising therapeutic candidates, demonstrating rapid bactericidal activity and effectiveness against resistant strains [6]. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), a key metric for evaluating AMP potency, plays a crucial role in guiding treatment strategies. However, conventional MIC determination remains labor-intensive and time-consuming, thus motivating the development of efficient computational prediction methods [7]. Machine learning has advanced AMP analysis, initially focusing on classification. Classifiers, such as AntiBP and AMPScanner, utilized algorithms to identify antibacterial peptides, but provided only binary outputs [8–15]. Subsequently, to address this limitation, a new class of models was developed to estimate MIC values directly. Xiao et al. employed pseudo-amino acid composition and Gaussian kernel regression to model AMP activity against E. coli [16], while Yan et al. applied a multi-branch convolutional neural network (CNN) with attention mechanisms, trained on data from the DBAASP database [17]. More recently, ensemble learning has been applied to improve generalization across species. For instance, Chung et al. developed an ensemble deep learning model for MIC regression across multiple pathogens [18], and AMPActiPred implemented a three-stage deep forest framework capable of both AMP identification and MIC prediction [19]. Additionally, ESKAPEE-MICpred incorporated genomic and language model features to capture pathogen-specific AMP interactions [20]. Despite these advances, most existing models remain limited in their ability to capture spatial dependencies within AMP sequences, as traditional encoding schemes may not fully preserve the spatial patterns necessary for accurate MIC prediction [21]. Parallel innovations in AI have further expanded the possibilities for AMP discovery and optimization. Generative adversarial networks (GANs), such as the feedback-based design framework by Zervou et al., have been used to synthesize novel peptides with enhanced antimicrobial properties [22]. Transformer-based architectures like ProtTrans have demonstrated strong performance in capturing long-range sequence dependencies relevant to antimicrobial activity [23]. Reinforcement learning has also shown promise, as demonstrated by Wang et al., who developed an optimization framework that iteratively refines AMP sequences to balance potency and toxicity [24]. Moreover, hybrid models that integrate empirical screening data with deep learning architectures, such as combinations of CNNs and recurrent neural networks [14], reflect a growing trend toward more comprehensive modeling of AMP functionality. Collectively, these strategies underscore the transformative potential of AI-driven methodologies in advancing both AMP prediction and rational design [25–27]. Despite recent progress in computational approaches, critical challenges remain in accurately predicting AMP activity and MIC values. Many traditional models rely primarily on sequence-derived features, such as pseudo-amino acid composition, which often fail to capture spatial patterns essential for modeling AMP–bacteria interactions [21]. In addition, numerous existing frameworks lack integration of physicochemical properties that are vital to AMP function, thereby limiting predictive accuracy across diverse bacterial species [28]. Practical application is further hindered by the high synthesis cost and molecular complexity of AMPs, reinforcing the need for more efficient and informative predictive strategies [6]. To address these limitations, we propose a novel computational pipeline that leverages Chaos Game Representation (CGR) encoding to convert AMP sequences into image-based frequency matrices, effectively preserving the spatial distribution of sequence motifs [29, 30]. This encoding facilitates the application of computer vision methods, enabling the model to capture both local sequential features and global structural patterns [31]. Building on this foundation, we developed ANIA, a deep learning framework designed to predict MIC values of AMPs against three clinically relevant pathogens: S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa [31, 32]. Unlike conventional binary classifiers, ANIA adopts a regression-based strategy to estimate MIC values as continuous outcomes, enabling fine-grained assessments of antimicrobial activity [7]. These repositories provided validated sequences and MIC values, supporting predictive modeling. The ESKAPE pathogens were selected for their multidrug resistance and clinical relevance [5]. The dataset comprised 8283, 7582, and 5621 sequences for S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa, respectively, partitioned into 4:1 training and test sets with 5-fold cross-validation. The number of AMP sequences allocated to the training and test sets for each bacterial species is summarized in Supplementary Table S1. To transform AMP sequences into a format compatible with deep learning models designed for image-based data, we employed CGR encoding to convert each sequence into a two-dimensional frequency matrix. CGR is a mathematical technique based on iterated function systems, which maps sequential data into a fractal representation within a unit square, effectively preserving the spatial distribution of sequence motifs [29]. This representation has been widely recognized as a robust feature encoding strategy for biological sequences, including DNA, RNA, and proteins, and has facilitated the application of computer vision-based methods in various prediction tasks [30, 31, 36, 37]. In our study, this encoding enables the transformation of AMP sequences into image-like representations suitable for MIC regression modeling. The FCGR matrix serves as an image-based representation of the AMP sequence, capturing the spatial distribution of sequence motifs that are critical for MIC estimation. This format is well-suited for computer vision-based deep learning models, enabling them to leverage structured motif frequency patterns to enhance predictive accuracy. Moreover, the FCGR representation facilitates interpretability by allowing visual analysis of sequence motif distributions, as demonstrated in our feature interpretation experiments. Initial attempts to predict MIC values of AMPs using conventional sequence encodings and traditional machine learning methods demonstrated limited performance, particularly when applied to spatially structured representations [12, 18]. To overcome these limitations, we employed CGR to convert AMP sequences into 16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\times$\end{document}16 single-channel frequency matrices, referred to as FCGR. However, standard machine learning algorithms were not well suited for extracting hierarchical patterns from these image-like features. This motivated the development of ANIA, a deep learning framework specifically designed to predict MIC values against S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa using FCGR-based inputs [32, 41]. Predicting MIC values from FCGR representations requires the model to capture both localized motif patterns, which reflect physicochemical residue interactions, and global spatial dependencies, which describe the overall organization of functional regions within the peptide map. To meet these dual requirements, ANIA was designed as a hybrid architecture that combines convolutional and attention-based mechanisms. Specifically, ANIA integrates two stacked Inception modules to extract multi-scale local motifs. In this study, two Inception modules were stacked sequentially, providing an optimal balance between predictive performance, architectural complexity, and computational efficiency based on empirical evaluation (see Supplementary Table S3). The extracted features are subsequently processed by a Transformer encoder that models long-range dependencies across the FCGR representation. A final regression head is employed to estimate MIC values. This hybrid design leverages the complementary strengths of convolutional feature extraction and attention-driven contextual learning, enabling the model to jointly learn fine-grained spatial features and global dependency patterns from image-based peptide representations. This representation has been widely recognized as a robust feature encoding strategy for biological sequences, including DNA, RNA, and proteins, and has facilitated the application of computer vision-based methods in various prediction tasks [30, 31, 36, 37]. In our study, this encoding enables the transformation of AMP sequences into image-like representations suitable for MIC regression modeling. The FCGR matrix serves as an image-based representation of the AMP sequence, capturing the spatial distribution of sequence motifs that are critical for MIC estimation. This format is well-suited for computer vision-based deep learning models, enabling them to leverage structured motif frequency patterns to enhance predictive accuracy. Moreover, the FCGR representation facilitates interpretability by allowing visual analysis of sequence motif distributions, as demonstrated in our feature interpretation experiments. Initial attempts to predict MIC values of AMPs using conventional sequence encodings and traditional machine learning methods demonstrated limited performance, particularly when applied to spatially structured representations [12, 18]. To overcome these limitations, we employed CGR to convert AMP sequences into 16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\times$\end{document}16 single-channel frequency matrices, referred to as FCGR. However, standard machine learning algorithms were not well suited for extracting hierarchical patterns from these image-like features. This motivated the development of ANIA, a deep learning framework specifically designed to predict MIC values against S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa using FCGR-based inputs [32, 41]. Predicting MIC values from FCGR representations requires the model to capture both localized motif patterns, which reflect physicochemical residue interactions, and global spatial dependencies, which describe the overall organization of functional regions within the peptide map. To meet these dual requirements, ANIA was designed as a hybrid architecture that combines convolutional and attention-based mechanisms. Specifically, ANIA integrates two stacked Inception modules to extract multi-scale local motifs. In this study, two Inception modules were stacked sequentially, providing an optimal balance between predictive performance, architectural complexity, and computational efficiency based on empirical evaluation (see Supplementary Table S3). The extracted features are subsequently processed by a Transformer encoder that models long-range dependencies across the FCGR representation. A final regression head is employed to estimate MIC values. This hybrid design leverages the complementary strengths of convolutional feature extraction and attention-driven contextual learning, enabling the model to jointly learn fine-grained spatial features and global dependency patterns from image-based peptide representations. Using E. coli as a representative example, we first examined the local sequence composition underlying each pixel to determine the types of 3-mer motifs that were encoded within the FCGR space. Because each pixel typically aggregates multiple 3-mers, Fig. 5A displays the most representative motif in each pixel, providing a fine-grained map of motif distribution across the grid. We then analyzed the model’s Grad-CAM attention map (Fig. 5B) to identify the pixels receiving the strongest activations during prediction. These attention-focused regions were enriched with recurring motif families characterized by cationic–hydrophobic combinations (e.g. RRI, KSI, KVA, KAI), short alanine- and leucine-dominated nonpolar fragments (e.g. LLS, KSA, LSA), and mixed polar–hydrophobic triplets containing aromatic or bulky residues (e.g. WWR, CYR, GVA). Collectively, these findings suggest that the ANIA model focuses on composite sequence–structure features shaped by the balance between cationic–hydrophobic enrichment and localized flexibility. To further interpret the model’s focus regions, we highlighted two major subregions—colored in orange and green in Fig. 5B—that exhibited the strongest Grad-CAM activations in E. coli. We next examined the statistical association between these spatial patterns and predicted MIC values. A cell-wise Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 5C) revealed that the attention-focused subregions displayed moderately strong negative correlations with predicted log MIC values, indicating that higher motif frequency in these areas tended to drive the model toward predicting higher antimicrobial potency. To assess whether these correlations were reflected at the sequence level, we compared the FCGR intensity distributions between high-activity and low-activity peptides (Fig. 5D). The resulting difference map showed that the same attention-highlighted regions were preferentially enriched in high-activity peptides, whereas low-activity peptides exhibited more diffuse or depleted signal patterns. This correspondence between model attention and predicted activity grouping suggests that the ANIA model supports its decisions on spatially localized motif clusters that it internally associates with stronger activity. Finally, we evaluated the physicochemical properties underlying these spatial motifs by computing cell-wise weighted average hydrophobicity using the ARGP820101 index (Fig. 5E) [51]. The attention-focused regions exhibited higher hydrophobicity values in high-activity peptides, consistent with prior experimental findings that greater hydrophobicity enhances peptide–membrane interactions and antimicrobial efficacy [28,52,53]. Cross-species comparison of attention-weighted motif distributions consistently supported these compositional trends across E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus (see Supplementary Note 1.5). Taken together, this integrative analysis identifies spatially coherent subregions within the FCGR matrix that exert a strong influence on the prediction of antimicrobial potency. By integrating spatial localization, correlation structure, and physicochemical context, our interpretability analysis reveals how ANIA internally associates specific motif compositions with high predicted activity, thereby clarifying the model’s decision basis. These insights not only enhance the transparency of the prediction process but also provide a foundation for rational AMP design guided by model-interpretable sequence features. To further evaluate the predictive capability of the ANIA model, we conducted a comparative analysis against three established MIC prediction tools: ESKAPEE-Pred [20], esAMPMIC [18], and AMPActiPred [19]. These models were selected based on their relevance to AMP MIC prediction and public availability. Given that many prior studies suffer from dataset inconsistencies and reproducibility limitations, we curated an independent test set targeting S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa, ensuring no sequence overlap with the training data of the compared tools. These results demonstrate ANIA’s superior ability to capture MIC-relevant sequence patterns, particularly under challenging conditions such as those presented by P. aeruginosa. To establish a baseline for MIC prediction, we evaluated conventional machine learning models trained on iFeature-derived descriptors [42], comprising physicochemical, structural, and compositional features (Supplementary Table S5). The models comprised linear regressors (Linear Regression [43], Lasso [44], Ridge [45], Elastic Net [46]), tree-based ensembles (Random Forest, RF [47], Gradient Boosting [48], XGBoost [49]), and a kernel-based method (Support Vector Machine, SVM [50]). Model performance was evaluated on independent test sets for S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa (Table 1). Gradient Boosting achieved the highest predictive performance, with the strongest PCC and explained variance across species. RF and XGBoost also performed robustly, while linear models showed reduced accuracy, particularly for P. aeruginosa. SVM exhibited intermediate results. Notably, E. coli showed the best overall results, possibly due to its larger dataset and stronger sequence length–MIC association. The rise of bacterial resistance highlights the need for alternative therapies. AMPs, with broad-spectrum efficacy, offer a promising option. Predicting AMP activity is crucial for their development. Here, we present ANIA, a model predicting AMP MICs for S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa, meeting the demand for accurate, interpretable tools. The ANIA model integrates spatial features extracted via FCGR with a deep learning architecture. To enhance transparency, we incorporated multiple interpretability techniques—including motif-level composition mapping, Grad-CAM visualizations, correlation mapping, motif frequency analysis, and physicochemical profiling. Comparative assessments demonstrated that ANIA consistently outperformed existing tools such as ESKAPEE-Pred, esAMPMIC, and AMPActiPred, achieving higher correlation coefficients and reduced errors. The most notable improvement was observed in P. aeruginosa, where prior models exhibited limited predictive capacity. Motif-based analysis identified four key FCGR subregions linked to low MICs, marked by strong Grad-CAM attention and negative correlations. Hydrophobicity mapping showed higher hydrophobicity in these areas aligned with stronger activity, consistent with prior findings on AMP–membrane interactions. To enhance usability, we developed the ANIA web interface, allowing users to input peptide sequences and obtain MIC predictions for three clinically relevant bacterial species. This resource offers a practical tool for evaluating peptide potency and supports rational AMP design. Despite its strong performance, our study has certain limitations. The dataset, though carefully curated, remains modest in size, which may restrict model generalizability. Moreover, while FCGR-based encoding effectively captures spatial features, exploring alternative representations—including structure-informed or evolutionary embeddings, as well as peptide descriptors that account for post-translational modifications—may offer further improvements [54–56]. Future research should aim to expand the dataset, incorporate descriptors and extend prediction to antiviral or anticancer activity. Moreover, the ANIA framework, which combines CGR-based spatial encoding with Inception-Attention modeling, is conceptually generalizable to other peptide-related prediction tasks, such as toxicity or hemolytic activity forecasting. In conclusion, ANIA provides a robust, interpretable MIC prediction framework, supporting peptide discovery and advancing strategies against antimicrobial resistance. Antimicrobial resistance necessitates the development of predictive tools for evaluating peptide efficacy beyond conventional classification tasks. ANIA is a deep learning framework that integrates CGR and a hybrid Inception–Transformer architecture to predict MIC values of AMPs. Benchmark comparisons with existing tools demonstrate that ANIA consistently outperforms baseline models across three clinically relevant bacteria, especially P. aeruginosa. Motif-level interpretability analyses identify biologically meaningful FCGR subregions, linking sequence motifs and physicochemical properties to antimicrobial potency. A publicly available ANIA web server enables MIC prediction from user-submitted AMP sequences, facilitating rational peptide design and translational applications.
