Supplementary Material 1 Supplementary Material 1 The widespread application of antibiotics causes bacteria to develop resistance mechanisms1. Based on a 2019 report by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control (CDC) and Prevention, around 2.8 million cases of infections caused by resistant bacteria arise annually in the United States, leading to more than 35,000 deaths2. In Europe, approximately 33,000 deaths are attributed to resistant bacterial infections each year3. Therefore, given the severe antibiotic resistance issues arising from the overuse of antibiotics and the resulting threat to public health, there is an immediate need to develop novel alternatives to antibiotics4,5. It is worth noting that Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have been described as broad-spectrum antimicrobial or immunomodulatory molecules that can directly target microorganisms, often resulting in cell lysis or regulating the host’s immune system to strengthen defense against microorganisms6. Additionally, they exhibit a faster action rate compared to traditional antibiotics7. After millions of years of evolution, although bacteria have been continuously exposed to AMPs, significant development of resistance has not occurred8. With their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, fast-acting mechanism, and low resistance risk, AMPs are regarded as ideal candidates for replacing traditional antibiotics in the future9,10. Screening for new AMPs through wet-lab experiments on a large scale is time-consuming, expensive, and labor-intensive11. Therefore, over the past few decades, machine learning-based approaches have been extensively utilized to accelerate the identification of AMPs and have achieved remarkable results. Initially, Fjell et al.12 used a Hidden Markov Model for the clustering and analysis of AMPs. Later, Randou et al.13 introduced a logistic regression-based method for AMP identification. Subsequently, Veltri et al.14 and Meher et al.15 developed AMP recognition models using Random Forest and Support Vector Machine, respectively, and demonstrated good performance on benchmark datasets. Currently, owing to the powerful sequence representation and automatic feature extraction capabilities of deep learning methods16–18, models based on deep learning are gaining increasing attention in scientific research, particularly in applications within fields such as bioinformatics. Han et al.19 developed an ensemble model called TriStack, which combines deep learning and machine learning methods for AMP identification. Li et al.20 applied an attention mechanism as the backbone network in their model, while Veltri et al.21 adopted Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks as classifiers for AMP recognition. Zhang et al.22 proposed a BERT-based model for AMP identification. The model is first pre-trained on sequence data obtained from the UniProt23 database and then fine-tuned on six different datasets to enhance its performance. Sharma et al.24 compared the performance of eight different models in the task of AMP identification and conducted an in-depth analysis of the impact of various input features and network architectures on model performance. Through systematic experiments and evaluations, they found that the model using word2vec as the input feature and bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM)25 as the core framework achieved the best performance. In addition, some models leveraged structural information to learn features. For example, the models developed by Hao et al.26 and Cordoves-Delgado et al.27 combined both sequence and structural features to perform prediction, employing Graph Neural Networks (GNN) as the backbone network. Protein structures encompass all essential information encoded in their sequences. Therefore, in general, structure-based models tend to offer superior performance compared to sequence-based models. However, to maintain high model accuracy, structure-based approaches must rely on accurate protein structures as input28,29. Therefore, utilizing protein sequences for AMP identification remains an urgent and critical research challenge. Although machine learning and deep learning-based approaches have shown significant progress in AMP identification, traditional sequence-driven models have yet to achieve optimal performance in practical applications and still exhibit certain limitations. For example, recent models such as AMPlify and TriStack have achieved notable results but predominantly rely on conventional handcrafted features and limited feature fusion strategies. In particular, AMPlify uses BiLSTM for feature extraction, which increases computational overhead. Overall, most existing methods face several issues: firstly, most existing methods rely on a limited set of feature descriptors, frequently neglecting critical features with evolutionary information, physicochemical properties, or features extracted through pre-trained models, which impacts the models’ accuracy and generalization ability. Secondly, many existing methods typically employ simple feature fusion strategies or a unified network architecture for feature extraction, neglecting the complementarity and diversity among different features. Finally, although LSTM25 and other recurrent neural networks exhibit significant advantages in modeling sequence context relationships, their large number of parameters may introduce redundant information or increase computational overhead during short peptide feature extraction, thereby affecting the model’s computational efficiency and generalization ability. Pretraining and contrastive learning are two effective representation learning methods. Pretraining involves leveraging large amounts of unlabeled data to train models within an unsupervised context, with the trained parameters then transferred to downstream tasks for adaptive optimization or feature extraction30. This approach is commonly applied in applications like protein structure and function prediction31,32. Contrastive learning, on the other hand, focuses on constructing a representation space where similar samples are positioned closer together, while samples from different categories are kept apart. This strategy significantly enhances the quality of protein embeddings and has improved model performance in areas like protein-ligand interaction prediction33,34 and protein-DNA interaction prediction35. Furthermore, CNN36,37 serve as a powerful tool for feature extraction, effectively capturing local patterns within sequential data. However, traditional CNNs exhibit limitations in modeling long-range dependencies. To address the limitations of conventional CNN in modeling long-range dependencies, we introduce Gated Convolutional Neural Network (GCNN)38. This method combines the parallelism of convolutional operations with the selectivity of gating mechanisms, thereby enhancing the capacity for feature representation. Moreover, it achieves performance comparable to, or even surpassing, that of LSTM models, while maintaining lower computational cost. GCNN has already shown promising results in tasks such as affinity prediction39. In this work, we develop a novel dual-module neural network framework, CG-AMP, specifically designed for the identification of AMPs. This framework extracts rich feature representations from different perspectives of AMP sequences, encompassing features obtained through a pre-trained language model, features containing evolutionary information, and features reflecting physicochemical properties. We define the integrated features that combine evolutionary information and physicochemical attributes as multi-dimensional features. To enhance the efficiency of learning these multi-view features, we propose a dual-module strategy. The contrastive learning module integrates a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) encoder and optimizes the pre-trained features through contrastive learning, thereby enhancing their discriminative ability, while the GCNN module employs a GCNN to capture the sequential contextual information of the multidimensional features. ​Each module is responsible for extracting and learning different types of complementary features, which are subsequently integrated through an efficient fusion mechanism, thereby significantly enhancing the accuracy of AMP identification. Compared with other integration strategies, the proposed design achieves deep complementarity between semantic and physicochemical features and balances global and local dependency modeling. As a result, it generates peptide representations that are more informative, stable, and generalizable, substantially enhancing the accuracy and robustness of AMP identification. We employed two benchmark datasets, AMPlify20 and DAMP21, for model validation and performance evaluation. The AMPlify dataset was compiled by integrating sequence data from multiple authoritative public databases. Its positive sample set was primarily sourced from the Antimicrobial Peptide Database (APD3)40 and the Database of Anuran Defense Peptides (DADP)41. After removing redundant sequences and filtering based on length (excluding peptide sequences longer than 200 residues), a final positive sample set of 4,173 unique and mature AMPs was obtained. The negative sample set was carefully curated from the UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot23 database. First, researchers excluded all sequences containing any of the following 16 keywords associated with antimicrobial activity: antibiotic, antifungal, anti-protist, antimicrobial, histatin, bacteriocin, microbicidal, defense, defensin, antimalarial, antiparasitic, cathelicidin, fungicide, anticancer, antiviral, and antibacterial. Additionally, duplicate entries were removed, and sequences containing any non-standard amino acid residues were filtered out. Ultimately, 4,173 sequences were randomly selected from the remaining 128,445 non-AMP sequences to match the size of the positive sample set. Additionally, the length distribution of the negative sample set was adjusted to match that of the positive sample set. This strategy ensured the balance and reliability of the dataset. The AMPlify dataset was split into a training set and a test set with a 4:1 proportion. The training set was then further divided into a training subset and a validation set in a 7:1 ratio (as shown in Table 1). The DAMP database was developed by Veltri et al. By applying strict sequence filtering criteria with a sequence similarity threshold of 40%, the researchers constructed a balanced dataset consisting of 3,556 samples (1,778 AMPs and 1,778 non-AMPs). In this study, we propose a computational framework named CG-AMP, designed for the prediction and identification of AMPs. The construction of CG-AMP involves four core steps: data preparation, input feature encoding, neural network architecture design, and model evaluation. First, during the data preparation phase, we utilize benchmark datasets from AMPlify and DAMP to ensure the reliability of data used for model training and validation. Second, to transform AMP sequence information into a format suitable for neural network processing, we employ a combination of a pre-trained language model, one-hot encoding, BLOSUM62 encoding, and Z-scale encoding to convert sequences into numerical matrices, thereby completing the representation of input features. Third, we design a dual-module feature extraction framework to separately process ESM-2 embeddings and multi-dimensional handcrafted features (BLOSUM62, Z-scale, and one-hot encoding). Each feature type is independently encoded via contrastive learning and gated convolutional neural network (GCNN) modules to capture semantic and physicochemical information. We evaluate several feature fusion strategies, including simple concatenation, linear fusion, and attention-based fusion, and found that linear fusion achieves the best balance between model performance and complexity. The selected features are biologically complementary: ESM-2 embeddings capture contextual and evolutionary semantics, while handcrafted features provide residue-level physicochemical information, enabling a rich and multi-perspective representation of peptide sequences. Finally, we conduct a comprehensive performance evaluation of CG-AMP using two test datasets to assess the accuracy and reliability of its predictive capability. The results indicate that, while maintaining the same hyperparameter settings (optimized on the AMPlify dataset), CG-AMP not only achieves outstanding performance across multiple benchmark datasets but also demonstrates strong generalization capability. In conclusion, CG-AMP integrates contrastive learning with a GCNN within a dual-module architecture to achieve accurate and robust AMP prediction. By effectively capturing and fusing multiple complementary feature representations, the proposed framework demonstrates clear methodological advancements and consistent performance improvements over existing approaches, providing new insights and valuable guidance for AMP-related research. In this study, to extract sequence information from multiple perspectives, we employ several different kinds of encoding methods (as shown in Table 2). First, we leverage the ESM-2 model to obtain pre-trained feature representations of peptide sequences42. In this process, each amino acid is represented as a vector with 1280 dimensions, resulting in a feature matrix M with dimensions of L×1280, where L denotes the peptide sequence length. The ESM-2 language model, pre-trained on a large-scale protein sequence dataset, is capable of capturing rich contextual information and complex sequence patterns, providing powerful feature representations for downstream tasks. In addition, we incorporate three other types of amino acid information to represent the peptide sequences: one-hot encoding, BLOSUM62 encoding43, and Z-scale encoding44. One-hot encoding converts each amino acid residue categorical value into a vector with length equal to the total number of categories, where only the element corresponding to the specific category is 1, and all other elements are 0. BLOSUM62 encoding transforms each peptide sequence into an L×20 numerical matrix. This numerical matrix not only captures the rich evolutionary information of amino acid residues but also reflects the trends of residue substitution in the process of natural selection. Z-scale encoding transforms each peptide sequence into an L×5 numerical matrix. Z-scale encoding is based on the Z-scale normalization method, which standardizes and compares various physicochemical properties of amino acids. As a result, Z-scale encoding captures the physical and chemical properties of the amino acids in the peptide sequence. We employ a contrastive learning approach to train a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) encoder, thereby enhancing the discriminative ability of pre-trained features. Contrastive Learning45 is a feature learning method based on representation space optimization, which enhances the model’s representational ability by constructing a discriminative feature space. The core of this method lies in learning a metric space such that embeddings of similar samples are positioned close together, while embeddings of dissimilar samples are kept far apart. This dual optimization objective is achieved by concurrently maximizing the distance between different classes and minimizing the distance within the same class, significantly improving the discriminative power of peptide embeddings. In our framework, the contrastive pairs are constructed in a supervised manner rather than by data augmentation. Specifically, two sequences belonging to the same class (both AMPs or both non-AMPs) are regarded as a positive pair, whereas sequences from different classes (one AMP and one non-AMP) form a negative pair. This design enables the model to explicitly learn class-level discriminative representations instead of relying on stochastic sequence perturbations. The Gated Convolutional Neural Network (GCNN) was originally proposed for language modeling tasks38. It integrates Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) with Gated Linear Units (GLU), a type of gating mechanism, ultimately constructing an efficient and powerful framework for sequence modeling. Unlike Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), which rely on sequential computations based on time steps, the GCNN extracts local features through parallelized convolution operations and introduces gating mechanisms at the channel level to enhance feature selection and information flow control. Notably, by coupling the gating mechanism with linear transformations, GCNN enables the network to retain hierarchical non-linear modeling capabilities while providing a linear path for gradient transmission. This effectively alleviates the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks. In tasks involving long-term dependencies, the GCNN demonstrates higher computational efficiency compared to traditional methods such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), owing to its parallel computing capabilities and hierarchical structure. Specifically, by introducing the gating mechanism, GCNN further optimizes the control of information flow. This not only enhances the model’s ability to capture long-range dependencies but also significantly improves performance in complex sequence modeling tasks. We extract the contextual information of peptide sequences using GCNN, where the sequence embeddings are represented by multi-dimensional features. In this study, we designed a three-layer GCNN module to extract contextual information from peptide sequences. Each layer consists of three parallel 1D convolutional branches with kernel sizes of 3, 5, and 7, enabling multi-scale receptive field learning. The input to each convolutional layer has 256 channels, and each convolution produces 512 channels, which are processed through the GLU mechanism to enhance feature selection and control information flow. No dilation is applied. Residual connections are added after each GCNN block to alleviate the vanishing gradient problem and enhance training stability. Finally, the outputs of the three convolutional branches are concatenated to form the overall representation of the module. The ESM-2 language model, pre-trained on a large-scale protein sequence dataset, is capable of capturing rich contextual information and complex sequence patterns, providing powerful feature representations for downstream tasks. In addition, we incorporate three other types of amino acid information to represent the peptide sequences: one-hot encoding, BLOSUM62 encoding43, and Z-scale encoding44. One-hot encoding converts each amino acid residue categorical value into a vector with length equal to the total number of categories, where only the element corresponding to the specific category is 1, and all other elements are 0. BLOSUM62 encoding transforms each peptide sequence into an L×20 numerical matrix. This numerical matrix not only captures the rich evolutionary information of amino acid residues but also reflects the trends of residue substitution in the process of natural selection. Z-scale encoding transforms each peptide sequence into an L×5 numerical matrix. Z-scale encoding is based on the Z-scale normalization method, which standardizes and compares various physicochemical properties of amino acids. As a result, Z-scale encoding captures the physical and chemical properties of the amino acids in the peptide sequence. We employ a contrastive learning approach to train a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) encoder, thereby enhancing the discriminative ability of pre-trained features. Contrastive Learning45 is a feature learning method based on representation space optimization, which enhances the model’s representational ability by constructing a discriminative feature space. The core of this method lies in learning a metric space such that embeddings of similar samples are positioned close together, while embeddings of dissimilar samples are kept far apart. This dual optimization objective is achieved by concurrently maximizing the distance between different classes and minimizing the distance within the same class, significantly improving the discriminative power of peptide embeddings. In our framework, the contrastive pairs are constructed in a supervised manner rather than by data augmentation. Specifically, two sequences belonging to the same class (both AMPs or both non-AMPs) are regarded as a positive pair, whereas sequences from different classes (one AMP and one non-AMP) form a negative pair. This design enables the model to explicitly learn class-level discriminative representations instead of relying on stochastic sequence perturbations. The Gated Convolutional Neural Network (GCNN) was originally proposed for language modeling tasks38. It integrates Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) with Gated Linear Units (GLU), a type of gating mechanism, ultimately constructing an efficient and powerful framework for sequence modeling. Unlike Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), which rely on sequential computations based on time steps, the GCNN extracts local features through parallelized convolution operations and introduces gating mechanisms at the channel level to enhance feature selection and information flow control. Notably, by coupling the gating mechanism with linear transformations, GCNN enables the network to retain hierarchical non-linear modeling capabilities while providing a linear path for gradient transmission. This effectively alleviates the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks. In tasks involving long-term dependencies, the GCNN demonstrates higher computational efficiency compared to traditional methods such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), owing to its parallel computing capabilities and hierarchical structure. Specifically, by introducing the gating mechanism, GCNN further optimizes the control of information flow. This not only enhances the model’s ability to capture long-range dependencies but also significantly improves performance in complex sequence modeling tasks. We extract the contextual information of peptide sequences using GCNN, where the sequence embeddings are represented by multi-dimensional features. In this study, we designed a three-layer GCNN module to extract contextual information from peptide sequences. Each layer consists of three parallel 1D convolutional branches with kernel sizes of 3, 5, and 7, enabling multi-scale receptive field learning. The input to each convolutional layer has 256 channels, and each convolution produces 512 channels, which are processed through the GLU mechanism to enhance feature selection and control information flow. No dilation is applied. Residual connections are added after each GCNN block to alleviate the vanishing gradient problem and enhance training stability. Finally, the outputs of the three convolutional branches are concatenated to form the overall representation of the module. The design of CG-AMP was a key factor in its outstanding performance: the model integrated multiple features to represent sequence information and incorporated contrastive learning and a gated convolutional neural network (GCNN) within its architecture to learn complementary feature representations. To assess the contribution of each component, we conducted a series of ablation experiments, which included the following four variants: (1) CG-AMP-M (pre-trained features module): Removed the multi-dimensional features input and the corresponding GCNN module. (2) CG-AMP-P (multi-dimensional features module): Removed the pre-trained features input and its associated contrastive learning module. (3) CG-AMP-MC: Further removed the contrastive learning module based on CG-AMP-M. (4) CG-AMP-PG: Further removed the GCNN module based on CG-AMP-P. All ablation variants were trained and tested under the same hyperparameter settings and evaluated on two benchmark datasets. The experimental results on the AMPlify dataset (Fig. 3A; Table 6) showed that CG-AMP-M exhibits a performance drop compared to CG-AMP, with accuracy and MCC decreasing by 1.2% and 2.4%, respectively. Furthermore, CG-AMP-P demonstrated an even greater decline in performance compared to CG-AMP-M, with accuracy and MCC decreasing by 1.7% and 3.5%, respectively. These findings not only highlighted the critical roles of both the multi-dimensional features module and the pre-trained features module within the model, but also underscored the superiority of the pre-trained features module. This may have been attributed to the richer information encoded in the ESM-2 features used by the pre-trained module. Moreover, CG-AMP-P outperformed CG-AMP-PG by 12% in accuracy (Acc) and 24% in MCC, while CG-AMP-M showed an improvement of 1% in Acc and 2% in MCC over CG-AMP-MC. These experimental results fully validate the crucial role of the contrastive learning and GCNN modules in extracting effective features. Additionally, the superior performance of CG-AMP-MC compared to CG-AMP-PG further emphasize the key role of pre-trained features in the model. We repeated the above ablation experiments on the DAMP dataset, and the results (Fig. 3B; Table 7) further validated the model’s effectiveness and robustness, corroborating the reliability of the aforementioned conclusions. To optimize the feature fusion strategy for the representations processed by the contrastive learning module and the GCNN module in the CG-AMP model, we systematically compared three different fusion methods after dimensionality reduction (maximum pooling followed by projection to a 64-dimensional space). The experimental process included: (1) Linear Fusion: A weighted combination of embeddings, where weights were learnable parameters. (2) Concatenation Fusion: Direct concatenation of feature vectors at the channel level. (3) Attention-based Fusion: Adaptive feature integration through a multi-head attention mechanism. As shown in Fig. 3C and D, linear fusion exhibited the best performance, while concatenation fusion and attention-based fusion methods showed relatively poorer results. This phenomenon may be attributed to two factors: first, the concatenation method, being overly simplistic, failed to effectively mine feature information, resulting in the underutilization of potential correlations between features. Second, while attention-based fusion theoretically had advantages, its complexity may have led to the over-complication of feature information in this application scenario, thus affecting the overall performance of the model. This study proposes a novel dual-module feature fusion computational framework, CG-AMP, which combined multi-source sequence features and a dual-module feature extraction strategy for the recognition of AMPs. Specifically, the features we utilized included pre-trained features (ESM-2) and multi-dimensional features, including BLOSUM62, Z-scale, and one-hot encoding. Additionally, the CG-AMP framework consisted of two complementary feature extraction modules: First, to address the potential redundancy in the initial pre-trained features, we introduced an encoder based on a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) and trained it using a contrastive learning strategy. Through this approach, the encoder not only effectively learned the features extracted by the ESM-2 pre-trained model but also actively removed redundant information, thereby enhancing its ability to identify key information and improving the discriminative power of the features. Second, to efficiently capture long-range dependencies between sequence features, we employed a GCNN to model the multi-dimensional features extracted from the sequences. By parallelizing convolution operations and incorporating gating mechanisms, GCNN was able to deeply explore the latent global and local information between features, providing richer and more fine-grained representations. In selecting the feature fusion strategy, we evaluated several methods, including simple concatenation, linear fusion, and attention-based fusion. Experimental results indicate that linear fusion outperforms more complex strategies, likely because the features optimized through contrastive learning already exhibit strong discriminative power and complementarity. Although attention-based fusion theoretically enables richer feature interactions, it introduces additional parameters and computational overhead, which may increase the risk of overfitting. Therefore, linear fusion offers an efficient, robust, and interpretable approach for integrating features while preserving their independence and complementarity, demonstrating that when features are sufficiently optimized, a simple fusion strategy can effectively balance predictive performance and model complexity. In summary, the high performance of the CG-AMP model was attributed to two main factors: (1) CG-AMP extracted sequence features from multiple perspectives, encompassing the contextual information, evolutionary data, and physicochemical properties of the peptide sequences; (2) CG-AMP achieved complementary fusion of features through its dual-module architecture, fully leveraging the rich information within the sequences. This enhanced the model’s ability to recognize complex sequence patterns and strengthened its generalization capability. CG-AMP demonstrated significant performance advantages in the AMP prediction task. However, despite the model’s strong performance, there was still potential for further optimization. For example, some of the initial values and ranges of hyperparameters in this study were derived from previous research and were preliminarily determined under limited experimental conditions. In the future, we can further optimize the parameter configuration through large-scale model training. Additionally, the features extracted using the ESM-2 language model have already positively impacted the model’s performance. To further improve the model, future work could explore integrating features from other pre-trained models, such as ProtT551, to obtain more comprehensive sequence representations. Currently, the model primarily relies on sequence-level information. This is partly because three-dimensional structure prediction of peptides still carries considerable uncertainty, which may introduce errors and affect prediction accuracy. Therefore, using sequence information remains a more robust strategy. However, relying solely on sequence information may limit the model’s ability to capture structural and functional features of peptides. Moreover, the interpretability of the model remains limited, and its generalizability to highly diverse peptide families still requires systematic validation. Future research will proceed along two directions. First, we plan to incorporate secondary and tertiary structural information of peptides52, such as α-helix content, hydrophobic surface area, and contact map features, and generate structural embeddings using high-accuracy structure prediction tools like AlphaFold253, aiming to enhance the model’s ability to represent spatial conformations and identify key functional sites. Second, we aim to fine-tune CG-AMP or apply multi-task learning on datasets of peptides with diverse functions, such as antiviral peptides (AVPs)54 and anticancer peptides (ACPs)55, to systematically assess its cross-task adaptability and transferability. These efforts will lay the foundation for developing a more generalizable and interpretable framework for peptide function prediction. At the same time, with the rapid development of artificial intelligence technologies, high-precision tools specifically designed for peptide sequence structure prediction may emerge in the future. Utilizing these structural insights is expected to further enhance the model’s performance. As a method focused on AMP recognition, CG-AMP’s flexible architecture gives it broad applicability, and it holds promise for future expansion into other sequence-based biological classification tasks.
